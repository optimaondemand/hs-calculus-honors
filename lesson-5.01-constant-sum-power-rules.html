📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You already know how to find a derivative. It is time to stop doing it the slow way.
In Module 4 you built \(f'(x)\) from the limit definition every single time, for \(x^2\), for \(\sqrt{x}\), for \(x^2+4x\). That repetition was necessary the first time, so that you would trust what a derivative actually is before you ever shortcut it. Today you prove, once and for all, three rules that let you skip the limit for the building blocks that make up nearly every function you will differentiate this year: a constant, a sum of two functions, and a power of \(x\). A rule earns the right to be used without re-derivation only after it has been proved. That is exactly what today is for.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to prove the Constant Rule and the Constant Multiple Rule from the limit definition of the derivative.
2
I will be able to prove the Sum and Difference Rules from the limit definition, using the limit laws from Module 1.
3
I will be able to prove the Power Rule for positive integer exponents using the Binomial Theorem, and combine all three rules to differentiate any polynomial without returning to the limit definition.

📚 PART 1 · THE CONSTANT RULE AND THE CONSTANT MULTIPLE RULE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before any algebra: if \(f(x) = 9\) for every \(x\), what do you expect \(f'(x)\) to be, and why? (No wrong answer. Just make a prediction from the graph.)

Proof: the Constant Rule

Let \(f(x) = c\) for some constant \(c\), for every \(x\). Apply the limit definition from Lesson 4.02 directly:

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{c - c}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{0}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} 0 \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

So \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[c] = 0\) for every constant \(c\). This matches your prediction exactly: the graph of a constant function is a horizontal line, and a horizontal line has slope 0 everywhere.

Proof: the Constant Multiple Rule

Now suppose \(g(x) = c \cdot f(x)\), where \(f\) is differentiable and \(c\) is a constant. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} g'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{c f(x+h) - c f(x)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} c\left(\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\right) \\ &= c \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \\ &= c \, f'(x) \end{aligned} \]

The third step pulls the constant \(c\) outside the limit, which is legal because of the constant-multiple limit law you already proved usable back in Lesson 1.02. So \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[c\,f(x)\big] = c\, f'(x)\): a constant factor rides along for free.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Every derivative rule you prove today rests on the same limit laws Augustin-Louis Cauchy helped make rigorous in the 1820s. Newton and Leibniz used shortcuts like these a century and a half earlier, by instinct and geometric reasoning, long before anyone could prove they were legitimate. You are retracing, in a few pages, an argument it took mathematics roughly 150 years to make airtight.
📚 PART 2 · THE SUM AND DIFFERENCE RULES

Let \(s(x) = f(x) + g(x)\), where \(f\) and \(g\) are both differentiable. Apply the limit definition to \(s\):

\[ \begin{aligned} s'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\big[f(x+h)+g(x+h)\big] - \big[f(x)+g(x)\big]}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \left(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} + \frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\right) \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \;+\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h} \\ &= f'(x) + g'(x) \end{aligned} \]

The third line splits one limit into two, which is legal because of the sum limit law from Lesson 1.02: the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, whenever both limits exist. Since \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable, both \(f'(x)\) and \(g'(x)\) exist, so the split is justified. The identical argument, with a minus sign carried through, proves the Difference Rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)-g(x)\big] = f'(x) - g'(x)\).

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The Sum Rule works precisely because addition splits cleanly across the difference quotient. Do not assume the same trick works for a product: as you will see next lesson, \((f\cdot g)'\) is not \(f' \cdot g'\). A rule proved for one operation is not automatically true of another; each one earns its own proof.
📚 PART 3 · THE POWER RULE AND YOUR FIRST FULL POLYNOMIAL

For a positive integer \(n\), the Binomial Theorem expands \((x+h)^n\) as

\[ (x+h)^n = x^n + n\,x^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^2 + \cdots + h^n \]

Every term after the first two carries a factor of \(h^2\) or higher. Watch what happens when this expansion goes into the limit definition for \(f(x) = x^n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{n\,x^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^2 + \cdots + h^n}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \left(n\,x^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h + \cdots + h^{n-1}\right) \\ &= n\,x^{n-1} \end{aligned} \]

Every term except \(n\,x^{n-1}\) still carries at least one factor of \(h\), so every one of them vanishes as \(h \to 0\), leaving only \(n\,x^{n-1}\). This is the Power Rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[x^n\big] = n\,x^{n-1}\).

📌 SEE IT FOR TWO SMALL CASES FIRST
n = 2: \((x+h)^2 = x^2+2xh+h^2\)
\(\dfrac{2xh+h^2}{h} = 2x+h \;\to\; 2x\), matching \(n x^{n-1} = 2x^1\).
n = 3: \((x+h)^3 = x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3\)
\(\dfrac{3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3}{h} = 3x^2+3xh+h^2 \;\to\; 3x^2\), matching \(n x^{n-1} = 3x^2\).

Worked example: a full polynomial, no limit required

Differentiate \(f(x) = 3x^4 - 7x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x + 9\). Apply the Power Rule to each term, the Constant Multiple Rule to carry each coefficient along, the Sum and Difference Rules to combine the terms, and the Constant Rule to the final 9:

\[ f'(x) = 3(4x^3) - 7(3x^2) + 2(2x) - 5(1) + 0 = 12x^3 - 21x^2 + 4x - 5 \]

Compare this to what Lesson 4.03 would have demanded: a full difference quotient, a full binomial expansion of \((x+h)^4\) and \((x+h)^3\), and a careful simplification, all to differentiate one function. Three proved rules just replaced that entire process with a line of arithmetic. That trade, rigor first and speed second, is the whole point of today.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A shortcut is only trustworthy once it has been proved from the definition it shortcuts. The Constant, Sum, Difference, and Power Rules all trace back to the same limit definition you built by hand in Module 4, and that ancestry is exactly what lets you use them freely from here forward. Every polynomial in this course is now yours to differentiate in one line. Next lesson asks what happens when two functions are multiplied or divided together, where a new and less obvious rule is required.
