📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What number do all the Riemann sums close in on, and what does it mean when part of the graph lies below the axis?
Lessons 13.01 and 13.02 built the machinery: rectangles, sigma notation, and a limit as the rectangles grow thin. Today the machinery gets a name. The limit of Riemann sums is the definite integral, and once it has a name and a notation, you can reason with it instead of recomputing it each time. You will meet its notation, learn that it counts area above the axis as positive and area below as negative, collect the rules that let you split and combine integrals, and learn when the limit fails to exist. Newton and Leibniz each had a version of this idea; the careful definition we use, as a limit of sums, was made rigorous by Cauchy and Riemann in the nineteenth century.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define the definite integral as a limit of Riemann sums, read and write its notation, and evaluate a simple integral from the definition.
2
I will be able to interpret an integral as signed area, evaluate integrals of lines and circles by geometry, and distinguish an integral from an area.
3
I will be able to use the properties of the integral (linearity, additivity, reversing limits, comparison and bounds) and say when an integral does not exist.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, plus the summation formula \(\sum i^2=\tfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}\) and the limit-at-infinity idea from Lesson 2.01.
▶
The geometry formulas for the area of a triangle, rectangle, trapezoid, and circle, and a ruler for sketching graphs.
▶
A willingness to keep two questions apart: what is the integral, and how much area is enclosed?
📚 PART 1 · FROM SUMS TO THE DEFINITE INTEGRAL
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
In Lessons 13.01 and 13.02 you built left, right, and midpoint sums, and you watched them close in on a single number as the number of rectangles grew. Suppose someone hands you a continuous function on \([a,b]\) and asks for the number those sums are closing in on. Does that number depend on which kind of sum you chose (left, right, or midpoint)?

For two thousand years that number was found one problem at a time, by cleverness. Archimedes squeezed the area of a parabolic segment between inscribed and circumscribed figures. Today we name the whole idea once and for all. Cut \([a,b]\) into \(n\) equal pieces of width \(\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}\), pick a sample point \(x_i^*\) in each piece, and add up the thin rectangle areas. Then let \(n\) grow without bound.

The definite integral
Let \(f\) be defined on \([a,b]\). The definite integral of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\) is \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x,\qquad \Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n}, \] provided the limit exists and is the same number no matter how the sample points \(x_i^*\) are chosen. In that case we say \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\).

Read the notation aloud, because every piece of it is a piece of the idea. The elongated \(\int\) is an old-fashioned letter S, for summa, the Latin word for sum; Leibniz drew it that way in 1675. The numbers \(a\) and \(b\) are the limits of integration. The \(f(x)\) is the height of a rectangle, and the \(dx\) is the width of one, the limiting form of \(\Delta x\). The letter \(x\) is a dummy variable: \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) and \(\int_a^b f(t)\,dt\) are the same number, just as \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} i\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} k\) are.

A worked limit, using the method of Lesson 13.02. Find \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 x^2\,dx\) straight from the definition, with right endpoints \(x_i=\dfrac{3i}{n}\) and \(\Delta x=\dfrac{3}{n}\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\frac{3i}{n}\Bigr)^2\frac{3}{n}=\frac{27}{n^3}\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2=\frac{27}{n^3}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}=\frac{9(n+1)(2n+1)}{2n^2}. \]

As \(n\to\infty\), \(\dfrac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{n^2}\to 2\) (the leading coefficients, the idea of Lesson 2.01), so the sum tends to \(\dfrac{9\cdot2}{2}=9\). Therefore

\[ \int_0^3 x^2\,dx=9. \]

Which functions are guaranteed to have such a limit? Here is the fact that covers nearly everything you will meet.

Continuous functions are integrable
If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\). The same is true if \(f\) has only finitely many jump discontinuities and is bounded.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
We state this theorem without proving it, and we say so plainly: its proof uses the completeness of the real numbers and belongs to a later course in analysis. In this course, accept it as a fact you can rely on, and notice what it is not saying. It does not say that a discontinuous function can never be integrated, and it does not tell you how to compute the number. It only tells you that, for a continuous function, the number exists.

Convince a classmate: Explain to a partner why the letter inside the integral, \(x\) or \(t\), does not change the value of \(\int_0^3 x^2\,dx\).

📚 PART 2 · SIGNED AREA AND GEOMETRY

A Riemann sum adds the quantities \(f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x\), and where \(f\) is negative those quantities are negative. So the integral is not simply "the area under the curve." It is a signed area: area above the \(x\)-axis counts as positive, area below counts as negative.

Signed area
\(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) equals the area of the regions between the graph and the \(x\)-axis that lie above the axis, minus the area of the regions that lie below it.

Worked example. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^5 (x-2)\,dx\) with geometry alone. The line \(y=x-2\) crosses the axis at \(x=2\). From \(0\) to \(2\) it lies below the axis and encloses a triangle of base \(2\) and height \(2\), area \(\tfrac12\cdot2\cdot2=2\). From \(2\) to \(5\) it lies above the axis and encloses a triangle of base \(3\) and height \(3\), area \(\tfrac12\cdot3\cdot3=\tfrac92\). Signed area:

\[ \int_0^5 (x-2)\,dx=\frac92-2=\frac52. \]

The total (unsigned) area between the line and the axis is \(\tfrac92+2=\tfrac{13}{2}\), a different number answering a different question. Keep the two questions apart: "what is the integral?" and "how much area is enclosed?" are not the same question whenever the graph dips below the axis.

Geometry gives exact integrals. When the graph is a line, a rectangle, a triangle, or part of a circle, you know the area formula already:

\[ \int_0^4 x\,dx=\tfrac12(4)(4)=8,\qquad \int_{-2}^{2}\sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx=\tfrac12\pi(2)^2=2\pi. \]

The second graph is the upper half of the circle \(x^2+y^2=4\), a semicircle of radius \(2\), so its area is half of \(\pi r^2\). No limit of sums was needed. The ancient geometers would have felt right at home.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Integral does not mean area. \(\displaystyle\int_{-3}^{3} x^3\,dx=0\), because the area below the axis on \([-3,0]\) exactly cancels the area above it on \([0,3]\). A student who reads that as "there is no area between the curve and the axis" has misread the symbol. When a problem asks for area you split at the places where the graph crosses the axis; when it asks for the integral you do not.

📚 PART 3 · THE RULES OF THE INTEGRAL

Because the integral is a limit of sums, it inherits the rules of sums. These rules let you break a hard integral into easy ones, and they are the working vocabulary of the rest of the course. Assume \(f\) and \(g\) are integrable on every interval below.

\[ \int_a^a f(x)\,dx=0 \]
No width, no area.
\[ \int_b^a f(x)\,dx=-\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]
Reversing the direction of travel reverses the sign (\(\Delta x\) becomes negative).
\[ \int_a^b c\,f(x)\,dx=c\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]
A constant multiple comes outside.
\[ \int_a^b \bigl[f(x)\pm g(x)\bigr]\,dx=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\pm\int_a^b g(x)\,dx \]
The limit of a sum is the sum of the limits (Lesson 1.02).
\[ \int_a^c f(x)\,dx+\int_c^b f(x)\,dx=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]
Additivity: cut the interval anywhere, even outside \([a,b]\) if \(f\) is integrable there.
\[ \text{If } f(x)\ge g(x)\text{ on }[a,b]\text{, then }\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\ge\int_a^b g(x)\,dx \]
Comparison: a taller function encloses at least as much signed area.

Worked example (using only the rules). Suppose \(\int_0^2 f(x)\,dx=3\), \(\int_2^5 f(x)\,dx=-1\), and \(\int_0^5 g(x)\,dx=4\). Find \(\int_0^5 \bigl[2f(x)-g(x)\bigr]\,dx\).

By additivity, \(\int_0^5 f(x)\,dx=3+(-1)=2\). By linearity,

\[ \int_0^5 \bigl[2f(x)-g(x)\bigr]\,dx=2(2)-4=0. \]

Notice what we never did: we never needed to know what \(f\) or \(g\) is. The rules are enough.

Bounding an integral without computing it

Comparison has a handy consequence. If \(m\le f(x)\le M\) on \([a,b]\), then the integral lies between the area of the smallest and the largest rectangle that fit the interval:

\[ m(b-a)\le\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\le M(b-a). \]

For \(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\) on \([0,1]\), the function falls from \(f(0)=1\) to \(f(1)=\tfrac12\), so \(\tfrac12\le f(x)\le1\), and therefore \(\tfrac12(1)\le\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x^2}\le1(1)\). The integral is trapped between \(0.5\) and \(1\) before we know anything else about it. (In a later lesson you will find its exact value, about \(0.785\), and see that it sits comfortably inside the bounds.)

📚 PART 4 · WHEN THE INTEGRAL DOES NOT EXIST

The definition has fine print, and an honest mathematician reads it. The integral is defined only when the limit of the sums exists. For a continuous function on a closed interval, it always does. For other functions, it can fail.

The integral that does not exist. Consider \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx\). The graph of \(f(x)=1/x^2\) shoots up without bound near \(x=0\) (Lesson 2.02's vertical asymptote), and \(f\) is not even defined at \(0\). A Riemann sum would have to evaluate \(f\) at a sample point in the piece containing \(0\), and by choosing that point closer and closer to \(0\) you can make the sum as large as you like, so the sums have no limit. This integral does not exist as a definite integral, and no formula, however tempting, can make it exist.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
This is the same honesty you practiced with the Mean Value Theorem in Lesson 8.03: a result is a promise that holds only while its hypotheses hold. Later this semester you will learn a shortcut for evaluating integrals. Before you trust any shortcut, ask first whether the integral exists. A machine will sometimes hand back a number for \(\int_{-1}^{1}x^{-2}\,dx\) anyway, and the number will be meaningless.
🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool or computer algebra system can evaluate a definite integral in a blink. Treat its answer as a claim to check, not a verdict. Does the answer have the right sign for the picture? Is it between the bounds \(m(b-a)\) and \(M(b-a)\)? Does the integral exist at all? The tool does not know which of these questions you needed answered. You do.
▶  Try it first: using signed area, evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^4 \bigl|x-2\bigr|\,dx\), then explain why \(\displaystyle\int_0^4 (x-2)\,dx\) is a different number.
The graph of \(y=|x-2|\) is a "V" with its vertex at \((2,0)\) and it lies on or above the axis, so there is no negative area. Two triangles, each with base \(2\) and height \(2\), give \(2+2=4\), so \(\int_0^4|x-2|\,dx=4\). For \(y=x-2\), the left triangle lies below the axis and counts as \(-2\) while the right triangle counts as \(+2\), so \(\int_0^4(x-2)\,dx=0\). Same triangles, but the sign decides the answer.
▶  Try it first: write \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 (2x+1)\,dx\) as a sum of two integrals you can evaluate by geometry, and find its value.
Linearity gives \(2\int_0^3 x\,dx+\int_0^3 1\,dx\). The first is a triangle: \(\tfrac12(3)(3)=\tfrac92\), times \(2\) gives \(9\). The second is a rectangle of height \(1\) and width \(3\), area \(3\). Total: \(9+3=12\). (Check by trapezoid: the line goes from height \(1\) to height \(7\) over width \(3\), so the area is \(\tfrac{1+7}{2}\cdot3=12\). It agrees.)

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) is the limit of Riemann sums, and it counts area above the axis as positive and area below as negative. It obeys the rules of sums, it is guaranteed to exist for a continuous function, and it can fail to exist when the function is unbounded. Lesson 13.04 turns from exact values to practical ones: when a function is given only by a table of data, or has no simple area formula, you can still approximate its integral well, and the Trapezoidal Rule is the tool.
