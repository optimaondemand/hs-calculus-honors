📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Zoom in far enough on a smooth curve and it stops looking like a curve. That one fact lets you estimate hard numbers with easy ones.
Lesson 9.01 gave you the equation of a tangent line, and Lesson 9.02 showed where the idea breaks down. Today the tangent line becomes a tool. You will use it to estimate values like \(\sqrt{4.1}\) by hand, decide whether each estimate is too high or too low, and discover what “differentiable” really means: a function that looks like a straight line when you zoom in far enough.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to write the linearization \(L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\) of a differentiable function and use it to estimate nearby values.
2
I will be able to use the bending of the graph (the sign of \(f''\)) to say whether a linear estimate is too high or too low, and judge how far from \(a\) it can be trusted.
3

📚 PART 1 · THE TANGENT LINE AS AN ESTIMATOR
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Without a calculator, what would you guess \(\sqrt{4.1}\) is close to? Pick the line of thinking that feels closest to yours.

Lesson 9.01 taught you to write the equation of a tangent line, and Lesson 9.02 showed you where a tangent line stands vertical or fails to exist. Today the tangent line gets a job: standing in for a function that is hard to compute, near one point where it is easy.

Here is the picture. Zoom in on a smooth curve at a point, and then zoom in again. The curve flattens toward a straight line, and that line is the tangent line. So, close to the point, the tangent line is a very good copy of the curve, and a line is easy to evaluate.

The linearization of \(f\) at \(a\)
If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), the linearization of \(f\) at \(a\) is the function \[ L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a). \] It is the tangent line at \((a,f(a))\), written as a function. For \(x\) near \(a\), \(f(x)\approx L(x)\).

Example 1: \(\sqrt{4.1}\). Let \(f(x)=\sqrt x\). The point \(a=4\) is the right choice because \(f(4)=2\) is known exactly and \(4.1\) is close. Differentiate: \(f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\), so \(f'(4)=\dfrac14\). Then

\[ L(x)=2+\tfrac14(x-4)=1+\tfrac{x}{4}. \]

So \(\sqrt{4.1}\approx L(4.1)=2+\tfrac14(0.1)=2.025\). A calculator gives \(\sqrt{4.1}=2.02485\ldots\), so the estimate is off by about \(0.00015\). The skill is not the arithmetic. It is choosing \(a\): pick a point near \(x\) where \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\) are both easy to compute.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
Look at the last row. Using the linearization at \(a=4\) to estimate \(\sqrt9\) gives \(3.25\), which is wrong by \(0.25\), about \(8\%\). Nothing is broken: the tangent line is a local tool. The farther \(x\) is from \(a\), the less the line resembles the curve. A linear estimate is only as good as the answer to the question “how close is \(x\) to \(a\)?”
📚 PART 2 · TOO HIGH OR TOO LOW?

A useful estimate does not stop at a number. It also says which way it errs. In the table every error is positive: \(L(x)\) is always bigger than \(\sqrt x\). In the figure, the dashed tangent line sits above the curve everywhere except at the point of tangency.

That is not an accident of square roots. It depends on how the curve bends away from its tangent line. A curve that bends downward (like a frown) falls below its tangent line, so the linearization overestimates. A curve that bends upward (like a smile) rises above its tangent line, so the linearization underestimates. The measure of bending is the second derivative \(f''\), the derivative of \(f'\) from Lesson 8.01. Module 11 develops it fully; for now, use it as a check on every estimate you make.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
This is the first of several times that the second derivative will turn out to control how far off a first-derivative estimate is. For \(\sqrt x\) near \(4\), the error is about \(-\tfrac1{64}(x-4)^2\) (a pattern the numbers in this lesson will confirm, not something proved today). Because the error depends on \((x-4)^2\), halving the distance from \(a\) cuts the error to about a quarter.
⚡ TRY IT · LINEAR ESTIMATES AND THEIR DIRECTION
Use a linearization, then say what you can honestly say about the estimate.
1. The linearization of \(f(x)=x^3\) at \(a=2\) is \(L(x)=12x-16\). Use it to estimate \(2.01^3\).
2. For \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\) at \(a=1\), \(L(x)=2-x\), so \(L(1.1)=0.9\) while \(\dfrac1{1.1}=0.90909\ldots\). Why is the estimate too low?
3. We used \(L(x)=1+\tfrac x4\) to estimate \(\sqrt9\) and got \(3.25\), but \(\sqrt9=3\). What does that tell you?
▶  Try it in your notebook first: linearize \(f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}\) at \(a=8\), estimate \(\sqrt[3]{8.3}\), and decide whether your estimate is too high or too low. Then reveal the check.
\(f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{3x^{2/3}}\), so \(f'(8)=\dfrac{1}{3\cdot4}=\dfrac1{12}\) and \(L(x)=2+\dfrac{x-8}{12}\). Then \(\sqrt[3]{8.3}\approx L(8.3)=2+\dfrac{0.3}{12}=2.025\). The true value is \(2.02469\ldots\), so the estimate is slightly high. That matches the second derivative: \(f''(x)=-\dfrac{2}{9}x^{-5/3}\lt 0\), so the curve bends down and lies below its tangent line.
📚 PART 3 · LOCAL LINEARITY: WHAT DIFFERENTIABLE REALLY MEANS

Why does the tangent line work so well near \(a\)? Because the error shrinks faster than the distance does. Define the error \(E(x)=f(x)-L(x)\). For \(\sqrt x\) near \(4\), compare \(E\) with the distance \(h=x-4\):

\(h=x-4\)\(E=\sqrt{4+h}-L(4+h)\)\(E/h\)
0.1\(-0.000154\)\(-0.00154\)
0.01\(-0.0000016\)\(-0.000156\)

When \(h\) shrinks by a factor of 10, the error shrinks by a factor of about 100, so the ratio \(E/h\) shrinks too. That is not luck. It is what differentiability means:

Differentiable means locally linear
\(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) exactly when \[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)-L(x)}{x-a}=0. \] The error in the tangent-line estimate is small even compared to the tiny distance \(x-a\).

Why. By the definition of the derivative, \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}=f'(a)\). Subtract \(f'(a)\) from the quotient:

\[ \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}-f'(a)=\frac{f(x)-f(a)-f'(a)(x-a)}{x-a}=\frac{f(x)-L(x)}{x-a}. \]

The left side tends to \(f'(a)-f'(a)=0\), so the right side does too. \(\blacksquare\) (If the first limit did not exist, the whole chain would fail. That is the corner case.)

A corner has no linearization. Take \(f(x)=|x|\) at \(a=0\) (Lesson 4.04). Suppose some line \(mx\) were a good approximation. Then \(\dfrac{|x|-mx}{x}\) would have to tend to \(0\). But for \(x>0\) it equals \(1-m\), and for \(x\lt 0\) it equals \(-1-m\), and no single \(m\) makes both zero. However far you zoom in, the corner stays a corner. This is the one-sided-limit argument of Lesson 1.04, in a new role.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
A linear estimate without a statement of how far you strayed from \(a\), and which way the curve bends, is a guess wearing a number. Honest approximation says three things: the estimate, its likely direction of error, and how close \(x\) was to \(a\). Scientists call this reporting your uncertainty, and it is the habit of truthfulness applied to arithmetic.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: use the linearization of \(f(x)=e^x\) at \(a=0\) to estimate \(e^{-0.05}\), and say whether your estimate is too high or too low. Then reveal the check.
\(f'(x)=e^x\), so \(f(0)=f'(0)=1\) and \(L(x)=1+x\). Then \(e^{-0.05}\approx1-0.05=0.95\). The true value is \(0.95123\ldots\), so the estimate is slightly low, as predicted by \(f''(x)=e^x>0\) (the curve smiles, above its tangent line). The same sign rule holds on both sides of \(a\).
🤖 AI AWARENESS
An AI tool can give you the linearization of a function in a second, and it will usually be right. What it often leaves out is the part you now know to ask for: which direction the estimate errs, and how far from \(a\) the estimate stops being trustworthy. Make the tool, or yourself, finish the sentence.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Near a point, a differentiable function is its tangent line plus an error that shrinks faster than the distance from the point. That makes the tangent line a trustworthy estimator, close to \(a\) and honest about its limits. Lesson 9.04 puts a number on that error: it turns the tangent line's prediction into differentials, and uses them to answer the question every measurement raises, “how wrong could my answer be?”
