📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A tank fills at a changing rate. If you know the rate at every moment, you can find how much water is in the tank. If you know how much is in it, you can find the rate. Are those two facts really one fact?
All year you have worked with two ideas that seem to live in different worlds. The derivative asks how fast something is changing at one instant. The definite integral asks how much has piled up over an interval. Each was built from limits, and each took real work to compute. Today a single theorem shows they are inverses of each other. The theorem is called fundamental because without it integration would be a slow limit of sums for every problem, and with it integration becomes one of the most powerful computational ideas in mathematics. Like every theorem in this course, it comes with hypotheses, and part of today's work is saying exactly where they are used.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define an accumulation function \(F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\), find its values from the graph of \(f\), and read where it increases, decreases and bends.
2
I will be able to state the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1, including its hypothesis, and explain why \(F'(x)=f(x)\).
3
I will be able to differentiate functions defined by integrals, including those with a variable upper or lower limit, using the Chain Rule.

📚 PART 1 · ACCUMULATION FUNCTIONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A tank fills from a hose whose flow rate is a constant 2 liters per minute, starting from empty at \(t=0\). Let \(A(x)\) be the amount of water in the tank after \(x\) minutes. What is \(A'(x)\), the rate at which the amount is changing?

Module 13 gave you the definite integral as a limit of Riemann sums, and it gave you its meaning: the signed area between a curve and the horizontal axis. Now we ask a new kind of question. Instead of asking for the area up to one fixed endpoint, let the endpoint move. Fix a starting point \(a\), and for each \(x\) let

\[ F(x)=\int_a^{x} f(t)\,dt \]

be the signed area under the graph of \(f\) from \(a\) out to \(x\). This is an accumulation function: for each input \(x\) it reports how much has piled up so far. Notice that \(t\) is a dummy variable. It is the variable the integral runs through, and it disappears when the integral is finished, so \(x\) is free to be the upper limit. Writing \(\int_a^x f(x)\,dx\) would use \(x\) for two different jobs at once, which is why we use \(t\) inside.

The graph of \(f\): a rate that rises, holds steady, then falls to zero. The shaded region is the area accumulated from \(t=0\) to \(t=6\).

Use the graph, with \(a=0\), and areas of triangles, rectangles and trapezoids to fill in \(F\) at several inputs. From \(0\) to \(2\) the region is a trapezoid with parallel sides \(1\) and \(3\) and width \(2\), so \(F(2)=\tfrac{1+3}{2}\cdot2=4\). From \(2\) to \(4\) it is a \(2\times3\) rectangle, so \(F(4)=4+6=10\). From \(4\) to \(6\) it is a triangle of base \(2\) and height \(3\), so \(F(6)=10+3=13\).

Look at how \(F\) behaves, not just its values. Between \(t=2\) and \(t=4\), the graph of \(f\) sits at height \(3\), and \(F\) climbs by \(3\) for each unit step. Between \(t=4\) and \(t=6\), \(f\) is positive but shrinking, so \(F\) still climbs, but more and more slowly. Does \(F\) grow at a rate that equals the height of \(f\)? On \(0\le x\le2\), \(f(t)=1+t\), and a direct computation gives \(F(x)=x+\tfrac{x^2}{2}\), so

\[ F'(x)=1+x=f(x). \]

The derivative of the accumulated area is the height of the graph. That is not an accident of this one example.

📚 PART 2 · THE FUNDAMENTAL THEOREM, PART 1

This is one of the great theorems of mathematics, and it is called, without exaggeration, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Part 1 is the statement we have just noticed.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1
Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), and define \(F(x)=\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) for \(x\) in \([a,b]\). Then \(F\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and \[ F'(x)=f(x). \] In Leibniz notation: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=f(x)\).

Said in words: accumulating and then differentiating gets you back where you started. Differentiation undoes integration, which is why slope and area, two ideas that began as separate problems, are the same subject.

Why it is true

Fix \(x\) and take a small \(h>0\). The difference quotient of \(F\) is

\[ \frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h}=\frac1h\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt, \]

because the integral from \(a\) to \(x+h\) minus the integral from \(a\) to \(x\) is the integral over the sliver from \(x\) to \(x+h\) (the additivity property from Lesson 13.03). That sliver sits over the interval \([x,x+h]\). Because \(f\) is continuous there, the Extreme Value Theorem (Lesson 3.04) says \(f\) has a minimum value \(m_h\) and a maximum value \(M_h\) on that interval. The area of the sliver is trapped between the rectangle of height \(m_h\) and the rectangle of height \(M_h\), both of width \(h\):

\[ m_h\cdot h\;\le\;\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt\;\le\;M_h\cdot h\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_h\;\le\;\frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h}\;\le\;M_h. \]

Now let \(h\to0^+\). The interval \([x,x+h]\) shrinks to the single point \(x\), and since \(f\) is continuous at \(x\), both \(m_h\) and \(M_h\) are squeezed to \(f(x)\). By the Squeeze Theorem (Lesson 2.03), the difference quotient has right-hand limit \(f(x)\). The case \(h<0\) is the mirror image, so \(F'(x)=f(x)\). \(\blacksquare\)

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
Be honest about what this argument leaned on. It used two facts about integrals, the additivity and the comparison property, that Lesson 13.03 stated and used. It also assumed that the integral of a continuous function exists in the first place. Those facts are true and are proved in a full real-analysis course; here we rely on them openly rather than pretend they cost nothing. Notice, too, exactly where continuity of \(f\) was used: it is what forces \(m_h\) and \(M_h\) to close in on \(f(x)\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Isaac Barrow, Newton's teacher at Cambridge, proved a geometric version of this theorem in the 1660s. Newton and Leibniz, working independently, recognized that it let them compute areas by reversing derivatives, and that recognition, more than any single formula, is what we call the invention of calculus. Lesson 16.02's Mean Value Theorem for Integrals gives another route to the same proof.
📚 PART 3 · DIFFERENTIATING A FUNCTION DEFINED BY AN INTEGRAL

Part 1 lets you differentiate a function that is defined by an integral, even when you cannot find a formula for the integral. For instance, \(F(x)=\int_1^x\sqrt{1+t^3}\,dt\) has no tidy closed form, yet the theorem says at once that \(F'(x)=\sqrt{1+x^3}\). Plug in \(x=2\): \(F'(2)=\sqrt{9}=3\).

Often the upper limit is not plain \(x\) but a function of \(x\). Then \(F(x)=\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt\) is a composition: the outer function is \(u\mapsto\int_a^u f(t)\,dt\) and the inner function is \(u=g(x)\). Differentiate by the Chain Rule from Lesson 6.03.

Part 1 with a variable upper limit
If \(f\) is continuous and \(g\) is differentiable, then \[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt=f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\cdot g'(x). \]

Example 1. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_0^{x^2}\cos t\,dt=\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x\). Swap \(t\) for the upper limit \(x^2\), then multiply by the derivative of the upper limit.

Example 2 (variable lower limit). \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_x^5 t^2\,dt\). Reversing the limits flips the sign of an integral, so this equals \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[-\displaystyle\int_5^x t^2\,dt\Bigr]=-x^2\).

Example 3. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_0^{3x}e^{-t^2}\,dt=e^{-(3x)^2}\cdot 3=3e^{-9x^2}\).

⚡ TRY IT · DIFFERENTIATING AN INTEGRAL
For each function defined by an integral, choose its derivative. Decide first which Chain Rule factor, if any, you owe.
1. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_1^x\sqrt{1+t^3}\,dt\)
2. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_0^{x^2}\cos t\,dt\)
3. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_x^5 t^2\,dt\)
4. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_0^{3x}e^{-t^2}\,dt\)
▶  Try it in your notebook first: let \(G(x)=\displaystyle\int_1^{x^3}\ln t\,dt\). Find \(G'(x)\) and then \(G'(2)\). Then reveal the check.
The integrand is \(\ln t\) and the upper limit is \(g(x)=x^3\), with \(g'(x)=3x^2\). So \(G'(x)=\ln(x^3)\cdot3x^2=9x^2\ln x\) (using \(\ln x^3=3\ln x\)). At \(x=2\): \(G'(2)=9\cdot4\cdot\ln2=36\ln2\approx24.95\). Notice we never needed a formula for \(G\) itself.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
A common slip is to forget the Chain Rule factor when the upper limit is not plain \(x\). Another is to write the answer in terms of \(t\) (the dummy variable) instead of \(x\). The derivative of an integral from \(a\) to \(g(x)\) is a function of \(x\) only; \(t\) never survives.
📚 PART 4 · READING F FROM THE GRAPH OF f, AND THE FINE PRINT

Part 1 also tells you what an accumulation function looks like, using only the graph of \(f\). Since \(F'=f\):

▶  Where \(f>0\), \(F\) is increasing; where \(f<0\), \(F\) is decreasing.
▶  Where \(f\) changes from positive to negative, \(F\) has a local maximum; from negative to positive, a local minimum (the First Derivative Test, Lesson 10.02, applied to \(F\)).
▶  Because \(F''=f'\), \(F\) is concave up where \(f\) is increasing and concave down where \(f\) is decreasing.

In the graph of this lesson, \(f\) is positive everywhere on \((0,6)\), so \(F\) only climbs. \(f\) is increasing on \((0,2)\), so \(F\) bends upward there; \(f\) is constant on \((2,4)\), so \(F\) is a straight line of slope \(3\); and \(f\) is decreasing on \((4,6)\), so \(F\) bends downward and levels off as \(f\) reaches \(0\). That matches the values \(0,4,10,13\) from Part 1.

The fine print. The theorem asks that \(f\) be continuous, and the hypothesis matters. Let \(f(t)=0\) for \(t<1\) and \(f(t)=1\) for \(t\ge1\), a step with a jump at \(t=1\). Its accumulation function from \(0\) is \(F(x)=0\) for \(x\le1\) and \(F(x)=x-1\) for \(x\ge1\). The function \(F\) is continuous, but at \(x=1\) it has a corner: slope \(0\) from the left and slope \(1\) from the right (Lesson 4.04). So \(F'(1)\) does not exist, even though \(F\) is perfectly well defined. Where \(f\) is continuous, \(F'=f\); at the jump, the theorem makes no promise, and in fact the promise fails.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
A computer algebra system or an AI tutor will happily differentiate \(\int_1^x\sqrt{1+t^3}\,dt\) and hand you \(\sqrt{1+x^3}\). That is correct, and a good check. But if a tool gives you an answer for a problem with a variable limit and you cannot say where the Chain Rule factor came from, you have not yet learned the theorem. Do the work, then use the tool to confirm it.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1, says that if \(f\) is continuous, then the accumulation function \(F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) satisfies \(F'(x)=f(x)\). Accumulating and then differentiating returns the original function, and with a variable upper limit \(g(x)\) the Chain Rule adds the factor \(g'(x)\). Lesson 14.02 turns this around to give Part 2: because differentiation undoes integration, a definite integral can be computed from any antiderivative, with no limit of sums at all.
