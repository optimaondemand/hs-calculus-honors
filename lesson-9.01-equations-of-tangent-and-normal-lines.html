📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
At one point, a curve and a straight line agree on direction. Which line is it, and what line stands at a right angle to it?
Since Module 4 you have computed derivatives for their own sake. Starting this quarter, the derivative goes to work. The first job is the oldest one in calculus: to write down, exactly, the line that touches a curve at a point and points the same way the curve does. That line is the tangent line, and the line at a right angle to it is the normal line. Today you will write both from an explicit formula and from an implicit equation, you will hunt for the points where the tangent is horizontal, and you will learn what to do when you are asked for a tangent line through a point that is not on the curve at all.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to write the equation of the tangent line and the normal line to a curve at a given point, whether the curve is given explicitly or implicitly.
2
I will be able to find every point where a curve has a horizontal tangent line, and write the tangent and normal lines there.
3
I will be able to find the tangent lines to a curve that pass through a point not on the curve, and check each answer.

📚 PART 1 · THE TANGENT LINE AND THE NORMAL LINE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Stand at the point \((1,1)\) on the parabola \(y=x^2\), where the tangent line has slope \(2\). Now picture the line through that same point that is perpendicular to the tangent, the one that leaves the curve at a right angle. Take a guess: what is its slope?

In Lesson 4.02 you met the tangent line as the best straight-line picture of a curve at one point. Its equation comes straight from the point-slope form of a line: if \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), the tangent line at \(x=a\) is

\[ y-f(a)=f'(a)\,(x-a)\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a). \]

Every tangent-line problem is the same three steps: find the point \((a,f(a))\), find the slope \(f'(a)\), and substitute into point-slope form. The only real work is the derivative, and Modules 5 through 7 gave you every tool you need for it.

The normal line to a curve at a point is the line through that point that is perpendicular to the tangent line there. Two non-vertical lines are perpendicular exactly when the product of their slopes is \(-1\) (you can check this by taking direction vectors \((1,m)\) and \((1,m')\): they are perpendicular when \(1+mm'=0\)). So if the tangent slope is \(m=f'(a)\ne0\), the normal slope is

\[ m_{\perp}=-\frac{1}{f'(a)},\qquad\text{so the normal line is}\qquad y-f(a)=-\frac{1}{f'(a)}\,(x-a). \]

The tangent and the normal to \(y=x^2\) at \((1,1)\) meet at a right angle.

Two special cases are worth knowing by name, because they are where the formula \(-1/f'(a)\) stops working. If \(f'(a)=0\), the tangent line is horizontal and the normal line is the vertical line \(x=a\), which has no slope. In the other direction, if the tangent line is vertical, the normal line is the horizontal line \(y=f(a)\). Lesson 9.02 is about that second case.

Example 1: a tangent line and a normal line

Let \(f(x)=x^3-2x+1\). Find the tangent line and the normal line at \(x=2\).

Point. \(f(2)=8-4+1=5\), so the point is \((2,5)\). Slope. \(f'(x)=3x^2-2\), so \(f'(2)=12-2=10\). Tangent line. \(y-5=10(x-2)\), which simplifies to \(y=10x-15\). Normal line. The normal slope is \(-\tfrac1{10}\), so \(y-5=-\tfrac1{10}(x-2)\), which simplifies to \(y=-\tfrac1{10}x+\tfrac{26}{5}\). Check. Both lines should pass through \((2,5)\): \(10(2)-15=5\) and \(-\tfrac{2}{10}+\tfrac{26}{5}=-0.2+5.2=5\).

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two slips cause most wrong answers on normal-line problems. The first is writing the normal slope as \(-f'(a)\) (negating but forgetting to flip). The second is using \(f(a)\), the height of the curve, where \(f'(a)\), the slope, belongs. A thirty-second check catches both: multiply your tangent slope by your normal slope. The product must be exactly \(-1\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Every normal line to a circle passes through the center. For \(x^2+y^2=25\) at \((3,4)\), implicit differentiation gives tangent slope \(-\tfrac34\), so the normal slope is \(\tfrac43\) and the normal line is \(y-4=\tfrac43(x-3)\), which simplifies to \(y=\tfrac43x\). That line goes through \((0,0)\), the center. Optics uses the normal line the same way: the angle at which light reflects off a mirror is measured from the normal to the surface, not from the surface itself.
📚 PART 2 · WHERE THE TANGENT IS HORIZONTAL

A horizontal tangent occurs where the slope is zero, so you find it by solving \(f'(x)=0\). This is a different question from the one in Part 1: instead of being handed the point, you search for it. Once you have each solution \(x=a\), the tangent line is the horizontal line \(y=f(a)\) and the normal line is the vertical line \(x=a\).

Example 2: a cubic

Find every horizontal tangent line to \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2+4\). Differentiate: \(f'(x)=3x^2-6x=3x(x-2)\). Setting \(f'(x)=0\) gives \(x=0\) and \(x=2\). Then \(f(0)=4\) and \(f(2)=8-12+4=0\). The horizontal tangent lines are \(y=4\) (touching the curve at \((0,4)\)) and \(y=0\) (touching at \((2,0)\)). The matching normal lines are the vertical lines \(x=0\) and \(x=2\).

Example 3: a function with a restricted domain

Find every horizontal tangent line to \(f(x)=x+\dfrac4x\). The domain excludes \(x=0\). Here \(f'(x)=1-\dfrac4{x^2}\), which is zero when \(x^2=4\), so \(x=\pm2\). Then \(f(2)=2+2=4\) and \(f(-2)=-2-2=-4\). The horizontal tangent lines are \(y=4\) and \(y=-4\). Notice that \(f'(x)\) is also undefined at \(x=0\), but \(x=0\) is not in the domain, so it produces no tangent line at all. Always check that a solution actually lies in the domain.

A point where \(f'(x)=0\) is a candidate for a local maximum or minimum, as Fermat's fact from Lesson 8.03 promised. It is a candidate and not a guarantee, and Module 10 is where you will learn to tell which is which.

📚 PART 3 · TANGENT AND NORMAL LINES TO IMPLICIT CURVES

When a curve is given implicitly, such as an ellipse or a circle, you cannot solve for \(y\) cleanly, but Lesson 7.01 gave you a better route: differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\) and solve for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). That formula, evaluated at the given point, is the tangent slope.

Example 4: an ellipse

Find the tangent and normal lines to \(x^2+4y^2=20\) at \((2,2)\). First confirm the point is on the curve: \(4+16=20\). \(\checkmark\) Differentiate both sides: \(2x+8y\,y'=0\), so \(y'=-\dfrac{x}{4y}\). At \((2,2)\): \(y'=-\dfrac{2}{8}=-\dfrac14\). The tangent line is \(y-2=-\tfrac14(x-2)\), or \(y=-\tfrac14x+\tfrac52\). The normal slope is \(4\), so the normal line is \(y-2=4(x-2)\), or \(y=4x-6\).

Example 5: horizontal tangents on a tilted ellipse

Find every point on \(x^2+xy+y^2=7\) where the tangent line is horizontal. Differentiate: \(2x+y+x\,y'+2y\,y'=0\), so \(y'(x+2y)=-(2x+y)\) and \(y'=-\dfrac{2x+y}{x+2y}\). A fraction is zero when its numerator is zero (and its denominator is not), so we need \(2x+y=0\), that is, \(y=-2x\). Substituting into the curve: \(x^2-2x^2+4x^2=3x^2=7\), so \(x=\pm\sqrt{7/3}=\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{21}}{3}\). The two points are \(\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{21}}3,\,-\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3\right)\approx(1.53,\,-3.06)\) and \(\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{21}}3,\,\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3\right)\approx(-1.53,\,3.06)\), with horizontal tangent lines \(y=-\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3\) and \(y=\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3\). At both points the denominator \(x+2y=-3x\neq0\), so the slope really is zero and not undefined. Keep this curve in mind: in Lesson 9.02, the denominator going to zero will be the point of the whole lesson.

▶  Try it first: find the tangent line and the normal line to \(f(x)=\sqrt x\) at \(x=4\). Then reveal the check.
\(f(4)=2\), and \(f'(x)=\dfrac1{2\sqrt x}\), so \(f'(4)=\dfrac14\). Tangent line: \(y-2=\tfrac14(x-4)\), which simplifies to \(y=\tfrac14x+1\). The normal slope is \(-4\), so the normal line is \(y-2=-4(x-4)\), which simplifies to \(y=-4x+18\). Check: \(\tfrac14\cdot(-4)=-1\), and both lines pass through \((4,2)\): \(\tfrac14(4)+1=2\) and \(-4(4)+18=2\).
📚 PART 4 · TANGENT LINES THROUGH A POINT OFF THE CURVE

Sometimes you are given a point that is not on the curve and asked for the tangent lines that pass through it. The usual instinct is to treat the given point as the point of tangency, and that instinct is wrong, because the point is not on the curve. The honest approach is to admit what you do not know: the point of tangency. Give it a name and let the algebra find it.

Example 6: tangent lines to a parabola through an outside point

Find the tangent lines to \(y=x^2\) that pass through \((1,-3)\). First note \((1,-3)\) is not on the curve, since \(1^2=1\ne-3\). Let the point of tangency be \((t,t^2)\). The slope there is \(2t\), so the tangent line at that point is \(y-t^2=2t(x-t)\), that is, \(y=2tx-t^2\). Require this line to pass through \((1,-3)\): \(-3=2t-t^2\), so \(t^2-2t-3=0\), so \((t-3)(t+1)=0\), and \(t=3\) or \(t=-1\). There are two tangent lines:

\[ t=3:\; y=6x-9\qquad\qquad t=-1:\; y=-2x-1. \]

Check both lines against the given point: \(6(1)-9=-3\) and \(-2(1)-1=-3\). \(\checkmark\) They touch the parabola at \((3,9)\) and \((-1,1)\) respectively.

Convince a classmate: in two sentences, why did we get two tangent lines here? What would you expect if the outside point were inside the parabola, for example \((0,5)\)? (Try the same algebra and see what happens to \(t\).)

▶  Try it first: find every tangent line to \(y=\dfrac1x\) that passes through \((0,3)\). Then reveal the check.
Let the point of tangency be \(\left(t,\tfrac1t\right)\). The slope is \(-\tfrac1{t^2}\), so the tangent line is \(y-\tfrac1t=-\tfrac1{t^2}(x-t)\), which simplifies to \(y=\tfrac2t-\tfrac{x}{t^2}\). Passing through \((0,3)\) requires \(3=\tfrac2t\), so \(t=\tfrac23\). There is exactly one tangent line, with slope \(-\tfrac94\): \(y=3-\tfrac94x\). Check: it touches the curve at \(\left(\tfrac23,\tfrac32\right)\), because \(3-\tfrac94\cdot\tfrac23=3-\tfrac32=\tfrac32=\dfrac1{2/3}\). \(\checkmark\)
🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool can draw a curve and a tangent line, and it will happily let you slide the point of tangency until the line looks right. That is a good way to check an answer like the one above, but a picture cannot tell you the exact value \(t=\tfrac23\), and it cannot tell you whether there are two tangent lines or only one. Do the algebra yourself, then use the tool to test it.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The tangent line at \(x=a\) is \(y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\), and the normal line has slope \(-1/f'(a)\) whenever \(f'(a)\) is neither zero nor undefined. The three questions of the day were: where is the tangent horizontal (solve \(f'(x)=0\)), what is the tangent at a given point of an implicit curve (differentiate implicitly), and which tangent lines pass through a point off the curve (let the point of tangency be \(t\) and solve). But every one of these used a slope that was a real number. Lesson 9.02 asks what happens when the slope is not: when the tangent line stands straight up.
