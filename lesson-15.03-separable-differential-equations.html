📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do you solve an equation whose unknown, a function, appears on both sides?
Last lesson you could only see the solutions of a differential equation: a slope field is a picture of the answer, not the answer itself. Today you learn to compute it. When the right-hand side of \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) factors into a part that depends only on \(x\) and a part that depends only on \(y\), you can separate the two, integrate each, and solve. The method is simple, and its honest use requires care about three things: checking the answer by substitution, stating where a solution is valid, and remembering the constant solutions that division can hide. This is Week 31 of the course.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to solve a separable differential equation by separating variables, integrating both sides, and applying an initial condition.

I will be able to decide whether a given differential equation is separable, and explain why a sum such as \(x+y\) is not.
3
I will be able to verify a solution by substitution, state the interval on which it is valid, and identify constant solutions that division can lose.

📚 PART 1 · SEPARATING VARIABLES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Lesson 15.02 taught you to read slope fields and to sketch solution curves by following the tick marks. Take \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{x}{y}\): its slope field is easy to draw, but is there a way to find those curves with algebra and integration instead of by eye?

Lesson 15.01 recovered a function from its derivative when the derivative was a function of \(x\) alone: \(y'=f(x)\) means \(y=\int f(x)\,dx\). The hard part of differential equations is that \(y\) usually appears on the right-hand side too. Today's technique handles the most important such case.

Separable equation
A differential equation is separable if it can be written \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=g(x)\,h(y)\), a product of a function of \(x\) alone and a function of \(y\) alone.

The method. Treat \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) as the ratio of \(dy\) to \(dx\) (Leibniz built his notation to permit this) and divide by \(h(y)\), multiply by \(dx\):

\[ \frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy=g(x)\,dx. \]

Now integrate both sides, one with respect to \(y\) and one with respect to \(x\), and put a single constant of integration on one side:

\[ \int \frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy=\int g(x)\,dx + C. \]

Why is this legitimate? Write \(\dfrac{1}{h(y)}\dfrac{dy}{dx}=g(x)\) and integrate both sides with respect to \(x\). On the left, the substitution rule (Lesson 14.04) says \(\int \dfrac{1}{h(y)}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\,dx=\int\dfrac{1}{h(y)}\,dy\). The “multiply both sides by \(dx\)” step is a shorthand for exactly that substitution, so the method is the Chain Rule run backward.

Example 1. Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{x}{y}\) with \(y(0)=2\).

Separate: \(y\,dy=x\,dx\). Integrate: \(\dfrac{y^2}{2}=\dfrac{x^2}{2}+C\), which we can write as \(y^2=x^2+K\) (where \(K=2C\) is just another constant). The condition \(y(0)=2\) gives \(4=0+K\), so \(K=4\) and \(y^2=x^2+4\). Because \(y(0)=2>0\), we keep the positive square root, so

\[ y=\sqrt{x^2+4}. \]

Check it, because a differential equation's answer can always be tested. \(y'=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}=\dfrac{x}{y}\) ✓, and \(y(0)=\sqrt4=2\) ✓. The family \(y^2=x^2+K\) is the set of hyperbolas you see below; the initial condition selects the one through \((0,2)\).

Solution curves of dy/dx = x/y: the family y² = x² + C, with the curve through (0, 2) highlighted.

Example 2. Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=2xy\) with \(y(0)=3\).

Separate: \(\dfrac{dy}{y}=2x\,dx\). Integrate: \(\ln|y|=x^2+C\). Exponentiate: \(|y|=e^{C}e^{x^2}\), so \(y=Ae^{x^2}\), where \(A=\pm e^{C}\) is any nonzero constant. The initial condition gives \(3=Ae^{0}=A\), so

\[ y=3e^{x^2}. \]

Check: \(y'=3e^{x^2}\cdot2x=2x\,y\) ✓, and \(y(0)=3\) ✓.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The step from \(\ln|y|=x^2+C\) to \(y=Ae^{x^2}\) trades the additive constant \(C\) for a multiplicative constant \(A=\pm e^{C}\). You will do this every time the left side integrates to a logarithm, and it is why the solutions of growth equations look like \(Ae^{kt}\). Lesson 7.03 proved that \(e^x\) is its own derivative; this is the moment that fact turns into a solution method.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=x^2y\) with \(y(0)=5\). Then reveal the check.
Separate: \(\dfrac{dy}{y}=x^2\,dx\). Integrate: \(\ln|y|=\dfrac{x^3}{3}+C\), so \(y=Ae^{x^3/3}\). With \(y(0)=5\): \(A=5\), so \(y=5e^{x^3/3}\). Check: \(y'=5e^{x^3/3}\cdot x^2=x^2y\) ✓.
📚 PART 2 · WHEN IT WORKS, WHEN IT FAILS, AND WHAT IT HIDES

A method is only as trustworthy as your knowledge of when it applies. Before you separate anything, ask: can the right-hand side be written as (something in \(x\)) times (something in \(y\))? If not, the method has no purchase.

⚡ TRY IT · SEPARABLE OR NOT?
For each equation, decide whether it is separable.
1. \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=x^2\,y\)
2. \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=x+y\)
3. \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{y\cos x}{1+y^2}\)
4. \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\sin(xy)\)

Example 3: a solution that division can hide. Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=y^2\) with \(y(0)=1\).

Separate: \(\dfrac{dy}{y^2}=dx\). Integrate: \(-\dfrac1y=x+C\), so \(y=-\dfrac{1}{x+C}\). From \(y(0)=1\): \(1=-\dfrac1C\), so \(C=-1\) and

\[ y=\frac{1}{1-x}. \]

Check: \(y'=\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^2}=y^2\) ✓, and \(y(0)=1\) ✓. Two honest observations. First, this solution exists only on \(x\lt 1\), the interval containing the starting point \(x=0\): as \(x\to1^-\), \(y\to\infty\), and the solution blows up in finite time. A differential equation does not promise a solution for all \(x\), and a solution should always be stated with the interval where it is valid. Second, look back at the step “divide by \(y^2\)”. That division is only legal where \(y\ne0\). The function \(y=0\) is a perfectly good solution (\(0'=0=0^2\)), but no value of \(C\) in \(y=-\dfrac{1}{x+C}\) produces it. It was lost when we divided.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Dividing by \(h(y)\) silently assumes \(h(y)\ne0\). Every number \(y_0\) with \(h(y_0)=0\) gives a constant solution \(y=y_0\) that separation can miss. Always solve \(h(y)=0\) before you divide, and list those constant solutions separately. Sometimes they are absorbed by the general formula (for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=2xy\), taking \(A=0\) gives \(y=0\)); sometimes, as with \(y^2\), they are not.

Example 4: a solution you cannot fully isolate. Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=e^{x-y}\) with \(y(0)=0\). Rewrite \(e^{x-y}=e^{x}e^{-y}\), so the equation is separable. Separate: \(e^{y}\,dy=e^{x}\,dx\). Integrate: \(e^{y}=e^{x}+C\). Use \(y(0)=0\): \(1=1+C\), so \(C=0\), \(e^{y}=e^{x}\), and \(y=x\). Check: \(y'=1\), and \(e^{x-y}=e^{0}=1\) ✓. For a different starting value, such as \(y(0)=\ln 4\), you would get \(4=1+C\), \(C=3\), and \(y=\ln(e^{x}+3)\). Check: \(y'=\dfrac{e^{x}}{e^{x}+3}\), and \(e^{x-y}=\dfrac{e^{x}}{e^{y}}=\dfrac{e^{x}}{e^{x}+3}\) ✓. Solving for \(y\) required a logarithm; sometimes the answer is left as an implicit relation such as \(y^2=x^2+4\), and that is a fully legitimate solution.

Convince a classmate: a classmate says “I separated, integrated, found \(y=-\dfrac{1}{x+C}\), and I'm done, so there are no other solutions.” Use \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=y^2\) to show what the classmate missed.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
A computer algebra system will solve a separable equation in one line, and it will usually hand you the general formula without telling you the interval where it is valid or which constant solutions it divided away. When you use a tool to check a differential equation, substitute its answer back into the equation yourself. A solution is something you can test; a tool's output is only a claim.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A separable equation \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=g(x)h(y)\) falls to two integrals, \(\int\dfrac{dy}{h(y)}=\int g(x)\,dx\), and the answer is not finished until you have checked it by substitution, stated where it is valid, and asked what the division by \(h(y)\) lost. Next lesson, Lesson 15.04, applies the method to the most important equation of all, \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=ky\), where the rate of change of a quantity is proportional to the quantity itself. Its solution is the exponential function, and it describes populations, radioactive decay, and money in the bank.
