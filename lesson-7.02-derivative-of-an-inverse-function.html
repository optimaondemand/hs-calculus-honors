📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One equation, differentiated implicitly, explains every inverse-function derivative you will ever need.
Lesson 6.04 found \(\frac{d}{dx}[\arcsin x]\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}[\arctan x]\) one at a time, each by a separate implicit-differentiation argument starting from \(\sin y = x\) or \(\tan y = x\). Today's lesson asks a more ambitious question: is there a single equation, differentiated once, that produces a rule for the derivative of any inverse function at all — without needing to know its formula? There is. Every inverse function \(g=f^{-1}\) satisfies exactly one equation, \(f(g(x))=x\), and implicit differentiation applied to that one equation is all it takes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to derive the general Inverse Function Rule, \((f^{-1})'(x) = \dfrac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(x))}\), from implicit differentiation.
2
I will be able to confirm that the general rule reproduces the arcsin and arctan derivatives already found in Lesson 6.04.
3
I will be able to find the derivative of an inverse function at a point, even with no explicit formula for the inverse itself.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, and implicit differentiation from Lesson 7.01.
▶
Your precalculus understanding of inverse functions: \(g=f^{-1}\) means \(f(g(x))=x\) for every \(x\) in the domain of \(g\), and the graphs of \(f\) and \(g\) are reflections of each other across the line \(y=x\).
📚 PART 1 · DERIVING THE GENERAL RULE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If \(g=f^{-1}\), then \(f(g(x))=x\) for every \(x\). If you differentiated both sides of that equation with respect to \(x\) right now, which rule would you need for the left side?

Let \(g=f^{-1}\), so that \(f(g(x))=x\) for every \(x\) in the domain of \(g\). Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). The left side is a composite function, so it needs the Chain Rule; the right side differentiates to 1:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(g(x))\big] = \frac{d}{dx}[x] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x) = 1 \]

Solve for \(g'(x)\), provided \(f'(g(x)) \ne 0\):

\[ \boxed{\big(f^{-1}\big)'(x) = \dfrac{1}{f'\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)}} \]

In words: to find the derivative of the inverse at \(x\), find where the inverse sends \(x\) (that is, \(f^{-1}(x)\)), evaluate the original function's derivative there, and take the reciprocal. This single equation is exactly the same move as Lesson 6.04's arcsin derivation, run in general rather than for one specific function.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
This rule has a picture behind it. The graphs of \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) are mirror images across the line \(y=x\), and reflecting a line across \(y=x\) swaps its rise and its run — which flips its slope to the reciprocal. \((f^{-1})'(x) = \frac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(x))}\) is that geometric fact, stated algebraically.
📚 PART 2 · CHECKING THE RULE AGAINST WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW

A genuinely general rule should reproduce every special case you have already proved by hand. Test it on \(\arcsin x\), the inverse of \(f(y) = \sin y\), where \(f'(y) = \cos y\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[\arcsin x] = \frac{1}{\cos(\arcsin x)} \]

Lesson 6.04 already worked out that \(\cos(\arcsin x) = \sqrt{1-x^2}\) (since \(\arcsin x\) is an angle whose sine is \(x\), and cosine is non-negative on its restricted range), so this becomes \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\) — exactly the formula Lesson 6.04 found the long way. The general rule and the specific derivation agree, as they must.

A genuinely new example: no formula required

Let \(f(x) = x^3+2x-1\). Since \(f'(x)=3x^2+2\) is always positive, \(f\) is increasing everywhere and has an inverse \(g=f^{-1}\), even though solving \(y=x^3+2x-1\) for \(x\) by hand is not realistic. Find \(g'(2)\).

The rule needs \(g(2) = f^{-1}(2)\): the input to \(f\) that outputs 2. Solve \(x^3+2x-1=2\), that is, \(x^3+2x-3=0\). Testing \(x=1\): \(1+2-3=0\). So \(g(2)=1\), even without a general formula for \(g\). Now apply the rule:

\[ g'(2) = \frac{1}{f'(g(2))} = \frac{1}{f'(1)} = \frac{1}{3(1)^2+2} = \frac{1}{5} \]

The derivative of the inverse at a point, found without ever writing down a formula for the inverse itself. This is the real power of the rule: it needs only one input value of the original function, found by inspection or a little algebra, not a full inverse formula.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
\((f^{-1})'(2)\) is not \(\dfrac{1}{f'(2)}\). The rule evaluates \(f'\) at \(f^{-1}(2)\), not at 2 itself. In the example above, that meant evaluating \(f'\) at \(g(2)=1\), not at \(x=2\). Skipping the step of finding \(g(2)\) first is the most common way this rule goes wrong.
