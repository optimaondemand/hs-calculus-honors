📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If you know how fast something is changing, how much has it changed? And if you know only the graph of the rate, what does the graph of the total look like?
Lessons 14.01 and 14.02 gave you the Fundamental Theorem in its two forms: accumulate a function and you get an antiderivative, and evaluate an antiderivative at two ends and you get a definite integral. Today both halves go to work. Read as a statement about change, the theorem says that the integral of a rate is a total. Read as a statement about shape, it says that the graph of \(f\) tells you everything about where its accumulation function rises, falls, peaks, and bends. Then, in Lesson 14.04, you add the last tool the module needs.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use the Net Change Theorem to find total change and final amount from a rate, with units and an initial value.
2
I will be able to find displacement and total distance traveled from a velocity function, using a sign chart to split the interval.
3
I will be able to describe where \(g(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\) increases, decreases, has extrema, and bends, from the graph of \(f\), and compute its values as signed areas.

📚 PART 1 · THE NET CHANGE THEOREM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A reservoir's volume changes at a rate of \(V'(t)=12-2t\) thousand liters per hour, where \(t\) is hours from midnight. You know the rate at every moment of the next eight hours. Do you know how much the volume will have changed by the end of hour 8?

Lesson 14.02 gave you the rule \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)\) where \(F'=f\). Read that rule with a different eye. Suppose \(F\) is some quantity (a volume, a position, a population) and \(F'\) is how fast that quantity is changing. Then the rule says something you can say out loud: the integral of a rate is the total change.

The Net Change Theorem
If \(F'\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then \[ \int_a^b F'(x)\,dx \;=\; F(b)-F(a). \] In words: the accumulated rate of change equals the net change in the quantity. Rearranged, \(F(b)=F(a)+\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx\): final amount = starting amount + net change.

Nothing new is being proved; this is the second half of the Fundamental Theorem read as a statement about the world. What is new is the habit of mind. Whenever a problem hands you a rate and asks for a total, an integral is waiting.

Worked example: the reservoir

The volume changes at \(V'(t)=12-2t\) thousand liters per hour for \(0\le t\le 8\). Find the net change in volume over those eight hours, and then the volume at hour 8 if the reservoir held 50 thousand liters at \(t=0\).

An antiderivative of \(12-2t\) is \(12t-t^2\). So

\[ \int_0^8 (12-2t)\,dt = \bigl[\,12t-t^2\,\bigr]_0^8 = (96-64)-0 = 32. \]

The volume rises by 32 thousand liters over the eight hours, so \(V(8)=50+32=82\) thousand liters.

▶  Predict first: is the volume still rising at hour 8? Then check it against the integral over the first six hours.
The rate \(12-2t\) is positive for \(t\lt 6\) and negative for \(t>6\), so the volume rises until hour 6 and falls afterward. Over \([0,6]\): \(\int_0^6(12-2t)\,dt=72-36=36\), so \(V(6)=86\). Over \([6,8]\): \(\int_6^8(12-2t)\,dt=(96-64)-(72-36)=-4\), so the volume falls back to \(V(8)=82\). Notice that the net change over \([0,8]\) is \(36+(-4)=32\): the falling part subtracts from the rising part, just as area below the axis counts as negative.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The integral of a rate gives the change, not the amount. If the problem asks for the volume at hour 8, finishing at \(32\) is a mistake: that is only how much the volume moved. You still have to add the starting amount, \(50+32=82\). Check your final line by reading the units and the question: “thousand liters of change” is not “thousand liters in the reservoir.”
📚 PART 2 · DISPLACEMENT AND TOTAL DISTANCE

Velocity is the rate of change of position, so the Net Change Theorem applies to motion immediately. If \(s'(t)=v(t)\), then

\[ \int_a^b v(t)\,dt = s(b)-s(a) = \text{displacement}. \]

Displacement is net change in position: it counts motion in the positive direction as positive and motion in the negative direction as negative, so a particle that walks 3 meters east and 3 meters back west has displacement 0. Back in Lesson 8.02 and Lesson 12.03 you separated displacement from total distance traveled, which counts every meter walked no matter which way. The integral gives displacement directly. For total distance you need the speed, \(|v(t)|\):

\[ \text{total distance} = \int_a^b |v(t)|\,dt. \]

In practice you do not integrate \(|v|\) in one step. You find where \(v\) changes sign, split the interval there, and integrate each piece with the right sign.

Worked example: a particle that turns around twice

A particle moves along a line with velocity \(v(t)=t^2-4t+3\) meters per second for \(0\le t\le 4\). Find its displacement and its total distance traveled.

Step 1, sign chart. Factor: \(v(t)=(t-1)(t-3)\). So \(v>0\) on \((0,1)\), \(v\lt 0\) on \((1,3)\), and \(v>0\) on \((3,4)\). The particle turns around at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\).

Step 2, one antiderivative. Let \(S(t)=\dfrac{t^3}{3}-2t^2+3t\), so \(S'=v\). The values at the key times are \(S(0)=0\), \(S(1)=\tfrac43\), \(S(3)=0\), \(S(4)=\tfrac43\).

Step 3, displacement. \(\displaystyle\int_0^4 v\,dt=S(4)-S(0)=\tfrac43\) meters.

Step 4, total distance. Integrate each piece, flipping the sign where \(v\lt 0\):

\[ \int_0^4|v|\,dt=\underbrace{\bigl(S(1)-S(0)\bigr)}_{\frac43}-\underbrace{\bigl(S(3)-S(1)\bigr)}_{-\frac43}+\underbrace{\bigl(S(4)-S(3)\bigr)}_{\frac43}=\tfrac43+\tfrac43+\tfrac43=4. \]

The particle ends only \(\tfrac43\) meters from where it started, yet it has traveled 4 meters. The two numbers answer different questions, and a problem will tell you which one it wants by the words it uses: displacement, net change, final position mean the plain integral; total distance, how far did it travel, distance covered mean the integral of \(|v|\).

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not get total distance by adding up \(\int_0^4 v\,dt\) and hoping. That gives \(\tfrac43\), the displacement. And do not drop the sign-chart step: if you integrate \(v\) straight through \([1,3]\) where \(v\) is negative, the negative area cancels a positive area you have already counted, and you undercount the distance.
⚡ TRY IT · Displacement or distance?
For each question about a particle with velocity \(v(t)\) on \([0,6]\), choose the integral that answers it.
1. “How far is the particle from its starting point at \(t=6\)?” (it started at the origin and ends at positive position)
2. “What is the total length of path the particle covered?”
3. “The odometer reading changes by how much?” (an odometer adds up distance, never subtracts)
📚 PART 3 · READING AN ACCUMULATION FUNCTION FROM A GRAPH

The same two ideas run in the opposite direction too. Lesson 14.01 showed that if you accumulate a function, \(g(x)=\displaystyle\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\), then \(g'(x)=f(x)\). That one fact, \(g'=f\), lets you read the shape of \(g\) straight off the graph of \(f\), without ever finding a formula for \(g\). The tools are the ones from Modules 10 and 11, with a change of names:

Reading \(g\) from the graph of \(f\)
Let \(g(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\), so \(g'=f\) and \(g''=f'\).
• \(g\) is increasing where \(f>0\) and decreasing where \(f\lt 0\).
• \(g\) has a local maximum where \(f\) crosses from positive to negative, and a local minimum where \(f\) crosses from negative to positive (the First Derivative Test, Lesson 10.02, applied to \(g\)).
• \(g\) is concave up where \(f\) is increasing and concave down where \(f\) is decreasing (Lesson 11.01, applied to \(g\), since \(g''=f'\)).
• \(g(0)=0\), and the value of \(g(x)\) is the signed area between the graph of \(f\) and the \(t\)-axis from \(0\) to \(x\).

Worked example: from a graph of f to a picture of g

The graph below shows \(f\): a straight line from \((0,2)\) down to \((4,-2)\), flat at \(-2\) from \(t=4\) to \(t=6\), then a straight line rising to \((8,0)\). Let \(g(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\).

Where is \(g\) increasing? \(f>0\) on \([0,2)\) and \(f\lt 0\) on \((2,8)\). So \(g\) rises until \(x=2\) and falls after, which makes \(x=2\) the location of the absolute maximum of \(g\) on \([0,8]\) (\(f\) crosses from positive to negative there).

How does \(g\) bend? \(f\) is decreasing on \((0,4)\), constant on \((4,6)\), and increasing on \((6,8)\). So \(g\) is concave down on \((0,4)\), a straight line on \((4,6)\) (slope \(-2\)), and concave up on \((6,8)\). The slope of \(g\) is as steep as it ever gets downward at \(x\) between 4 and 6, and it eases to \(0\) at \(x=8\).

What are the values? Signed areas, one piece at a time (each is a triangle, trapezoid, or rectangle, so you need no antiderivative):

\[ g(2)=\tfrac12(2)(2)=2,\quad g(4)=2-2=0,\quad g(6)=0-4=-4,\quad g(8)=-4-2=-6. \]

That last piece deserves a second look: on \([6,8]\) the graph of \(f\) lies below the axis (from \(-2\) up to \(0\)) and encloses a triangle of area 2, so it contributes \(-2\) to the integral and \(g(8)=-4-2=-6\).

▶  Commit first: estimate \(g(3)\), \(g(5)\) and \(g(7)\) using areas, then check.
From \(t=2\) to \(t=3\) the graph of \(f\) drops from \(0\) to \(-1\): a triangle of area \(\tfrac12\) below the axis, so \(g(3)=2-\tfrac12=\tfrac32\). From \(t=4\) to \(t=5\) the graph is flat at \(-2\): a rectangle of area 2 below the axis, so \(g(5)=0-2=-2\). From \(t=6\) to \(t=7\) the graph rises from \(-2\) to \(-1\): a trapezoid of area \(\tfrac{(2+1)}{2}\cdot1=\tfrac32\), so \(g(7)=-4-\tfrac32=-\tfrac{11}{2}\). Together with \(g(0)=0,\,g(1)=\tfrac32,\,g(2)=2\), these values trace the picture you described: up to a peak at \(x=2\), then down, steepest through \([4,6]\), flattening at the end.
🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool or a computer algebra system can draw \(g\) for you in a second. Use that second as a check, after you have predicted where \(g\) rises, falls, peaks and bends from the graph of \(f\) alone. If the tool disagrees with your prediction, one of you has made a mistake, and finding out which is where the learning happens.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The integral of a rate is a total: \(\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\) is the net change, and the final amount is the starting amount plus that change. Displacement integrates the velocity; total distance integrates its absolute value, and a sign chart tells you where to split. Turned around, \(g'=f\) lets you read the shape of an accumulation function off the graph of \(f\). Lesson 14.04 adds the last technique of the module: when an antiderivative is hiding behind a chain-rule pattern, substitution brings it out.
