📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How little do you have to know about a function to draw its whole graph?
Since Module 1 you have collected tools: limits and asymptotes, the first derivative's sign, the second derivative's sign. Each one answers one question about a graph. Today they work as a team. You will follow a single checklist from the formula to the finished picture, and you will see that a sketch built this way is not a guess dressed up as a graph. It is an argument, with every curve segment licensed by a sign chart and every landmark by a computation.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to carry out the full curve-sketching checklist (domain, intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes, \(f'\) and \(f''\) sign charts) for a polynomial and a rational function.
2
I will be able to combine the sign of \(f'\) and the sign of \(f''\) into four graph shapes and use them to join landmark points correctly.
3
I will be able to explain why a zero of \(f'\) or \(f''\), or a change of sign across an asymptote, is not by itself an extremum or an inflection point.

▶
Your math notebook, a pencil, and graph paper if you like. A graphing tool is for checking a finished sketch, never for producing one.

📚 PART 1 · THE CHECKLIST
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You are asked to sketch \(y = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x\) with no graphing tool. What would you do first?

Sketching a curve was a serious mathematical art long before calculators. In 1704 Isaac Newton appended a treatise on cubic curves to his Opticks and sorted them into dozens of species by their shapes, working with equations and a pen. What changed with the derivative is that the work became a procedure: a checklist whose every step has a reason. You now own every tool on that checklist. Lessons 11.01 and 11.02 gave you the last two, concavity and inflection points. Today you assemble them.

The Curve-Sketching Checklist
  1. Domain. Where is \(f\) defined? Any gaps or breaks?
  2. Intercepts. Solve \(f(0)\) for the \(y\)-intercept and \(f(x)=0\) for the \(x\)-intercepts, when the algebra allows.
  3. Symmetry. If \(f(-x)=f(x)\) the graph is mirrored across the \(y\)-axis; if \(f(-x)=-f(x)\) it is symmetric about the origin.
  4. Asymptotes and end behavior. Vertical asymptotes from the domain breaks; horizontal asymptotes and end behavior from limits at \(\pm\infty\) (Lessons 2.01 and 2.02).
  5. First derivative. Find the critical points, make a sign chart for \(f'\), and read increasing and decreasing and the local extrema (Lessons 10.01 and 10.02).
  6. Second derivative. Make a sign chart for \(f''\) and read concavity and inflection points (Lessons 11.01 and 11.02).
  7. Assemble. Plot the landmark points, add the asymptotes, and join them following the signs of \(f'\) and \(f''\).

Two habits make the checklist work. First, decide the order of evidence: domain, asymptotes, and symmetry come from \(f\) itself and cost almost nothing, so do them first. Second, let the two sign charts agree with each other. A graph that is increasing and concave down looks like the left side of a hill; decreasing and concave up looks like the left side of a valley. Four combinations of sign, four shapes, and the sketch is those four shapes joined at the landmarks.

\(f'\)\(f''\)What the graph is doing
++Rising and getting steeper (the right side of a valley)
+−Rising and flattening out (the left side of a hill)
−−Falling and getting steeper (the right side of a hill)
−+Falling and flattening out (the left side of a valley)
📚 PART 2 · A POLYNOMIAL, STEP BY STEP

Example 1. Sketch \(f(x)=x^3-6x^2+9x\). Factor first: \(f(x)=x(x-3)^2\). Every step below is a checklist line.

Domain, intercepts, symmetry. A polynomial is defined for every real number. The factored form gives the \(x\)-intercepts \(x=0\) and \(x=3\), and \(f(0)=0\). The factor \((x-3)^2\) is squared, so at \(x=3\) the graph touches the axis and turns back rather than crossing it. Neither \(f(-x)=f(x)\) nor \(f(-x)=-f(x)\), so there is no symmetry.

End behavior. The leading term is \(x^3\), so \(f(x)\to+\infty\) as \(x\to+\infty\) and \(f(x)\to-\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\). A polynomial has no asymptotes.

\[ f'(x)=3x^2-12x+9=3(x-1)(x-3), \qquad f''(x)=6x-12=6(x-2). \]

First derivative. The critical points are \(x=1\) and \(x=3\). Second derivative. \(f''(x)=0\) at \(x=2\) only, and \(f''\) changes sign there. Now organize both sign charts together:

The landmarks follow. At \(x=1\), \(f'\) changes from \(+\) to \(-\), a local maximum, and \(f(1)=1\cdot 4=4\). At \(x=3\), \(f'\) changes from \(-\) to \(+\), a local minimum, and \(f(3)=0\). (The Second Derivative Test agrees: \(f''(1)=-6<0\) and \(f''(3)=6>0\).) At \(x=2\), concavity changes from down to up, so \((2,\,f(2))=(2,2)\) is an inflection point.

The finished sketch of y = x(x - 3)^2. Gold dots are the landmarks the two sign charts produced.

Notice how little work the sketch needed: four points, all supplied by the derivatives, and a rule for the shape between them. Now check yourself on reading the charts.

⚡ TRY IT · READ THE CHARTS
Use Example 1's sign charts for \(f(x)=x(x-3)^2\) to answer each row.
1. On \((2,3)\) we have \(f'<0\) and \(f''>0\). What is the graph doing there?
2. At \(x=3\), \(f'(3)=0\) and \(f''(3)=6\). What does the Second Derivative Test say?
3. Why is \((2,2)\) an inflection point, not just a place where \(f''=0\)?
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
A zero of \(f'\) or \(f''\) is a candidate, never a conclusion. Check the sign chart on both sides. For \(y=x^4\), \(f''(0)=0\) but concavity never changes, so there is no inflection point; for \(y=x^3\), \(f'(0)=0\) but the function is increasing on both sides, so there is no extremum. Signs decide, not zeros.
📚 PART 3 · A RATIONAL FUNCTION AND ITS ASYMPTOTES

Example 2. Sketch \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2}{x^2-4}\). A rational function brings in the first three checklist lines in earnest, because the algebra of \(f\) itself tells you a great deal before any calculus.

Domain. The denominator \(x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)\) vanishes at \(x=\pm2\), so the domain is every real number except \(\pm2\). Intercepts. \(f(0)=0\), and \(f(x)=0\) only at \(x=0\). Symmetry. \(f(-x)=\dfrac{x^2}{x^2-4}=f(x)\), so \(f\) is even: the graph is mirrored across the \(y\)-axis, and we only need to understand \(x\ge 0\).

Asymptotes. From Lessons 2.01 and 2.02: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{x^2}{x^2-4}=1\), so \(y=1\) is a horizontal asymptote on both ends. Near \(x=2\) the numerator is close to \(4\) and the denominator is close to \(0\), so the sign of the denominator decides the direction: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2^+}f(x)=+\infty\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2^-}f(x)=-\infty\). By the symmetry, \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-2^-}f(x)=+\infty\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-2^+}f(x)=-\infty\).

\[ f'(x)=\frac{-8x}{(x^2-4)^2}, \qquad f''(x)=\frac{8(3x^2+4)}{(x^2-4)^3}. \]

Reading \(f'\). The denominator \((x^2-4)^2\) is positive wherever it is defined, so the sign of \(f'\) is the sign of \(-8x\): positive for \(x<0\), negative for \(x>0\). The only critical point inside the domain is \(x=0\) (the values \(\pm2\) make \(f'\) undefined, but they are not in the domain, so they are not critical points). \(f'\) changes from \(+\) to \(-\) at \(0\), so \((0,0)\) is a local maximum.

Reading \(f''\). The numerator \(8(3x^2+4)\) is always positive, so the sign of \(f''\) is the sign of \((x^2-4)^3\), which is the sign of \(x^2-4\). That is positive when \(|x|>2\) and negative when \(|x|<2\). \(f''\) is never zero.

Interval\((-\infty,-2)\)\((-2,0)\)\((0,2)\)\((2,\infty)\)
Sign of \(f'\)++−−
Sign of \(f''\)+−−+
Shape of \(f\)rising, concave up (from 1 up to \(+\infty\))rising, concave down (from \(-\infty\) up to 0)falling, concave down (from 0 down to \(-\infty\))falling, concave up (from \(+\infty\) down toward 1)
The finished sketch of y = x^2/(x^2 - 4). Dashed lines are the asymptotes x = -2, x = 2 and y = 1.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
Concavity does change at \(x=2\) and \(x=-2\), yet there are no inflection points on this graph. An inflection point has to be a point on the graph, and \(f\) is not defined at \(\pm2\). The sign chart for \(f''\) changes across an asymptote, not across a point of the curve. Check the domain before you name an inflection point.

📚 PART 4 · YOUR TURN: WHEN THE EVIDENCE SURPRISES YOU

A sketch is a claim: this is the shape the evidence demands. Now try a function where the evidence has a surprise in it. Do the whole checklist on paper before you open the reveal.

▶  Try it first: sketch \(f(x)=x^4-4x^3\) using the checklist.
Setup. Domain: all real numbers. Factor: \(f(x)=x^3(x-4)\), so the \(x\)-intercepts are \(x=0\) and \(x=4\), and \(f(0)=0\). End behavior: \(f\to+\infty\) at both ends (even degree, positive leading coefficient). No symmetry, no asymptotes.

Derivatives. \(f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2=4x^2(x-3)\) and \(f''(x)=12x^2-24x=12x(x-2)\).

Sign charts. \(f'\) is zero at \(x=0\) and \(x=3\), but \(4x^2\ge0\) means the sign of \(f'\) is the sign of \(x-3\): negative on \((-\infty,0)\), negative on \((0,3)\), positive on \((3,\infty)\). So \(x=0\) is a critical point where \(f'\) does not change sign: not an extremum. At \(x=3\) the sign goes \(-\) to \(+\): a local (and absolute) minimum, \(f(3)=81-108=-27\).
\(f''\) is zero at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\) and changes sign at both: positive on \((-\infty,0)\), negative on \((0,2)\), positive on \((2,\infty)\). So \((0,0)\) and \((2,-16)\) are both inflection points.

The surprise. The point \((0,0)\) is a critical point and an inflection point: the tangent is horizontal there, but the graph is falling on both sides and it flips from concave up to concave down. The graph comes down from the upper left, flattens briefly at the origin, keeps falling through the inflection point at \((2,-16)\), bottoms out at \((3,-27)\), then rises.
🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool can draw \(y=x^4-4x^3\) in a second. It cannot tell you why the origin is both flat and a bend, and in a poorly chosen window it can hide the inflection point at \(x=2\) entirely. Use a graphing tool to check a sketch you have already reasoned out, then make sure you can explain every feature on it with a sign chart. If the tool and your sketch disagree, find out which of you made the error.

Convince a classmate: Explain to a classmate why a sketch with the correct landmarks can still be wrong, and what the sign charts of \(f'\) and \(f''\) add that the landmarks alone do not.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A good curve sketch is an argument: gather the evidence from \(f\) itself (domain, intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes), then from \(f'\) and \(f''\) sign charts, and join the landmarks with the four shapes the signs allow. Zeros of derivatives are candidates, and signs make the decision. Lesson 11.04 reverses the direction: instead of building a graph from a formula, you will be handed a graph of \(f\), \(f'\), or \(f''\) and asked to work out the other two.
