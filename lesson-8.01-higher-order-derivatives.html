📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If the derivative measures how a function changes, what does it mean to ask how the change itself is changing?
Since Module 4 you have turned a function \(f\) into a new function \(f'\). Nothing stops you from doing it again. \(f'\) is a function, so it has a derivative of its own: the second derivative \(f''\). And \(f''\) has a derivative, \(f'''\), and so on for as long as the function allows. These are the higher-order derivatives. Leibniz was already writing the second derivative as \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\) in the 1680s, and Joseph-Louis Lagrange later gave us the compact prime notation you have been using. Today you learn to read both notations, to see the patterns that repeated differentiation produces (some functions die out, some cycle, some refuse to change at all), and to say in plain words what \(f''\) measures. Tomorrow, that last idea becomes the language of motion.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to compute \(f''\), \(f'''\), and \(f^{(n)}\) for polynomial, trigonometric, exponential, and logarithmic functions, using the rules from Modules 5 through 7.
2
I will be able to read and write higher-order derivatives in both prime notation and Leibniz notation, and avoid the confusions between \(f^{(n)}\), \(f^n\), and \((f')^2\).
3
I will be able to describe what \(f''\) measures as the rate of change of the slope, and predict an \(n\)th derivative from the pattern of the first few.

📚 PART 1 · THE DERIVATIVE OF THE DERIVATIVE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You differentiate \(f(x)=x^3\) and get \(f'(x)=3x^2\). If you differentiate \(f'\) once more, what are you really asking about the original curve?

Repeated differentiation is just the derivative applied to its own output. Each application earns a new name:

  • The first derivative: \(f'(x)\), or \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).
  • The second derivative: \(f''(x)\), or \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\). In Leibniz notation, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\!\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)\) is written \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\).
  • The third derivative: \(f'''(x)\), or \(\dfrac{d^3y}{dx^3}\).
  • The \(n\)th derivative, once primes get unwieldy: \(f^{(n)}(x)\), or \(\dfrac{d^ny}{dx^n}\). The parentheses matter: \(f^{(4)}\) means "differentiate four times," while \(f^4\) would mean \(f\) raised to the fourth power.

Worked example: differentiate until it stops

Let \(f(x)=x^5-4x^3+2x\). Differentiate repeatedly, one clean line at a time, using only the Power Rule and the Sum Rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 5x^4-12x^2+2 \\ f''(x) &= 20x^3-24x \\ f'''(x) &= 60x^2-24 \\ f^{(4)}(x) &= 120x \\ f^{(5)}(x) &= 120 \\ f^{(6)}(x) &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

Read what happened. Each derivative lowered the degree by one, so after five derivatives the polynomial of degree 5 had become the constant \(120\), and the sixth derivative is \(0\) and stays \(0\) forever. Here is the reason: the \(n\)th derivative of \(x^n\) is \(n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots 1 = n!\), a constant, and the derivative of a constant is zero. So a polynomial of degree \(n\) has \(f^{(n+1)}(x)=0\). Check the leading term above: \(1\cdot 5! = 120\) ✓.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Three look-alikes are not the same thing. \(f''(x)\) is the derivative of the derivative. \((f'(x))^2\) is the square of the derivative. And \(f(x)^2\) is the square of the function. For \(f(x)=x^3\): \(f''(x)=6x\), but \((f'(x))^2=(3x^2)^2=9x^4\). Likewise \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\) is not \(\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^2\): in the first the 2 counts differentiations, in the second it counts a square. Keep the notation exact and the arithmetic follows.
📚 PART 2 · PATTERNS THAT APPEAR

Polynomials run out of derivatives. Other functions behave very differently, and the first few derivatives usually announce the pattern. Your job is to differentiate three or four times, notice, and then state the \(n\)th derivative with confidence. That is exactly how mathematicians guess a general law before proving it.

Pattern 1: the cycle of period four (sine and cosine)

\[ \sin x \;\to\; \cos x \;\to\; -\sin x \;\to\; -\cos x \;\to\; \sin x \;\to\; \cdots \]

After four differentiations you are exactly where you started: \(f^{(4)}(x)=\sin x\) for \(f(x)=\sin x\). So the derivatives repeat in a cycle of length 4, and to find any high derivative you only need the remainder when the order is divided by 4. For example, \(100\) is a multiple of 4, so \(\dfrac{d^{100}}{dx^{100}}[\sin x]=\sin x\). For \(\sin 2x\) the Chain Rule adds a factor of 2 each time: \(\dfrac{d^2}{dx^2}[\sin 2x]=-4\sin 2x\), and \(\dfrac{d^4}{dx^4}[\sin 2x]=16\sin 2x\).

Pattern 2: the function that never changes (the exponential)

Since \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[e^x]=e^x\), every derivative of \(e^x\), of every order, is \(e^x\) itself: \(f^{(n)}(x)=e^x\). It is the one nonzero function that is its own derivative, which is why it sits at the center of growth and decay. Add a product and a pattern still appears. For \(f(x)=xe^x\) the Product Rule gives

\[ f'(x)=(x+1)e^x,\qquad f''(x)=(x+2)e^x,\qquad f'''(x)=(x+3)e^x, \]

each step adding 1 to the bracket, so the conjecture is \(f^{(n)}(x)=(x+n)e^x\).

Pattern 3: factorials appear (reciprocal and logarithm)

Differentiate \(f(x)=x^{-1}=\dfrac1x\) with the Power Rule and watch the coefficients:

\[ f'=-x^{-2},\quad f''=2x^{-3},\quad f'''=-6x^{-4},\quad f^{(4)}=24x^{-5} \]

The coefficients \(1,\,2,\,6,\,24\) are the factorials \(1!,\,2!,\,3!,\,4!\), and the signs alternate. So \(f^{(n)}(x)=(-1)^n\,n!\,x^{-(n+1)}\). Now \(\ln x\): since \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x]=\dfrac1x\), the derivatives of \(\ln x\) are the derivatives of \(\dfrac1x\), shifted down one order:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[\ln x]=x^{-1},\quad \frac{d^2}{dx^2}[\ln x]=-x^{-2},\quad \frac{d^3}{dx^3}[\ln x]=2x^{-3},\quad \frac{d^4}{dx^4}[\ln x]=-6x^{-4}, \]

which suggests \(\dfrac{d^n}{dx^n}[\ln x]=(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!\,x^{-n}\). Test \(n=1\): \((-1)^0\,0!\,x^{-1}=x^{-1}\) ✓. Test \(n=3\): \((-1)^2\,2!\,x^{-3}=2x^{-3}\) ✓.

🤖 AI AND COMPUTER ALGEBRA
A computer algebra system can print the 40th derivative of almost any function in a blink. It cannot tell you why the output looks the way it does, and it will happily hand you a long expression you could not check. The skill worth having is the one you just practiced: differentiate a few times by hand, spot the cycle or the factorial, and predict the answer yourself. Then, and only then, use a tool to confirm. A pattern you can state is knowledge; a pattern you merely received is not.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find \(f''(x)\) for \(f(x)=x^2\ln x\) \((x>0)\). Then reveal the check.
Product Rule first: \(f'(x)=2x\ln x + x^2\cdot\dfrac1x = 2x\ln x + x\). Then differentiate again, using the Product Rule on \(2x\ln x\): \(f''(x)=2\ln x + 2x\cdot\dfrac1x + 1 = 2\ln x + 3\). Two things to notice: the \(x\) from the term \(x^2\cdot\frac1x\) was already simplified before you differentiated again, which saves work, and the result \(2\ln x+3\) is a perfectly good function on its own.

📚 PART 3 · WHAT THE SECOND DERIVATIVE SAYS

Why bother with \(f''\) at all? Because it carries real information about the shape and behavior of the original function. Since \(f'\) is the slope of \(f\), the second derivative \(f''\) is the rate at which the slope is changing.

Worked example: the slope of the slope

Take \(f(x)=x^3\), so \(f'(x)=3x^2\) and \(f''(x)=6x\). Compare two points:

\[ \begin{aligned} x=2:&\quad f'(2)=12,\quad f''(2)=12 \\ x=-1:&\quad f'(-1)=3,\quad f''(-1)=-6 \end{aligned} \]

At \(x=2\) the slope is \(12\) and \(f''(2)=12>0\): the slope is increasing as \(x\) moves right, so the curve is getting steeper. At \(x=-1\) the slope is \(3\), still positive, but \(f''(-1)=-6<0\): the slope is decreasing as \(x\) moves right, so the curve is still rising but flattening out. Two numbers, \(f'\) and \(f''\), tell you not just where the curve is heading but how its heading is bending. In Quarter 3 this idea becomes concavity and the whole art of curve sketching. For now, hold on to the sentence: \(f''>0\) means the slope is increasing; \(f''<0\) means the slope is decreasing.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
You have felt a second derivative your whole life. When a car's speedometer needle climbs, you feel pushed back into the seat. Speed is the first derivative of position; that push is the second derivative. Isaac Newton's famous law, force equals mass times acceleration, is a statement about a second derivative: the physical world is written, at its most basic, in second derivatives of position. Tomorrow's lesson makes that precise.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A higher-order derivative is nothing new: it is the derivative applied again to its own output. Polynomials run out of derivatives, sine and cosine cycle with period four, \(e^x\) never changes, and \(\frac1x\) and \(\ln x\) produce factorials, so differentiating three or four times and noticing the pattern lets you predict \(f^{(n)}\). The second derivative \(f''\) measures how fast the slope is changing. Lesson 8.02 puts that to work on the most natural second derivative of all: the acceleration of a moving object.
