📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
No gaps. No jumps. No broken promises.
In Module 1 you learned that a limit only asks what a function approaches near a point; it never asks what the function equals at the point itself. Today you meet the functions where those two questions finally agree everywhere they are asked. When what a function approaches at a point matches exactly what it equals there, we say the function is continuous at that point. The definition has three separate conditions, and today you will learn to check each one by name, so that when continuity fails, you can say precisely why.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state and apply the three-part definition of continuity at a point.
2
I will be able to identify, when a function is not continuous at a point, exactly which of the three conditions fails.
3
I will be able to decide whether a function is continuous on an interval, including at the endpoints of a closed interval.

📚 PART 1 · THE THREE CONDITIONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Picture a graph with an open circle at \((3, 5)\): the curve climbs toward height 5 as \(x\) approaches 3 from both sides, but no point is actually plotted at \((3,5)\). Is this function continuous at \(x = 3\)? Make your best guess before reading on. (No wrong answer.)

Three conditions, checked in order

A function \(f\) is continuous at \(x = a\) when all three of the following are true.

1. \(f(a)\) is defined: the function actually has a value there.

2. \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) \) exists: the two one-sided limits from Lesson 1.04 agree and are finite.

3. \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a) \): the value the function approaches matches the value it actually takes there.

A function is continuous on an interval when it is continuous at every point of that interval. Each of the three conditions asks a separate, honest question about the function, and each one can fail on its own, independent of the other two.

1
Defined
\(f(a)\) exists
2
Has a limit
\( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \) exists
3
They agree
\( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a) \)
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
For centuries, mathematicians described a continuous curve the way Leonhard Euler did: one you could trace without lifting your pen. That picture is vivid, but it is not precise enough to settle hard cases. In 1817 Bernard Bolzano, and a few years later Augustin-Louis Cauchy, replaced the picture with the three-part test above, built entirely from the limit. Precision did not destroy the intuition; it gave the intuition something firm to stand on.
📚 PART 2 · WORKED EXAMPLES: TESTING ALL THREE CONDITIONS

Worked example 1: a familiar function, tested honestly

Recall \( f(x) = \dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2} \) from Lesson 1.01, where you found \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x) = 4 \). Is \(f\) continuous at \(x = 2\)?

Condition 1: \(f(2)\) requires dividing by zero, so \(f(2)\) is undefined. Condition 1 already fails.

The limit existing does not rescue the function. \(f\) is not continuous at \(x = 2\), because the very first condition, that the function be defined there, is never met. The limit answers a different question than continuity does.

Worked example 2: two conditions holding is not enough

Let \( g(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 & x \ne 1 \\ 3 & x = 1 \end{cases} \). Is \(g\) continuous at \(x = 1\)?

Condition 1: \(g(1) = 3\), defined.

Condition 2: \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1} g(x) = \lim_{x\to 1} x^2 = 1\), exists.

Condition 3: does the limit equal \(g(1)\)? Here \(1 \ne 3\), so condition 3 fails. Even though \(g\) is defined at 1 and the limit exists, \(g\) is not continuous at \(x = 1\), because the value it approaches and the value it actually takes there disagree.

⚠️ WATCH OUT
Passing two of the three conditions is not enough. All three must hold at the same point for continuity to hold there. Get in the habit of checking them in order and naming, out loud, which one first breaks down.
📚 PART 3 · CONTINUITY ON AN INTERVAL

A function is continuous on an open interval when it is continuous at every point inside it. On a closed interval \([a,b]\), the definition also asks about the endpoints: at \(a\) we only require the limit from the right to match \(f(a)\), and at \(b\) we only require the limit from the left to match \(f(b)\), since there is no "other side" to approach from at an endpoint.

Every polynomial function is continuous at every real number. This is not a new fact to memorize; it falls directly out of the limit laws from Lesson 1.02. Direct substitution is always valid for a polynomial, which means \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} p(x) = p(a) \) at every \(a\): the three conditions hold automatically, everywhere.

Explain to a classmate: why is every polynomial function continuous at every real number? Use the limit laws from Lesson 1.02 as your evidence, not just the word "obviously."
⚡ TRY IT · NAME WHAT FAILS
For each situation, decide what it tells you about continuity at the point in question.
1. \(f(5)\) is undefined, but \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 5} f(x) = 12 \).
2. \(h(2) = 7\), and \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} h(x) = 7 \).
3. One-sided limits at \(x=4\): \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4^-} k(x) = 3\) and \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4^+} k(x) = 9\).
4. \(m(0) = 2\), and \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0} m(x) \) exists and equals 5.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: test whether \( p(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1} & x \ne 1 \\ 2 & x = 1 \end{cases} \) is continuous at \(x = 1\) by checking all three conditions in order. Then reveal the check.
Condition 1: \(p(1) = 2\), defined. Condition 2: for \(x \ne 1\), \( \dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1} = \dfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x+1 \), so \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1} p(x) = 2\), exists. Condition 3: the limit (2) equals \(p(1)\) (2). All three conditions hold, so \(p\) is continuous at \(x = 1\). Notice that this function looks, at first glance, like it should have a hole at \(x=1\), but it was defined so that the hole is filled at exactly the right height. That kind of repair is exactly the idea you will use in the next lesson to classify what a discontinuity even is.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Continuity is not a vibe; it is three conditions, checked in order: is the function defined here, does it have a limit here, and do the two agree? When you can name exactly which condition fails, you have said something honest and precise about the function, not just a hunch about its picture. In the next lesson you will sort every way those conditions can fail into three named kinds of discontinuity.
