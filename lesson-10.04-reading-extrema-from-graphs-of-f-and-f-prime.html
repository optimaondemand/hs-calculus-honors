📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can find where a function peaks and dips without ever seeing the function, as long as you have its derivative's graph.
A graph of \(f'\) is a map of the slopes of \(f\): above the axis \(f\) rises, below the axis \(f\) falls, and a crossing is a turning point. Today you practice reading that map in both directions, from the graph of \(f'\) to the behaviour of \(f\), and from the graph of \(f\) to a description of \(f'\). You will meet functions with corners and cusps, where the derivative does not exist but extrema still occur, and three traps that catch careful students. This lesson completes the module: you now hold the whole toolkit for extrema.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read the graph of \(f'\) to state where \(f\) is increasing and decreasing and to locate and classify its local extrema.
2
I will be able to read the graph of \(f\), including graphs with corners and cusps, to describe the sign of \(f'\) and identify the critical points of both kinds.
3
I will be able to explain why a peak of \(f'\) is not an extremum of \(f\), and why a sign change of \(f'\) requires that \(f\) be continuous.

📚 PART 1 · FROM THE GRAPH OF f′ TO THE GRAPH OF f
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You are shown only the graph of a function's derivative, and it crosses the horizontal axis at one point, going from positive values to negative values. What can you say about the original function \(f\) at that point?

Everything in this module has come down to one fact: the sign of \(f'\) controls the direction of \(f\) (Lesson 10.02), and critical points are the only places where that direction can switch (Lesson 10.01). Today you use those facts in both directions, reading a graph of \(f'\) to describe \(f\), and reading a graph of \(f\) to describe \(f'\). Look at the graph below. It is the graph of \(f'\), not of \(f\).

The graph of f′, with zeros at x = −3, 1, and 4. Positive where the curve is above the axis, negative where it is below.

Step 1: sign chart. The curve is above the axis (so \(f'>0\)) for \(x\lt -3\), below it (\(f'\lt 0\)) on \((-3,1)\), still below it on \((1,4)\), and above it again for \(x>4\). At \(x=1\) the curve touches the axis and turns back without crossing. So \(f\) is increasing on \((-\infty,-3)\) and \((4,\infty)\) and decreasing on \((-3,4)\). Notice that the decreasing interval is one unbroken interval: the touch at \(x=1\) does not interrupt it.

Step 2: classify the zeros of \(f'\). At \(x=-3\), \(f'\) goes from \(+\) to \(-\): rising, then falling. So \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x=-3\). At \(x=4\), \(f'\) goes from \(-\) to \(+\): falling, then rising, so \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x=4\). At \(x=1\), \(f'=0\) but the sign does not change (negative on both sides). The graph of \(f\) levels off for an instant and keeps falling, so there is no extremum at \(x=1\). That is the same lesson as \(f(x)=x^3\) at \(x=0\) in Lesson 10.01: a critical point is a candidate, not a guarantee.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
The graph of \(f'\) tells you the direction of \(f\), not its heights. From this picture you can say where \(f\) has a local maximum and a local minimum, but you cannot say which is higher, or what \(f(-3)\) equals. Many different functions share one derivative graph; by Lesson 8.04 they differ only by a constant, and that constant moves the whole graph of \(f\) up or down.

📚 PART 2 · FROM THE GRAPH OF f TO THE GRAPH OF f′

Now reverse the direction. Suppose you are given the graph of \(f\). You can read the sign of \(f'\) from its steepness and tilt: where the graph of \(f\) rises, \(f'>0\); where it falls, \(f'\lt 0\); where it has a smooth peak or valley, \(f'=0\). Corners and cusps need extra care, because the derivative does not exist there. The function \(f(x)=|x^2-4|\) is a good test: it takes the parabola \(x^2-4\) and flips the part below the axis upward.

The graph of f(x) = |x² − 4| on [−3, 3].

The derivative, piece by piece. For \(|x|>2\), \(x^2-4>0\), so \(f(x)=x^2-4\) and \(f'(x)=2x\). For \(|x|\lt 2\), \(x^2-4\lt 0\), so \(f(x)=4-x^2\) and \(f'(x)=-2x\). At \(x=\pm2\) the one-sided slopes disagree: at \(x=2\), the slope from the left is \(-4\) and from the right is \(+4\). So \(f'(\pm2)\) does not exist. These are critical points of the second kind (Lesson 10.01), where \(f'\) is undefined, and \(f\) is continuous there, so the sign test still works.

Sign chart for \(f'\). On \((-\infty,-2)\): \(f'=2x\lt 0\), so \(f\) is decreasing. On \((-2,0)\): \(f'=-2x>0\), increasing. On \((0,2)\): \(f'=-2x\lt 0\), decreasing. On \((2,\infty)\): \(f'=2x>0\), increasing. So \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x=-2\) (value \(0\)), a local maximum at \(x=0\) (value \(4\)), and a local minimum at \(x=2\) (value \(0\)). Two of the extrema sit at corners where \(f'\) does not exist. A common instinct is that every extremum has a horizontal tangent; this graph shows that the corners count too.

Connecting to Lesson 10.03. On the closed interval \([-3,3]\), the candidates for absolute extrema are the critical points \(x=-2,0,2\) and the endpoints \(x=\pm3\): \(f(-3)=5\), \(f(-2)=0\), \(f(0)=4\), \(f(2)=0\), \(f(3)=5\). So the absolute maximum value is \(5\), attained at both endpoints, and the absolute minimum value is \(0\), attained at \(x=-2\) and \(x=2\). The local maximum \(4\) at \(x=0\) is not the absolute maximum.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Two more shapes to recognize. A cusp such as \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\) has a vertical tangent from both sides and a sharp bottom at \(x=0\): \(f'\) changes from negative to positive, so \(f\) has a local minimum even though \(f'(0)\) does not exist. A corner pointing up, as in \(f(x)=-|x|\), has \(f'\) change from \(+1\) to \(-1\), so \(f\) has a local maximum at \(0\) with no derivative there.

📚 PART 3 · THREE TRAPS

Reading graphs is where small misunderstandings do their damage. Three traps are worth a careful look, and each one is an honest question: what does this evidence actually prove?

Trap 1: the peak of \(f'\) is not a peak of \(f\). A high point on the graph of \(f'\) means \(f\) is changing fastest there; it does not mean \(f\) has an extremum there. Extrema of \(f\) happen where \(f'\) crosses the axis. For example, \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2\) has \(f'(x)=3x^2-6x=3x(x-2)\), whose lowest point is at \(x=1\), where \(f'(1)=-3\). But \(f(1)=-2\) is neither a maximum nor a minimum of \(f\): at that moment \(f\) is falling as steeply as it ever does. The true extrema are where \(f'\) crosses the axis: \(f(0)=0\) is a local maximum and \(f(2)=-4\) is a local minimum.

Trap 2: a sign change needs a continuous function. The test “\(f'\) changes sign, so \(f\) has an extremum” assumes \(f\) is continuous at that point. Define \(f(x)=x\) for \(x\lt 0\) and \(f(x)=-1-x\) for \(x\ge0\). Then \(f'=1\) on the left and \(f'=-1\) on the right: a sign change from \(+\) to \(-\). But \(f(0)=-1\), while the values just to the left are close to \(0\) (higher than \(-1\)) and the values just to the right are below \(-1\). So \(f(0)\) is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum. The jump in the graph broke the rule, as the theorem's fine print warned.

Trap 3: not every critical point is smooth. When you list the critical points of a piecewise or absolute-value function, include both kinds: where \(f'=0\), and where \(f'\) does not exist but \(f\) is defined (corners, cusps). Then test each by the sign of \(f'\) on both sides.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: the derivative of \(f\) is \(f'(x)=3x^2-6x\). Use only the sign of \(f'\) to list the intervals where \(f\) increases and decreases, then classify every critical point. Then reveal the check.
\(f'(x)=3x(x-2)\) is zero at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\). Test values: \(f'(-1)=9>0\), \(f'(1)=-3\lt 0\), \(f'(3)=9>0\). So \(f\) is increasing on \((-\infty,0)\) and \((2,\infty)\) and decreasing on \((0,2)\). At \(x=0\) the sign goes \(+\) to \(-\): a local maximum. At \(x=2\) the sign goes \(-\) to \(+\): a local minimum. The lowest point of the graph of \(f'\) (at \(x=1\)) tells you where \(f\) is falling fastest, not where it turns.

Convince a classmate: a friend points to the lowest point of a graph of \(f'\) and says “that's where \(f\) has its minimum.” In two sentences, say what that point actually tells you about \(f\), and where you should look instead.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
If you give an AI tool a graph and ask it to “find the maximum,” ask it first which graph it thinks this is: \(f\), \(f'\), or \(f''\). Mixing these up is the most common error in graph questions, for people and for machines. Before accepting an answer, re-derive it from the sign chart: where does the curve you are looking at cross the axis, and what does that mean for the function underneath it?

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The derivative is a map of the function it came from: its sign says whether \(f\) rises or falls, its zeros and sign changes mark the turning points, and corners and cusps need their own honest check. Read the right graph, make the sign chart, and say what the evidence proves and no more. Next module, Lesson 11.01 (Concavity and the Second Derivative) asks a new question about the same graphs: not just whether a curve is rising or falling, but how it is bending. The point on the graph of \(f'\) that fooled you in Trap 1, where \(f\) is changing fastest, will turn out to be exactly where the bending changes.
