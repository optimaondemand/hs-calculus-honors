📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A ball thrown straight up is, for one instant at the top of its flight, not moving at all. Is anything still happening to it?
Yes, and the second derivative is the reason. At the top the ball's velocity is zero, yet gravity keeps pulling it down at exactly the same rate as a moment before; its velocity is about to turn negative. Galileo spent years rolling balls down inclined planes to measure this, and discovered around 1604 that a falling body covers distance in proportion to the square of the time, which is the signature of constant acceleration. A generation later, calculus gave the whole story a precise language: position, velocity, and acceleration are three functions in a chain, each the derivative of the one before. Today you read that chain, and learn to look at the signs of two numbers and say exactly what a moving object is doing.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to compute velocity, speed, and acceleration from a position function, and state the units of each.
2
I will be able to decide whether an object is speeding up, slowing down, or momentarily at rest by comparing the signs of its velocity and acceleration.
3
I will be able to analyze motion along a line with a turnaround, and distinguish displacement from total distance traveled.

📚 PART 1 · POSITION, VELOCITY, ACCELERATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A particle's position at time \(t\) is \(s(t)\). Velocity is how fast the position changes. What do you think acceleration is, in terms of the derivative?

Suppose an object moves along a straight line, and \(s(t)\) is its position at time \(t\), measured from a fixed starting point with a chosen positive direction. Then:

  • Velocity is \(v(t)=s'(t)\). Its sign gives direction (positive means moving in the positive direction; negative, the opposite way) and its size gives how fast.
  • Speed is \(|v(t)|\), the size of the velocity without the direction.
  • Acceleration is \(a(t)=v'(t)=s''(t)\), the rate at which velocity changes.

Units come straight from the derivative as a ratio. If \(s\) is in feet and \(t\) is in seconds, then \(v\) is in feet per second (ft/s), and \(a\) is in feet per second per second, written ft/s². Always carry units in your final answers.

Worked example: a ball thrown upward

A ball is thrown straight up from a platform 80 feet above the ground. Its height in feet after \(t\) seconds is \(s(t)=-16t^2+64t+80\). Differentiate twice:

\[ \begin{aligned} s(t) &= -16t^2+64t+80 \\ v(t) &= s'(t) = -32t+64 \\ a(t) &= v'(t) = -32 \end{aligned} \]

The acceleration is the constant \(-32\) ft/s², which is gravity's pull near the Earth's surface (about \(-9.8\) m/s² in metric units); the minus sign says it points downward. Now ask three questions.

When does the ball reach its highest point? At the top it momentarily stops, so \(v(t)=0\): \(-32t+64=0\), giving \(t=2\) seconds. Its height is \(s(2)=-16(4)+64(2)+80=144\) feet.

When does it hit the ground? Set \(s(t)=0\): \(-16t^2+64t+80=0\). Dividing by \(-16\) gives \(t^2-4t-5=0\), which factors as \((t-5)(t+1)=0\). The root \(t=-1\) is before the throw and is rejected, so the ball lands at \(t=5\) seconds.

How fast is it going at impact? \(v(5)=-32(5)+64=-96\) ft/s. The velocity is \(-96\) ft/s (downward) and the speed is \(96\) ft/s.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Velocity and speed are not the same thing, and a negative velocity is not a slow velocity. \(v=-96\) ft/s means the ball is moving downward at 96 ft/s: that is fast. Direction lives in the sign; size lives in the absolute value. Likewise a negative acceleration does not by itself mean the object is slowing down. Whether it speeds up or slows down depends on how the acceleration's sign compares with the velocity's, which is the subject of Part 2.

3. At \(t=2\): \(v(2)=0\) and \(a(2)=-32\) ft/s². The ball is…
📚 PART 2 · READING THE SIGNS

Part 1 ended with a rule hiding in plain sight. Look at the signs of \(v\) and \(a\) together:

\(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign: the object is speeding up.
\(v\) and \(a\) have opposite signs: the object is slowing down.
\(v=0\): the object is momentarily at rest; if \(v\) changes sign there, it has turned around.

The reason is that speed is \(|v|\). If \(v>0\) and \(a>0\), the velocity is positive and getting larger, so \(|v|\) grows. If \(v\lt 0\) and \(a\lt 0\), the velocity is negative and getting more negative, so \(|v|\) grows again. If the signs differ, the acceleration is pulling the velocity toward zero, so \(|v|\) shrinks.

Worked example: a particle that turns around

A particle moves along a line with position \(s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t\) meters for \(t\ge 0\) seconds. Differentiate and factor:

\[ \begin{aligned} v(t) &= 3t^2-12t+9 = 3(t-1)(t-3) \\ a(t) &= 6t-12 = 6(t-2) \end{aligned} \]

The velocity is zero at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\); the acceleration is zero at \(t=2\). Those three times cut the timeline into pieces, and on each piece the signs of \(v\) and \(a\) do not change. Test one point in each piece:

IntervalSign of \(v\)Sign of \(a\)DirectionSpeed
\(0\lt t\lt 1\)\(+\)\(-\)positive directionslowing down
\(1\lt t\lt 2\)\(-\)\(-\)negative directionspeeding up
\(2\lt t\lt 3\)\(-\)\(+\)negative directionslowing down
\(t>3\)\(+\)\(+\)positive directionspeeding up

(Test points: \(t=0.5\) gives \(v=3.75,\ a=-9\); \(t=1.5\) gives \(v=-2.25,\ a=-3\); \(t=2.5\) gives \(v=-2.25,\ a=3\); \(t=3.5\) gives \(v=3.75,\ a=9\).) The particle starts at \(s(0)=0\), moves right and stops at \(s(1)=4\), turns around, passes \(s(2)=2\), stops again at \(s(3)=0\), and turns around once more.

Displacement versus distance

Over the first four seconds the particle's displacement, its net change in position, is \(s(4)-s(0)=4-0=4\) meters, since \(s(4)=64-96+36=4\). But the total distance traveled counts every step: \(0\to 4\) (4 m), then \(4\to 0\) (4 m), then \(0\to 4\) (4 m), a total of \(12\) meters. Displacement can be much smaller than distance, because motion in opposite directions cancels in the first and adds in the second. To find total distance you must split the interval at every turnaround, which is why finding where \(v=0\) matters.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Your car's speedometer reports \(|v|\), the speed, which is why it never shows a negative number when you reverse. The sign of velocity is information a speedometer throws away. Spacecraft engineers cannot afford to throw it away: a probe that is slowing down toward a planet and one that is speeding up away from it can show the same speed, but need entirely opposite corrections.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: a particle has position \(s(t)=t^3-9t^2+24t\) for \(t\ge0\). Find when it is momentarily at rest, its position at those times, and its acceleration at those times. Then reveal the check.
\(v(t)=3t^2-18t+24=3(t-2)(t-4)\), so the particle is at rest at \(t=2\) and \(t=4\). Positions: \(s(2)=8-36+48=20\) and \(s(4)=64-144+96=16\). Reading the story: \(v>0\) before \(t=2\) (testing \(t=1\) gives \(v=9\)), \(v\lt 0\) between 2 and 4 (testing \(t=3\) gives \(v=-3\)), and \(v>0\) after 4. So the particle moves right, turns around at position 20 (where \(a\lt 0\) pulls it back), moves left, turns around again at position 16 (where \(a>0\) pushes it forward), and moves right from then on.
📚 PART 3 · A QUESTION FOR NEXT TIME

Notice what you have been doing in this lesson: given a formula, you found the exact velocity at each instant. Now run the idea in the other direction, with measurements instead of a formula. Suppose a car covers 120 miles in exactly 2 hours. Its average velocity over the trip is \(\dfrac{120}{2}=60\) mph. The speedometer, of course, did not sit at 60 the whole time; the driver sped up and slowed down.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
The trip's average velocity was exactly 60 mph. Must the car's speedometer have read exactly 60 mph at some single instant during the trip?

Your intuition about that question is already a mathematical claim about derivatives and averages. Lesson 8.03 makes it precise, states when it is guaranteed, and proves it. 💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Position, velocity, and acceleration form a chain of derivatives: \(v=s'\) and \(a=v'=s''\). Compare the signs of \(v\) and \(a\): the same sign means speeding up, opposite signs mean slowing down, and \(v=0\) marks a moment of rest or a turnaround. Displacement is net change in position, while total distance requires splitting at every turnaround. Lesson 8.03 asks a question this lesson only hinted at: if you know the average velocity over a stretch of time, must the instantaneous velocity equal it somewhere? Rolle's Theorem and the Mean Value Theorem answer yes, and prove it.
