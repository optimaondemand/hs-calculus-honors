📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Spin any flat region around a line and the solid you make has circles for cross-sections, so its volume is nearly written for you.
In Lesson 16.03 you built volumes from cross-sections of your choosing. Today the cross-sections are chosen by geometry: rotate a region about a line and every slice perpendicular to that line is a disk or a ring. That turns \(A(x)\) into \(\pi R^2\) or \(\pi(R^2-r^2)\), and the integral does the rest. You will derive the volumes of the sphere and the cone, find volumes of vase-like solids, handle regions that leave a hole in the middle, and spin regions about axes other than the coordinate axes. This is the last new technique of the semester, and it ties back to the oldest results in the course's story, to Archimedes and Kepler.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use the disk method \(V=\pi\int R^2\,dx\) (or \(dy\)) to find the volume of a solid formed by rotating a region about the \(x\)-axis or the \(y\)-axis.
2
I will be able to use the washer method \(V=\pi\int (R^2-r^2)\,dx\) when the region does not touch the axis, identifying \(R\) and \(r\) as distances from the axis of rotation.
3
I will be able to set up volumes for regions rotated about lines such as \(y=2\) or \(y=-1\), starting from a sketch.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, and room to sketch a region, an axis, and a typical slice.
▶
The cross-section method from Lesson 16.03, the area-between-curves setup from Lesson 16.01, and the integration techniques of Module 14.
▶
The habit of sketching first. For solids of revolution, the sketch is the setup.
📚 PART 1 · SPINNING A REGION INTO DISKS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Take the flat region under the curve \(y=\sqrt{x}\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=4\) and spin it all the way around the \(x\)-axis. What is the shape of a slice of the solid cut by a plane perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis?

Lesson 16.03 showed that a volume is \(\int A\,dx\) once you know the cross-section area. A solid of revolution is a solid made by spinning a flat region about a line. It is the shape a potter's wheel or a lathe produces, a vase or a bowl or a table leg, and it has a gift for us: every cross-section perpendicular to the axis of spin is a circle (a disk) or a ring (a washer). So \(A\) is always built from \(\pi\) and a radius.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Johannes Kepler, in 1615, wrote a short book on the volumes of wine barrels, Nova stereometria doliorum vinariorum. The occasion was practical. At his wedding feast he watched a merchant gauge a barrel with a rod pushed in through the bung hole, and he doubted the rule. He imagined barrels, and many other solids of revolution, as stacks of thin disks. It is the same picture you are about to use, sixty years before Newton and Leibniz gave it a notation.

The disk method

Spin the region between \(y=f(x)\) and the \(x\)-axis, for \(a\le x\le b\), about the \(x\)-axis. The slice at \(x\) is a disk of radius \(R=f(x)\), so \(A(x)=\pi R^2=\pi\,[f(x)]^2\), and

\[ V=\pi\int_a^b [f(x)]^2\,dx. \]

The gold segment of length \(R=\sqrt{x}\) is the radius of the thin disk at position \(x\). The dashed curve is the mirror image of the boundary, to show the full width the spin sweeps out.

Worked example 1. Spin the region under \(y=\sqrt{x}\) on \([0,4]\) about the \(x\)-axis. The radius is \(R=\sqrt x\), so \(A=\pi(\sqrt x)^2=\pi x\), and

\[ V=\pi\int_0^4 x\,dx=\pi\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^4=8\pi. \]

Checking the method on solids you already know. Spin \(y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}\), the upper half of a circle of radius \(r\), about the \(x\)-axis on \([-r,r]\). The solid is a sphere, and

\[ V=\pi\int_{-r}^{r}(r^2-x^2)\,dx=\pi\left[r^2x-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-r}^{r}=\pi\left(2r^3-\frac{2r^3}{3}\right)=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3. \]

Now spin the line \(y=\dfrac{r}{h}x\) on \([0,h]\), which makes a cone of height \(h\) and base radius \(r\):

\[ V=\pi\int_0^h \frac{r^2}{h^2}x^2\,dx=\pi\,\frac{r^2}{h^2}\cdot\frac{h^3}{3}=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h. \]

Both are the classical formulas, and now you know why they are true. It is a pleasing fact, proved by Archimedes with an entirely different argument, that a cone, a sphere, and a cylinder of matching dimensions have volumes in the ratio \(1:2:3\) (cone of height \(2r\), sphere of radius \(r\), cylinder of radius \(r\) and height \(2r\)). Archimedes asked that a sphere inside a cylinder be carved on his tomb.

Rotating about the \(y\)-axis. If the region is described with \(x\) as a function of \(y\), slice perpendicular to the \(y\)-axis and integrate in \(y\). Spin the region bounded by \(y=x^2\), the \(y\)-axis, and \(y=4\) about the \(y\)-axis. At height \(y\) the radius of the disk is the distance from the axis to the curve, \(x=\sqrt y\), so

\[ V=\pi\int_0^4 (\sqrt y)^2\,dy=\pi\int_0^4 y\,dy=8\pi. \]

📚 PART 2 · WASHERS, AND OTHER AXES OF ROTATION

When the region does not touch the axis of rotation, the solid has a hole through it, and each cross-section is a washer: a disk with a smaller disk removed. If the outer radius is \(R\) and the inner radius is \(r\), the washer has area \(\pi R^2-\pi r^2\), so

\[ V=\pi\int_a^b \left(R^2-r^2\right)dx. \]

Here \(R\) is the distance from the axis to the far edge of the region, and \(r\) the distance from the axis to the near edge. For a region between two curves, with the axis along the \(x\)-axis, \(R\) is the upper curve and \(r\) the lower.

At position \(x\), the gold segment is the outer radius \(R=x\) and the navy segment is the inner radius \(r=x^2\). The washer is the gold disk with the navy disk removed.

Worked example 2. The region between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) on \([0,1]\) is spun about the \(x\)-axis. The line is farther from the axis than the parabola on this interval, so \(R=x\) and \(r=x^2\):

\[ V=\pi\int_0^1 \left(x^2-x^4\right)dx=\pi\left(\frac13-\frac15\right)=\frac{2\pi}{15}. \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The washer area is \(\pi R^2-\pi r^2\), not \(\pi(R-r)^2\). In the example above, \(\pi\int_0^1 (x-x^2)^2\,dx=\dfrac{\pi}{30}\), which is far too small: it is the volume of a solid whose radius is the gap between the curves, not the volume of a ring. Square each radius first, then subtract.

Worked example 3: the same region, spun about the \(y\)-axis. Now the slices are perpendicular to the \(y\)-axis, so we describe the curves with \(x\) as a function of \(y\): the line is \(x=y\) and the parabola is \(x=\sqrt y\). For \(0\le y\le 1\), \(\sqrt y\ge y\), so the parabola is the farther edge, with \(R=\sqrt y\), \(r=y\):

\[ V=\pi\int_0^1\left(y-y^2\right)dy=\pi\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)=\frac{\pi}{6}. \]

The same region gave two different solids, \(\tfrac{2\pi}{15}\) and \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\), because the axis of rotation changed what \(R\) and \(r\) mean. This is why the sketch comes first.

Worked example 4: an axis that is not a coordinate axis. Spin the region under \(y=\sqrt x\) on \([0,4]\) about the horizontal line \(y=2\). Distances are now measured from \(y=2\), not from \(y=0\). The region lies below the line. Its far edge from the axis is the \(x\)-axis (distance \(2-0=2\)), and its near edge is the curve (distance \(2-\sqrt x\)):

\[ V=\pi\int_0^4\left[2^2-\left(2-\sqrt x\right)^2\right]dx=\pi\int_0^4\left(4\sqrt x-x\right)dx=\pi\left[\frac{8}{3}x^{3/2}-\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^4=\frac{40\pi}{3}. \]

⚡ TRY IT · IDENTIFY R AND r
The region between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\), for \(0\le x\le 1\), is spun about different horizontal lines. For each axis, pick the outer radius \(R\) at position \(x\).
1. About the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\))
2. About the line \(y=1\) (above the region)
3. About the line \(y=-1\) (below the region)
▶  Try it in your notebook first: the region between \(y=x^2\) and \(y=1\) is spun about the line \(y=-1\). Sketch it, name \(R\) and \(r\), and set up the volume before opening this.
The curves meet at \(x=\pm1\), and \(y=1\) is above the parabola. Measured from \(y=-1\): the far edge is \(y=1\), so \(R=1-(-1)=2\); the near edge is the parabola, so \(r=x^2+1\). Then \(V=\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\left[4-(x^2+1)^2\right]dx=\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\left(3-2x^2-x^4\right)dx=\pi\left(6-\dfrac43-\dfrac25\right)=\dfrac{64\pi}{15}.\)

Convince a classmate: Two classmates compute the volume obtained by spinning the same region about the \(x\)-axis and about the \(y\)-axis, and they get different answers. Explain why this does not mean either of them made an error.

📚 PART 3 · THE SETUP ROUTINE, AND WHAT THIS MODULE HAS BEEN DOING

A full setup for any solid of revolution follows one routine, and it is the same routine as Lesson 16.03 with a rotating twist. (1) Sketch the region and the axis. (2) Draw one typical slice perpendicular to the axis, and ask whether it is a disk or a washer. (3) Label the radii as distances from the axis, not from the origin. (4) Choose the variable that runs along the axis, so the slice is perpendicular to it, and find its limits. (5) Integrate \(\pi R^2\) or \(\pi(R^2-r^2)\).

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
There is another way to find these volumes, called the method of cylindrical shells, which slices parallel to the axis instead of perpendicular to it. It is a fine technique and you may meet it in later study, but it is not needed in this course. Disks and washers, together with the cross-section method of Lesson 16.03, find every volume you will be asked for here.
🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool can draw a solid of revolution, and a computer algebra system can integrate \(\pi(R^2-r^2)\). Neither can tell you whether the region lies above or below the axis, so neither can choose \(R\) and \(r\) for you. Use tools to check a result, for example to confirm that your washer integral for a region spun about \(y=2\) agrees with the number you found by hand, but make the setup your own work, and keep a special case such as a sphere or a cone in mind as a test.

Look back over this module. Lesson 16.01 turned the integral into an area between curves. Lesson 16.02 turned it into an average value. Lessons 16.03 and 16.04 turned it into a volume. In every case a quantity that is spread out, changing from place to place, was cut into pieces small enough to be nearly constant, and the pieces were added back together exactly. That is what the integral is. Week 35, Semester 2 Finals, is your chance to step back and see how the whole semester fits together.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A solid of revolution is a stack of disks or washers. Sketch the region and the axis, draw one slice perpendicular to the axis, measure \(R\) and \(r\) as distances from the axis, and integrate \(\pi R^2\) or \(\pi(R^2-r^2)\) over the length of the axis, never \(\pi(R-r)^2\). Behind every volume is the pattern that has run through the whole year: cut a changing quantity into pieces so small they are nearly constant, add them up, and let the pieces shrink.
