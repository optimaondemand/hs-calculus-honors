📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The derivative of \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) does not exist at \(0\). Yet the graph has a perfectly good tangent line there. How can both be true?
Lesson 9.01 assumed every slope was a real number. But a curve can climb so steeply at a point that its slope is not a number at all, and the curve is still perfectly smooth there. Today is about that edge case. You will see that \(f'(a)\) failing to exist covers several different pictures: a corner, a vertical tangent, a cusp, a jump. You will learn the tests that tell them apart, and you will learn to look for them on purpose, in explicit functions and in implicit curves, because they are exactly the places a careless formula quietly breaks.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define a vertical tangent line with the difference quotient, and decide whether a function has one at a given point.
2
I will be able to distinguish a vertical tangent, a cusp, a corner, and a smooth point by the one-sided slopes of a function.
3
I will be able to find the points where an explicit function or an implicit curve has a vertical tangent line, and write the line.

📚 PART 1 · WHEN THE SLOPE IS INFINITE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
The graph of \(y=\sqrt[3]{x}\) passes through the origin, rising so steeply there that it looks almost straight up and down. Before any algebra, what do you expect the tangent line to be at the origin?

In Lesson 4.02 you defined the derivative at a point as the limit of the difference quotient, and in Lesson 4.04 you learned that a function can fail to have a derivative even where it is continuous. The usual example was the corner of \(|x|\). Today there is a second, very different way for \(f'(a)\) to fail to exist, and it looks nothing like a corner. The difference quotient can run off to infinity.

Recall the secant slope through \((a,f(a))\) and \((a+h,f(a+h))\) is \(\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\). As \(h\to0\) the secant lines usually settle toward a tangent line with a finite slope. But suppose the secant slopes grow without bound, getting steeper and steeper. Then the secant lines settle toward a vertical line instead.

Vertical tangent line
The graph of \(f\) has a vertical tangent line at \(x=a\) if \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) and the difference quotient goes to the same infinity from both sides: \[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}=+\infty\quad\text{or}\quad-\infty. \] The tangent line is the vertical line \(x=a\). At a left or right endpoint of the domain, the one-sided limit is enough.

Notice what this says about the derivative. A vertical line has no slope, so \(f'(a)\) does not exist, because the limit is not a real number. And yet the graph has a perfectly good tangent line there. Both statements are true at once, and keeping them straight is the discipline of this lesson.

Example 1: \(f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}\) at \(x=0\)

The function is continuous at \(0\) with \(f(0)=0\). The difference quotient is \(\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\dfrac{h^{1/3}}{h}=h^{-2/3}=\dfrac1{\sqrt[3]{h^2}}\). For every \(h\ne0\), \(h^2>0\), so this is positive, and as \(h\to0\) from either side it grows without bound. So the limit is \(+\infty\) from both sides, and the graph has a vertical tangent line \(x=0\) at the origin. This matches the formula from the extended power rule (Lesson 5.03): \(f'(x)=\tfrac13x^{-2/3}=\dfrac1{3\sqrt[3]{x^2}}\), which is undefined at \(0\) and grows without bound as \(x\to0\) from either side.

Example 2: a shifted cube root

Let \(f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x-2}+1\). Shifting the graph of \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) right \(2\) and up \(1\) moves the vertical tangent to the point \((2,1)\). Check: \(f'(x)=\dfrac1{3\sqrt[3]{(x-2)^2}}\), which grows without bound as \(x\to2\) from either side, and \(f\) is continuous at \(2\). The vertical tangent line is \(x=2\).

Example 3: \(f(x)=\sqrt{x}\) at the endpoint \(x=0\)

The domain is \(x\ge0\), so only \(h>0\) makes sense. The quotient is \(\dfrac{\sqrt h}{h}=\dfrac1{\sqrt h}\to+\infty\) as \(h\to0^+\). The graph has a vertical tangent line \(x=0\) at its left endpoint, which is the one-sided case the definition allows. You can see it in the graph: \(y=\sqrt x\) is the upper half of the parabola \(x=y^2\) turned on its side, and a parabola's tangent at its vertex is perpendicular to its axis.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
“\(f'(a)\) does not exist” does not mean “vertical tangent.” At the corner of \(|x|\) the derivative does not exist, and there is no tangent line at all. At a jump discontinuity the derivative does not exist, and again there is no tangent. A vertical tangent needs two things: continuity at the point and an infinite difference quotient with the same sign on both sides (or one side at an endpoint). Check both before you use the words.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
A vertical tangent line is not a vertical asymptote. A vertical asymptote (Lesson 2.02) is a line the curve approaches but never touches, because \(f\) blows up there. At a vertical tangent, \(f(a)\) is a perfectly ordinary finite number: the curve passes through the point and the tangent line touches it there. \(y=\dfrac1x\) has a vertical asymptote at \(0\) and no vertical tangent; \(y=\sqrt[3]{x}\) has a vertical tangent at \(0\) and no asymptote.
📚 PART 2 · VERTICAL TANGENTS, CUSPS, AND CORNERS

Now put the two ways of failing side by side. For a function continuous at \(a\), look at the two one-sided limits of the difference quotient (Lesson 1.04 treated one-sided limits; Lesson 4.04 applied them to \(|x|\)). For a function that is continuous at \(a\) and differentiable nearby, the one-sided limits of \(f'(x)\) as \(x\to a\) agree with them whenever those limits exist, so you can read the same information off the formula for the derivative. Anything else, for example a finite slope on one side and an infinite slope on the other, is a corner-type break with no single tangent line. The table is the whole classification for the functions in this course.

Example 4: a cusp, \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\)

The quotient at \(0\) is \(\dfrac{h^{2/3}}{h}=h^{-1/3}\). For \(h>0\) this is positive and grows without bound: \(+\infty\). For \(h<0\), \(h^{2/3}=(h^2)^{1/3}>0\) but \(h<0\), so the quotient is negative and grows without bound in size: \(-\infty\). The slopes are \(-\infty\) on the left and \(+\infty\) on the right, so the graph has a cusp. The formula agrees: \(f'(x)=\dfrac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x}}\), which is negative for \(x<0\) and positive for \(x>0\). Both branches leave the origin heading straight up, one to each side, so the graph comes to a point like a beak. Because the curve reverses direction there, we say \(f\) has a cusp at \(0\), not a vertical tangent, even though the direction there is vertical.

Example 5: a cusp on top, \(f(x)=2-(x-1)^{2/3}\)

Here \(f'(x)=-\dfrac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x-1}}\). For \(x\) just left of \(1\) the cube root is negative, so \(f'\to+\infty\); just right of \(1\) it is positive, so \(f'\to-\infty\). Opposite infinite slopes: a cusp at \((1,2)\), pointing up, and it is the highest point on the graph. Fermat's fact from Lesson 8.03 needed the derivative to exist at an extreme point; here it does not, and that is why a maximum can occur with no horizontal tangent. Module 10 will name such points critical points.

Example 6: the exponent alone does not decide, \(f(x)=x^{4/3}\)

The quotient is \(\dfrac{h^{4/3}}{h}=h^{1/3}\to0\) from both sides. So \(f'(0)=0\), the graph is smooth, and the tangent line is horizontal. For the family \(f(x)=x^{p}\) at \(0\) the quotient is \(h^{p-1}\), and everything depends on the sign of \(p-1\): for \(01\) it goes to \(0\). That is why \(x^{1/3}\) and \(x^{2/3}\) both fail and \(x^{4/3}\) does not.

📚 PART 3 · FINDING VERTICAL TANGENTS FROM A FORMULA

How do you find vertical tangents when a problem does not hand you the point? The same way you found horizontal ones in Lesson 9.01, but looking at the other half of a fraction. Write \(f'(x)\) as a single fraction. A horizontal tangent comes from a numerator of \(0\). A candidate for a vertical tangent (or a cusp) comes from a denominator of \(0\) where the numerator is not \(0\), at a point where \(f\) itself is defined and continuous. Then check the sign of \(f'\) on each side to decide between vertical tangent and cusp. Finally, look at the endpoints of the domain, where a square root often vanishes.

Example 7: \(f(x)=x\sqrt{4-x}\)

The domain is \(x\le4\). By the Product Rule and the Chain Rule, \(f'(x)=\sqrt{4-x}+x\cdot\dfrac{-1}{2\sqrt{4-x}}=\dfrac{2(4-x)-x}{2\sqrt{4-x}}=\dfrac{8-3x}{2\sqrt{4-x}}\).

Horizontal tangent: the numerator is \(0\) at \(x=\tfrac83\), and \(f\left(\tfrac83\right)=\tfrac83\sqrt{\tfrac43}=\dfrac{16\sqrt3}{9}\approx3.08\). The horizontal tangent line is \(y=\dfrac{16\sqrt3}{9}\). Vertical tangent: the denominator is \(0\) at \(x=4\), where the numerator is \(8-12=-4\ne0\). Here \(f(4)=0\), \(f\) is continuous from the left, and as \(x\to4^-\) we have \(f'(x)\to-\infty\). This is the right-hand endpoint of the domain, so the one-sided limit is enough: the graph has a vertical tangent line \(x=4\) at \((4,0)\).

▶  Try it first: classify the point on \(f(x)=(x+3)^{2/3}-1\) where the derivative fails to exist. Then reveal the check.
\(f'(x)=\dfrac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x+3}}\) is undefined at \(x=-3\), where \(f(-3)=-1\). For \(x\) just left of \(-3\), the cube root is negative so \(f'\to-\infty\); just right of \(-3\), \(f'\to+\infty\). Opposite infinite slopes: a cusp at \((-3,-1)\), pointing down (it is the lowest point on the graph). It is the graph of \(x^{2/3}\) shifted left \(3\) and down \(1\).
📚 PART 4 · VERTICAL TANGENTS ON IMPLICIT CURVES

Implicit curves (Lesson 7.01) are where vertical tangents appear most naturally, because a circle has vertical tangents at its leftmost and rightmost points, and no function \(y=f(x)\) can describe a circle there. The recipe is the mirror image of Lesson 9.01: solve for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) as a fraction and look at where the denominator is \(0\) and the numerator is not.

Example 8: a circle

On \(x^2+y^2=25\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac xy\). The denominator is \(0\) when \(y=0\), which gives the points \((5,0)\) and \((-5,0)\), where the numerator \(-x=\mp5\) is not \(0\). The tangent lines there are the vertical lines \(x=5\) and \(x=-5\), exactly as the picture of a circle suggests.

Example 9: the tilted ellipse from Lesson 9.01

On \(x^2+xy+y^2=7\), you found \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{2x+y}{x+2y}\). A vertical tangent needs \(x+2y=0\), that is, \(x=-2y\), with \(2x+y\ne0\). Substituting into the curve: \(4y^2-2y^2+y^2=3y^2=7\), so \(y=\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{21}}3\) and \(x=\mp\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3\). The two points are \(\left(-\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3,\dfrac{\sqrt{21}}3\right)\approx(-3.06,1.53)\) and \(\left(\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3,-\dfrac{\sqrt{21}}3\right)\approx(3.06,-1.53)\). At each, the numerator is \(2x+y=\pm\sqrt{21}\ne0\), so the slope is truly infinite. The vertical tangent lines are \(x=-\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3\) and \(x=\dfrac{2\sqrt{21}}3\). In Lesson 9.01 the numerator zero gave the horizontal tangents at \(x=\pm\sqrt{21}/3\); today the denominator zero gives the vertical ones. One curve, two questions, and now you can answer both.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
When both numerator and denominator are \(0\) at a point, the formula is silent: it says \(\tfrac00\) and decides nothing. For the curve \(y^2=x^3\), implicit differentiation gives \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{3x^2}{2y}\), which is \(\tfrac00\) at the origin, and the origin turns out to be a cusp of a different kind (the two branches \(y=\pm x^{3/2}\) both run off to the right along the \(x\)-axis). When the formula gives \(\tfrac00\), say so, and use the difference-quotient definition or the explicit branches to decide. Do not guess.
▶  Try it first: find every vertical tangent line to the circle \((x-1)^2+y^2=9\). Then reveal the check.
Differentiate: \(2(x-1)+2y\,y'=0\), so \(y'=-\dfrac{x-1}{y}\). The denominator is \(0\) when \(y=0\), which gives \((x-1)^2=9\), so \(x=4\) or \(x=-2\). The numerator \(-(x-1)\) is \(-3\) and \(3\) there, not \(0\). The vertical tangent lines are \(x=4\) and \(x=-2\), the rightmost and leftmost points of the circle with center \((1,0)\) and radius \(3\).

Convince a classmate: in your own words, why does \(f'(0)\) “does not exist” cover four quite different pictures (corner, vertical tangent, cusp, and a jump)? What extra information tells them apart?

🤖 AI AWARENESS
Graphing tools can mislead on exactly these functions. Some calculators evaluate \(x^{1/3}\) for negative \(x\) as a complex number, or not at all, so the graph of \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) shows only its right half, and \(x^{2/3}\) looks like half a curve instead of a cusp. If you graph these, use the real cube root (often written cbrt or surd) or check the left side by hand. A tool can show you a picture; only the one-sided limits can tell you what the picture means.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A vertical tangent line at \(x=a\) needs continuity at \(a\) and a difference quotient that goes to the same infinity on both sides. A cusp has opposite infinite slopes, a corner has finite slopes that disagree, and a smooth point has equal finite slopes. “The derivative does not exist” is true of the first three, and it is never the whole story. Next week the tangent line stops being a curiosity and becomes a tool. Lesson 9.03 uses the tangent line at a point where the derivative does exist as a stand-in for the curve itself, to estimate values you could not easily compute.
