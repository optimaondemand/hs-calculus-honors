📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Given three graphs and no formulas, how do you decide which one is the function, which is its slope, and which is the slope of the slope?
Scientists and engineers rarely get a tidy formula. They get a plot: position over time, a temperature record, a cost curve. To understand it they ask the questions of this module out loud: where is it rising, where is it bending, where does the bending reverse? Today you practice the language that connects a graph to its derivative graphs. By the end you should be able to hold \(f\), \(f'\), and \(f''\) in your mind as one three-part picture and move freely between them.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify which of three given graphs is \(f\), \(f'\), and \(f''\), and justify each choice with specific features.
2
I will be able to translate a verbal description of a function (its extrema, concavity, and inflection) into a sketch, and a graph of \(f'\) into statements about \(f\).
3
I will be able to explain how a cusp, a corner, or a jump in a derivative graph changes what the translation rules say about \(f\).

📚 PART 1 · THE DICTIONARY BETWEEN THE THREE GRAPHS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A graph is labeled \(f'\). It crosses the \(x\)-axis at \(x=3\), going from above the axis to below it. What does that tell you about the graph of \(f\) at \(x=3\)?

In Lesson 11.03 you started with a formula and built a graph. Today the starting point is a graph, and the job is to figure out how three graphs relate: \(f\), its first derivative \(f'\), and its second derivative \(f''\). This is how a scientist reads a plot of position, velocity, and acceleration, or how an economist reads a cost curve, its marginal cost, and the rate marginal cost is changing. The data seldom arrive as a neat formula; the reading has to come from the picture.

Everything today rests on one idea: each graph is a record of the slopes of the one before it. The height of \(f'\) is the slope of \(f\). The height of \(f''\) is the slope of \(f'\). So a feature of one graph, read the right way, is a feature of the next.

If \(f\) is…then the graph of \(f'\)…and the graph of \(f''\)…
increasingis above the \(x\)-axis (\(f'>0\))—
decreasingis below the \(x\)-axis (\(f'<0\))—
at a local maximum or minimumcrosses the \(x\)-axis there (a zero of \(f'\) with a sign change)is negative at a maximum, positive at a minimum (the Second Derivative Test)
concave upis rising (\(f'\) is increasing)is above the \(x\)-axis (\(f''>0\))
concave downis falling (\(f'\) is decreasing)is below the \(x\)-axis (\(f''<0\))
at an inflection pointhas a local maximum or minimum there (the slope stops growing and starts shrinking, or the reverse)crosses the \(x\)-axis (or fails to exist) with a sign change
Two Translation Rules
A zero of \(f'\) (with a sign change) is a peak or valley of \(f\). A peak or valley of \(f'\) is a zero of \(f''\) and an inflection point of \(f\). The same move, one derivative down.

For polynomials there is a handy sanity check. Each derivative lowers the degree by one, so if \(f\) is a cubic then \(f'\) is a parabola and \(f''\) is a line; if \(f\) is a quartic then \(f'\) is a cubic and \(f''\) is a parabola. Counting turning points works the same way: a parabola has one turning point, so a cubic \(f\) has at most two humps; a line has none, so a parabola \(f'\) has no inflection points.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool will happily plot \(f\) and \(f'\) from a formula, which makes it tempting to match graphs by letting the tool do it. Use it differently: sketch your predicted \(f'\) on paper from a given \(f\), then have the tool draw the true one and compare. Every place your sketch differs is a place where a rule above is not yet automatic. The matching skill transfers to graphs from experiments, where there is no formula to type in.
📚 PART 3 · CUSPS, CORNERS, AND JUMPS

Everything so far assumed \(f\) was smooth. Real graphs have places where the derivative fails to exist, and those places can fool the translation rules. Here are two that deserve a careful look.

A cusp: \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\). The graph is defined and continuous at \(0\), and it has a sharp point there. For \(x\ne0\), \(f'(x)=\dfrac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}\), which becomes unboundedly positive as \(x\to0^+\) and unboundedly negative as \(x\to0^-\). So \(f'\) has a vertical asymptote at \(0\) even though \(f\) does not. The second derivative is \(f''(x)=-\dfrac{2}{9}x^{-4/3}\), which is negative for every \(x\ne0\): the graph is concave down on both sides of the point, so the cusp is not an inflection point.

A cusp in f shows up as a vertical asymptote in f'.

A smooth point with a jumpy second derivative: \(f(x)=x|x|\). This is \(-x^2\) for \(x<0\) and \(x^2\) for \(x\ge0\). Then \(f'(x)=2|x|\), which has a corner at \(0\) but exists everywhere (including \(f'(0)=0\)), and \(f''(x)=2\) for \(x>0\) and \(-2\) for \(x<0\). At \(x=0\), \(f''\) does not exist. Yet concavity changes there, from down to up, and the graph has a tangent line at the origin, so \((0,0)\) is an inflection point even though \(f''(0)\) is undefined.

The inflection of x|x| at the origin: f' has a minimum there, f'' jumps from -2 to 2.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not read a sharp point as an inflection point, or an inflection point as a point where \(f''=0\). A cusp can sit inside a stretch of constant concavity (\(x^{2/3}\)), and an inflection can occur where \(f''\) is undefined (\(x|x|\)). The only reliable test is the one from Lesson 11.02: does the concavity change, and does the graph have a tangent line there? Also, a sharp point of \(f\) means \(f'\) has a break or an asymptote at that \(x\), not a zero.
⚡ TRY IT · READ THE EXCEPTIONS
Answer about the two examples in this part.
1. For \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\), what is true of \(f'\) near \(x=0\)?
2. Is the origin an inflection point of \(f(x)=x|x|\)?
📚 PART 4 · FROM WORDS TO A SKETCH

The last direction of translation is from words to a picture. A description like this one is exactly what a data analyst or an engineer hands you.

▶  Try it first: a continuous function \(f\) has \(f(0)=0\), a local minimum at \(x=-2\), a local maximum at \(x=2\), is concave up for \(x<0\), and concave down for \(x>0\). Sketch one.
Translate each phrase into a derivative fact, then draw. Local minimum at \(-2\) and local maximum at \(2\): \(f'(-2)=0\) with \(f'\) going from \(-\) to \(+\), and \(f'(2)=0\) with \(f'\) going from \(+\) to \(-\). So \(f'\) is positive between \(-2\) and \(2\), and negative outside. Concave up then down, changing at 0: \(f''\) changes from \(+\) to \(-\) at \(0\), so \(f\) has an inflection point at the origin, and \(f'\) has a maximum there.

The picture: starting at the far left, the graph is falling and concave up, bottoms out at \(x=-2\), rises (bending upward, then straightening out) through the origin at its steepest, bends over and flattens at the peak at \(x=2\), then falls. One function that fits is \(f(x)=4x-\dfrac{x^3}{3}\): then \(f'(x)=4-x^2\) and \(f''(x)=-2x\), which vanish exactly where required, with \(f(-2)=-\dfrac{16}{3}\) and \(f(2)=\dfrac{16}{3}\). Many other graphs fit the description too; any sketch that respects every stated fact is a correct answer.
▶  Try it first: the graph of \(f'\) is positive for \(x<1\), crosses zero at \(x=1\), and is negative for \(x>1\). It is decreasing everywhere. What can you say about \(f\)?
\(f'>0\) for \(x<1\) means \(f\) is increasing there; \(f'<0\) for \(x>1\) means \(f\) is decreasing; so \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x=1\). \(f'\) decreasing everywhere means \(f''<0\) everywhere, so \(f\) is concave down on its whole domain, with no inflection points. The graph is a single arch. The function \(f(x)=-(x-1)^2\) is an example: \(f'(x)=-2(x-1)\), which is positive for \(x<1\), zero at \(1\), negative after, and \(f''=-2<0\).

Convince a classmate: Explain to a classmate how to tell, from the graph of \(f'\) alone, where \(f\) is concave up. (Hint: it is not about where \(f'\) is positive.)
