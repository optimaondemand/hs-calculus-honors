📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A speedometer reads your speed at an instant. A road trip only ever gives you an average.
Every limit you have studied so far was preparing you for this question: how fast is something changing right now, at one exact moment, rather than over some stretch of time? Averaging over a stretch is easy: divide the change in output by the change in input. Pinning down a single instant is harder, because a single instant gives you nothing to divide by. Today you will watch the average rate of change over a shrinking interval settle on one exact number, the instantaneous rate of change, by letting that interval collapse toward zero.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to compute the average rate of change of a function over an interval as a difference quotient, and identify it as the slope of a secant line.
2
I will be able to explain instantaneous rate of change as the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks toward zero.
3
I will be able to estimate an instantaneous rate of change numerically from a table of shrinking difference quotients.

📚 PART 1 · TWO KINDS OF RATE

Average rate of change is a secant slope

For a function \(f\), the average rate of change over the interval from \(x\) to \(x+h\) is the change in output divided by the change in input:

\[ \text{average rate of change} = \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]

This expression is called a difference quotient. Graphically, it is the slope of the secant line through the two points \((x, f(x))\) and \((x+h, f(x+h))\), since slope is exactly change in output over change in input. If \(s(t)\) is the position of a moving object at time \(t\), the same expression \(\dfrac{s(t+h)-s(t)}{h}\) is its average velocity on the interval from \(t\) to \(t+h\).

The gold secant line connects two points on the curve. As \(h\) shrinks, the second point slides toward the first and the secant swings toward the tangent.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Galileo timed balls rolling down inclines and noticed that a falling object's average speed over a stretch of time was easy to measure, but its speed at one exact instant seemed to require dividing zero by zero. Both Newton and Leibniz, working independently in the late 1600s, built methods for handling exactly this kind of instantaneous rate, which is part of why calculus is sometimes described as two people solving the same problem from two directions at once.

📚 PART 2 · SHRINKING h TOWARD ZERO

Worked example: the difference quotient for \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x = 2\)

Let \(f(x) = x^2\). We build the average rate of change on \([2, 2+h]\) and simplify it fully before we let \(h\) shrink.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(2+h) - f(2)}{h} &= \frac{(2+h)^2 - 2^2}{h} \\ &= \frac{4 + 4h + h^2 - 4}{h} \\ &= \frac{4h + h^2}{h} \\ &= 4 + h \qquad (h \ne 0) \end{aligned} \]

So the average rate of change on \([2, 2+h]\) is simply \(4 + h\). Now watch what happens as \(h\) shrinks toward 0 from both directions:

Both sides press toward the same number: 4. This is the instantaneous rate of change of \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x = 2\), and it is also the slope of the tangent line to the curve at that point. You will give this number its own name and notation in the next lesson.

📚 PART 3 · INSTANTANEOUS VELOCITY

The same shrinking process answers a real physical question. Suppose an object dropped from a height falls \(s(t) = 16t^2\) feet after \(t\) seconds. What is its instantaneous velocity exactly 1 second after release?

▶  Try it in your notebook first: build and simplify the average-velocity difference quotient \( \dfrac{s(1+h) - s(1)}{h} \) for \(s(t) = 16t^2\). Then reveal the check.
\(s(1) = 16\). \(s(1+h) = 16(1+h)^2 = 16(1 + 2h + h^2) = 16 + 32h + 16h^2\). So \(s(1+h) - s(1) = 32h + 16h^2\), and the difference quotient simplifies to \(\dfrac{32h + 16h^2}{h} = 32 + 16h\). As \(h \to 0\), this approaches 32. The object's instantaneous velocity 1 second after release is 32 feet per second.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not plug \(h = 0\) directly into the unsimplified difference quotient; that gives \(\frac{0}{0}\), which is not an answer. Simplify the algebra first, the way you did with 0/0 limits in Module 1, and only then let \(h\) shrink to 0 in the simplified expression.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Average rate of change is the slope of a secant line over an interval of length \(h\). Instantaneous rate of change is what that slope approaches as \(h\) shrinks to 0. It is not an approximation once you take the limit; it is the exact value. In the next lesson you will name this limiting value \(f'(a)\) and use it to write the equation of a tangent line.
