📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two limits sat quietly in Module 2, waiting for a use. Today the derivative finally calls on them.
Lesson 2.03 proved, by squeezing a sliver of a circle between two triangles, that \( \displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{\sin h}{h}=1 \). At the time that limit had no obvious job; it was simply true. It has a job now. Every derivative rule in Module 5 was built the same way: write the difference quotient, take the limit, and see what falls out. Sine and cosine are no different, except that this time an angle-sum identity and that one special limit from Module 2 do all the work. By the end of today you will have two new rules, proved honestly from the definition, and a striking pattern connecting them.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to prove that \(\frac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x\) directly from the limit definition of the derivative.
2
I will be able to prove that \(\frac{d}{dx}[\cos x] = -\sin x\) by the same method, and state the four-step cycle the two derivatives fall into.
3
I will be able to differentiate combinations of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) using these rules together with the Sum and Constant Multiple Rules from Module 5.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, and your notes from Lesson 2.03 on the special limit \( \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1 \).
▶
The angle-sum identities \(\sin(x+h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h\) and \(\cos(x+h) = \cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h\) from your precalculus work, and the Sum and Constant Multiple Rules from Lessons 5.01–5.02.
📚 PART 1 · A SECOND SPECIAL LIMIT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before any algebra: as \(h\) gets very close to 0, what do you expect \(\dfrac{\cos h - 1}{h}\) approaches? Guess a number. (No wrong answer, just a prediction.)

Proving the derivative of \(\sin x\) will need a second limit alongside \( \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1 \), namely \( \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} \). Rather than squeeze this one geometrically, build it from the limit you already have. Multiply by a clever form of 1:

\[ \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = \frac{\cos h - 1}{h}\cdot\frac{\cos h + 1}{\cos h + 1} = \frac{\cos^2 h - 1}{h(\cos h + 1)} = \frac{-\sin^2 h}{h(\cos h + 1)} \]

using \(\cos^2 h - 1 = -\sin^2 h\) from the Pythagorean identity. Split the fraction to expose the special limit you already trust:

\[ \frac{-\sin^2 h}{h(\cos h + 1)} = -\frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{\sin h}{\cos h + 1} \]

As \(h \to 0\), the first factor \(\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1\) by Lesson 2.03, and the second factor \(\frac{\sin h}{\cos h + 1} \to \frac{0}{2} = 0\), since \(\sin h \to 0\) and \(\cos h + 1 \to 2\). A limit that goes to 1 times a limit that goes to 0 goes to 0, so

\[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = 0 \]

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Both special limits say something geometric in disguise: near \(h=0\), the graph of \(\sin h\) is almost indistinguishable from the line \(y=h\), while the graph of \(\cos h\) is almost indistinguishable from the constant \(y=1\). The two limits are the algebraic fingerprint of the unit circle looking, up close, like straight lines.
📚 PART 2 · PROVING THE DERIVATIVE OF SINE

Let \(f(x) = \sin x\). By the limit definition of the derivative from Lesson 4.02,

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin(x+h) - \sin x}{h} \]

Expand \(\sin(x+h)\) with the angle-sum identity, then group terms:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h - \sin x}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin x(\cos h - 1) + \cos x\sin h}{h} \]

Split into two fractions and pull the constants \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) (constant with respect to \(h\)) out of each limit:

\[ f'(x) = \sin x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} \;+\; \cos x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h} \]

Both limits are now known: the first is 0 from Part 1, and the second is 1 from Lesson 2.03. So

\[ f'(x) = \sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1 = \cos x \]

This proves, from nothing but the definition of the derivative and two limits already in hand, that

\[ \boxed{\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x} \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
This proof, and every trig derivative built on it, is only valid when \(x\) is measured in radians. The special limits from Lesson 2.03 fail in degree measure, so this rule and the ones in Lesson 6.02 all assume radians unless a problem says otherwise.
📚 PART 3 · THE DERIVATIVE OF COSINE, AND THE FOUR-STEP CYCLE

The derivative of \(\cos x\) follows the identical strategy. Let \(g(x) = \cos x\):

\[ g'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos(x+h) - \cos x}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h - \cos x}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos x(\cos h - 1) - \sin x\sin h}{h} \]

\[ g'(x) = \cos x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} \;-\; \sin x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = \cos x\cdot 0 - \sin x\cdot 1 \]

\[ \boxed{\dfrac{d}{dx}[\cos x] = -\sin x} \]

Differentiate either function repeatedly and the results cycle with period four, always returning to where they started:

\[ \sin x \;\xrightarrow{\;\frac{d}{dx}\;}\; \cos x \;\xrightarrow{\;\frac{d}{dx}\;}\; -\sin x \;\xrightarrow{\;\frac{d}{dx}\;}\; -\cos x \;\xrightarrow{\;\frac{d}{dx}\;}\; \sin x \]

🧩 WORTH KNOWING
This four-step cycle is not a curiosity; it is the reason sine and cosine describe oscillation throughout the sciences. A mass on a spring, a plucked string, and alternating current all satisfy an equation that says "the acceleration is proportional to the negative of the position" — and \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) are exactly the functions built so that differentiating twice returns \(-1\) times the original function. You will meet this equation, and this cycle again, when Module 12 turns to motion.

Worked example: combining the rules

Differentiate \(f(x) = 4\sin x - 3\cos x + 5\), then evaluate \(f'(0)\). Apply the Sum Rule and Constant Multiple Rule from Module 5 term by term:

\[ f'(x) = 4\cos x - 3(-\sin x) + 0 = 4\cos x + 3\sin x \]

\[ f'(0) = 4\cos 0 + 3\sin 0 = 4(1) + 3(0) = 4 \]

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\cos x] = -\sin x\), and both rules trace back to nothing more exotic than an angle-sum identity and the two special limits Module 2 proved weeks before either had a use. Sine and cosine are the foundation; every other trigonometric derivative in Lesson 6.02 is built by writing that function as a ratio of these two and calling on the Quotient Rule.
