📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two cars can end a trip in the same place after one drove 20 miles and the other drove 28. Calculus has to be able to tell them apart.
Lesson 8.02 gave you the vocabulary of motion: position, velocity, and acceleration. Today you turn it into a method. A sign chart for velocity tells you which way a particle is moving and exactly when it turns around. Comparing the signs of velocity and acceleration tells you whether it is speeding up or slowing down. Splitting a trip at its turnarounds tells you the distance actually traveled, which can be very different from the displacement. You will finish with the most famous motion in the history of science, a body in free fall, and with a habit that matters in every applied problem: stating the assumptions of your model and checking your answer against the story.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use a sign chart of \(v\) and the signs of \(v\) and \(a\) to describe when a particle moves forward or backward and when it speeds up or slows down.
2
I will be able to find displacement and total distance for a particle that changes direction, by splitting the interval at every time \(v=0\).
3
I will be able to solve a free-fall problem from \(s(t)=-4.9t^2+v_0t+s_0\): the peak, the landing time, the impact velocity, and the distance traveled, and state the model's assumptions.

📚 PART 1 · DIRECTION, SPEED, AND THE SIGN CHART
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A particle moves along a line. At one moment its velocity is \(-3\) ft/s. Which statement do you expect is true at that moment?

Lesson 8.02 introduced position \(s(t)\), velocity \(v(t)=s'(t)\), and acceleration \(a(t)=v'(t)=s''(t)\) for a particle on a line, and you found that a particle can turn around and end up far from where it has traveled. Today you turn that into a method precise enough to answer any question about a motion. Three questions cover almost everything: Which way is it moving? Is it speeding up or slowing down? How far has it really gone?

The vocabulary of motion on a line
Velocity \(v(t)=s'(t)\) carries a sign: positive means moving in the positive direction, negative the other way, zero means momentarily at rest.
Speed is \(|v(t)|\), the size of the velocity, never negative.
Acceleration \(a(t)=v'(t)\) is the rate of change of velocity.
Speeding up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign; slowing down when they have opposite signs.

Why the sign rule is true. Speed is \(|v|\). If \(v>0\), then \(|v|=v\), and the speed changes at the rate \(v'=a\): speeding up exactly when \(a>0\). If \(v<0\), then \(|v|=-v\), and the speed changes at the rate \(-v'=-a\): speeding up exactly when \(a<0\). In both cases speeding up means \(a\) and \(v\) agree in sign. At the instant \(v=0\) the particle is at rest, so it is neither.

Example 1. A particle moves along a line with position \(s(t)=t^3-9t^2+24t\) feet, \(t\) in seconds, for \(0\le t\le5\). Find velocity and acceleration and factor them:

\[ v(t)=3t^2-18t+24=3(t-2)(t-4),\qquad a(t)=6t-18=6(t-3). \]

The particle is at rest at \(t=2\) and \(t=4\), the only times \(v=0\). Test a value in each interval to build the sign chart for \(v\), and mark where \(a\) changes sign, at \(t=3\):

IntervalSign of \(v\)Sign of \(a\)Motion
\(0\(+\) (\(v(1)=9\))\(-\)forward, slowing down
\(2\(-\) (\(v(2.5)=-\tfrac94\))\(-\)backward, speeding up
\(3\(-\) (\(v(3.5)=-\tfrac94\))\(+\)backward, slowing down
\(4\(+\) (\(v(5)=9\))\(+\)forward, speeding up

Notice the particle's fastest backward moment: at \(t=3\) the acceleration is zero and the speed is \(|v(3)|=|3(1)(-1)|=3\) ft/s, the largest speed during the backward stretch. The position graph below shows the same story as the table: the curve climbs until the first turnaround, falls between the two, and climbs again.

Convince a classmate: the particle has a negative velocity at \(t=2.5\) and a negative acceleration there too. Is it slowing down? Use the sign rule, and say it in your own words, not only in symbols.

📚 PART 2 · TOTAL DISTANCE VERSUS DISPLACEMENT

Now the question the sign chart was built to answer: how far did the particle actually travel? Displacement and distance are different quantities, and a motion that turns around makes the difference visible.

Displacement and total distance
Displacement from \(t=a\) to \(t=b\) is \(s(b)-s(a)\), a signed number that only compares the end with the start.
Total distance is the sum of the lengths of the legs between turnarounds: split \([a,b]\) at every time \(v=0\) (inside the interval), and add the absolute values \(|s(\text{end of leg})-s(\text{start of leg})|\).

Example 1, continued. Positions at the key times: \(s(0)=0\), \(s(2)=8-36+48=20\), \(s(4)=64-144+96=16\), \(s(5)=125-225+120=20\). The turnarounds are \(t=2\) and \(t=4\), so there are three legs on \([0,5]\):

\[ \text{displacement}=s(5)-s(0)=20-0=20\ \text{ft}, \]

\[ \text{total distance}=|20-0|+|16-20|+|20-16|=20+4+4=28\ \text{ft}. \]

The particle ends 20 feet from where it started, yet it covered 28 feet of ground. The gap of 8 feet is the 4 feet it backed up and then had to cover again. If you had computed \(|s(5)-s(0)|=20\) and called it the distance, you would have been 8 feet short, which is exactly the error a sign chart prevents.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not find total distance by plugging only the two endpoints into \(|s(b)-s(a)|\), and do not integrate or add up \(v\) without checking its sign. Both methods treat the backward stretch as if it undid the forward stretch. The honest procedure is always the same: find every time \(v=0\), cut the interval there, and add the legs.

📚 PART 3 · FREE FALL: A MODEL WITH HONEST LIMITS

The oldest, most famous motion problem in the history of calculus is a falling body. Galileo measured it and found that an object released from rest falls distances in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 over successive equal time intervals; Newton explained why. Near Earth's surface, with position \(s(t)\) measured upward in meters, the model is

\[ s(t)=-4.9t^2+v_0t+s_0,\qquad a(t)=-9.8\ \text{m/s}^2, \]

where \(v_0\) is the initial velocity and \(s_0\) the initial height. The acceleration is the same \(-9.8\) the whole time, going up and coming down. That one constant already explains everything about the flight.

Example 2: a stone thrown from a bridge. A stone is thrown straight up from a bridge at \(14.7\) m/s, from a height of \(19.6\) m above the river:

\[ s(t)=-4.9t^2+14.7t+19.6,\qquad v(t)=-9.8t+14.7. \]

The peak. The stone is at rest at the top: \(v(t)=0\) gives \(t=1.5\) s, and \(s(1.5)=-11.025+22.05+19.6=30.625\) m. The stone rises \(30.625-19.6=11.025\) m above the bridge.

The splash. It hits the river when \(s(t)=0\): \(-4.9t^2+14.7t+19.6=0\). Divide by \(-4.9\): \(t^2-3t-4=0\), so \((t-4)(t+1)=0\). The root \(t=-1\) is before the throw, so the stone lands at \(t=4\) s, with velocity \(v(4)=-39.2+14.7=-24.5\) m/s. Its impact speed is \(24.5\) m/s, more than the \(14.7\) m/s with which it left your hand, because it fell the extra \(19.6\) m below the release point.

Displacement and distance. Displacement \(=s(4)-s(0)=0-19.6=-19.6\) m (it ends 19.6 m below where it started). Total distance: up \(11.025\) m, then down \(30.625\) m, a total of

\[ 11.025+30.625=41.65\ \text{m}. \]

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Galileo's insight, in about 1604, was to ask what changes at a steady rate. Not the position, not the velocity, but the velocity: constant acceleration. In calculus language, \(s''(t)=-9.8\) is a constant, which forces \(s\) to be a quadratic. You proved a piece of this idea in Lesson 8.04: a function is determined by its derivative up to a constant. Module 15 will turn that into a tool that runs the model backward.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
A model is honest about its limits. The formula \(s(t)=-4.9t^2+14.7t+19.6\) ignores air resistance, so it is accurate for a dense stone over a short fall and wrong for a falling feather. When you answer a motion problem, state the assumption (here, constant acceleration \(-9.8\) m/s\(^2\)) and check that your answer makes sense in the context: a negative time means the question was asked about a moment before the story began.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Velocity's sign is direction and its absolute value is speed. A particle speeds up when velocity and acceleration agree in sign and slows down when they disagree. Displacement compares the end with the start; total distance adds the legs between turnarounds, and you find the turnarounds by solving \(v=0\). Free fall is the same machinery with one constant acceleration, \(-9.8\) m/s\(^2\), plus the honesty to say what the model leaves out. Next, Lesson 12.04 on related rates asks what happens when several changing quantities are tied together by a single equation: differentiate the equation with respect to time, and the rates become tied together too.
