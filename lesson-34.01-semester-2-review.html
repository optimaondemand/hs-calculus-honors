Semester 2 Review: From Applications of the Derivative to the Integral
Review Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Slope and area look like two different subjects. Sixteen weeks of work say they are one subject read in two directions. Can you say, in one sentence, how?
Archimedes found areas and volumes by a method of exhaustion two thousand years before anyone had a derivative, and tangent lines were a separate craft altogether. Newton and Leibniz saw that the two crafts were inverse to each other, and that single insight is why calculus is one subject and not two. This semester you have travelled the same road: you put the derivative to work on tangent lines, extrema, curve shape, optimization, motion, and related rates, then built the integral from rectangles, and then watched the Fundamental Theorem tie it to everything before it. Today's review does not re-teach the semester. It shows you the joints between the parts, trains you to pick the right tool for a problem that carries no label, and asks you to be honest about what each theorem actually promises.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to trace how each idea of Semester 2 depends on the one before it, from tangent lines to volumes.
2
I will be able to choose the right tool for an unlabeled derivative-side or integral-side problem, and say why it fits.
3
I will be able to check a theorem's hypotheses, a model's units, and an optimum's justification before claiming a conclusion.

📚 PART 1 · THE MAP OF THE SEMESTER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read the map below: if you had to say what the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus does for you, which description feels closest? There is no wrong answer; commit to one and see how it holds up.

Here is the semester as one chain. Read the second line of each entry as the answer to a single question: what did this module hand to the next one?

It showed that near a point, a differentiable function is nearly a line.
MODULE 10

MODULE 12

MODULE 16

Joint 1: slope and area are inverses

Lesson 14.01 asked you to accumulate area from a fixed left end, and the answer was startling. For \(f(t)=t^2\), the accumulated area is \(F(x)=\int_0^x t^2\,dt=\tfrac{x^3}{3}\), and differentiating it returns the rate you started with:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_0^x t^2\,dt=\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{x^3}{3}=x^2 \]

That is Part 1 of the theorem. Part 2 runs it the other way: to find \(\int_a^b f\), find any antiderivative \(F\) and compute \(F(b)-F(a)\). Every derivative you learned in Semesters 1 and 2 is now also an integration fact, read backward.

Joint 2: reading graphs of \(f\), \(f'\), and \(f''\), then reading them in reverse

In Lesson 11.04 you matched a function to its derivative graphs: where \(f'\) is positive, \(f\) rises; where \(f''\) is positive, \(f\) bends up. Lesson 14.03 ran the same skill in reverse. If \(F(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\), then \(F'=f\) and \(F''=f'\), so the graph of \(f\) is the slope graph of \(F\): \(F\) rises where \(f\) is positive, has a maximum where \(f\) crosses from positive to negative, and bends up where \(f\) is increasing. Nothing new had to be learned; the old graph-reading ran in the other direction.

Joint 3: the Mean Value Theorem returns

The Mean Value Theorem of Lesson 8.03 says that somewhere inside an interval, the instantaneous rate equals the average rate. Lesson 16.02 gave the integral version: a continuous function on \([a,b]\) takes its average value \(\tfrac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f\) at some point of the interval. For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([0,3]\), the average value is \(\tfrac13\int_0^3x^2\,dx=\tfrac13\cdot9=3\), and \(f(c)=3\) at \(c=\sqrt3\approx1.73\), which lies inside \((0,3)\). The same theorem also underlies the proof of Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem, so the Semester 1 guarantee is quietly holding up Semester 2.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Substitution is the Chain Rule run backward, and only works when the inner derivative is actually present. \(\int2x\cos(x^2)\,dx=\sin(x^2)+C\) works because \(2x\) is the derivative of \(x^2\). If a different factor stood there, you would not get to “fix” it by guessing; check by differentiating your answer.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Velocity is a thread that runs the whole way through. In Lesson 8.02, \(s'\) was velocity. In Lesson 12.03 you split a trip by the sign of \(v\). In Lesson 15.01 you recovered position from velocity. For \(s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t\), \(v=3(t-1)(t-3)\), and \(\int_0^4v\,dt=s(4)-s(0)=4\) meters of displacement, yet the particle actually travels \(4+4+4=12\) meters because it turns around at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\). Displacement is the integral of \(v\); total distance is the integral of \(|v|\).
🤖 AI-AWARENESS
A computer algebra system will return \(\int_0^4v\,dt=4\) instantly. It will not tell you that the particle covered 12 meters, or that the two numbers answer different questions. Use a tool to check your answer, never to produce your reasoning. On the final, the reasoning is what is being assessed.
📚 PART 2 · WHICH TOOL DOES THIS CALL FOR?

A problem on the final will not say “disk method” at the top. It will hand you a situation, and recognizing the tool is part of the work. For each item below, decide which tool to reach for first; the feedback names the signal that should have told you.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH TOOL DOES THIS CALL FOR?
Choose the first tool for each problem. Do not solve it yet; just decide.
1. A farmer has \(40\) m of fence for a rectangular pen. Which dimensions give the largest area?
2. A \(13\)-ft ladder slides down a wall as its base moves away at \(2\) ft/s. How fast is the top falling when the base is \(5\) ft from the wall?
3. Find \(y\) if \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=2y\) and \(y(0)=3\).
4. The region under \(y=\sqrt x\) on \([0,4]\) is rotated about the \(x\)-axis. Find the volume.
5. Estimate \(\sqrt{26}\) without a calculator.
6. Water flows into a tank at \(r(t)=6-t\) gallons per minute for \(0\le t\le4\). How much water enters?
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Ask three questions in order. (1) Am I given a function and asked how it changes or where it is largest or smallest? That is the derivative side: tangent lines, extrema, concavity, optimization, rates. (2) Am I given a rate or a shape and asked for a total, an area, an average, or a volume? That is the integral side. (3) Am I given an equation about rates and asked for the function? That is a differential equation. Asking these in order before you pick up the pencil is a habit, not a trick.
📚 PART 3 · MIXED FLUENCY

Now do the work. Each block below has a question; try it in your notebook first, then open the check. Compare your steps, not just your answer.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: differentiate \(G(x)=\displaystyle\int_0^{x^2}\cos t\,dt\).
Part 1 of the theorem plus the Chain Rule: the upper limit is \(u=x^2\), so \(G'(x)=\cos(x^2)\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2)=2x\cos(x^2)\). (Check: \(G(x)=\sin(x^2)\), and its derivative is the same.)
▶  Try it in your notebook first: evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx\).
Let \(u=x^2\), so \(du=2x\,dx\). The limits change: \(x=0\to u=0\) and \(x=1\to u=1\). Then \(\int_0^1e^u\,du=e^1-e^0=e-1\).
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find the area between \(y=2x\) and \(y=x^2\).
Intersections: \(x^2=2x\) gives \(x=0\) and \(x=2\). On \((0,2)\) the line is above the parabola, so \(A=\int_0^2(2x-x^2)\,dx=\left[x^2-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=4-\tfrac83=\tfrac43\).
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find the absolute maximum and minimum of \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2\) on \([-1,3]\).
\(f\) is continuous on a closed interval, so both exist. \(f'(x)=3x^2-6x=3x(x-2)\) gives critical points \(0\) and \(2\). Compare values: \(f(-1)=-4\), \(f(0)=0\), \(f(2)=-4\), \(f(3)=0\). The absolute maximum is \(0\) (at \(x=0\) and \(x=3\)) and the absolute minimum is \(-4\) (at \(x=-1\) and \(x=2\)).
▶  Try it in your notebook first: solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=xy\) with \(y(0)=2\).
Separate: \(\dfrac{dy}{y}=x\,dx\). Integrate: \(\ln|y|=\tfrac{x^2}{2}+C\), so \(y=Ae^{x^2/2}\). The condition \(y(0)=2\) gives \(A=2\): \(y=2e^{x^2/2}\).

Notice what the optimization problem did: it compared every candidate, endpoints included, instead of stopping at the first critical point. That habit returns in Part 4.

📚 PART 4 · THE HONEST-REASONING CHECK

Honesty in mathematics has a precise meaning: you may claim only what a theorem's hypotheses, a model's units, and your own justification entitle you to claim. When a hypothesis fails, the theorem has made no promise. Settle each case below.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT ARE YOU ENTITLED TO CLAIM?
For each, say what is wrong or what the facts support.
1. A student computes \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac1{x^2}\,dx=\left[-\frac1x\right]_{-1}^{1}=-2\). What is wrong?
2. For \(f(x)=x^3\), \(f'(0)=0\). What are you entitled to claim about \(x=0\)?
3. A student finds a single critical point \(x=5\) in an optimization problem on \((0,10)\) and writes “so this is the maximum.” What is missing?
4. A particle has velocity \(v(t)\) in meters per second, with \(t\) in seconds. What are the units of \(\displaystyle\int_0^4v(t)\,dt\)?
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find the average value of \(f(x)=\sin x\) on \([0,\pi]\), then find every \(c\) in \((0,\pi)\) where \(f\) equals that average.
\(f\) is continuous, so the Mean Value Theorem for integrals applies. The average is \(\dfrac1\pi\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\approx0.637\). Solve \(\sin c=\tfrac2\pi\): \(c=\arcsin\tfrac2\pi\approx0.690\) and \(c=\pi-0.690\approx2.451\), and both lie in \((0,\pi)\). The theorem promised at least one such point; here there are two.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Slope and area are one subject. The derivative asks how fast a quantity changes; the integral asks how much has accumulated; and the Fundamental Theorem says each undoes the other. Everything else this semester, from tangent lines and optimization to differential equations and volumes, is that single idea put to work. Carry the habits as much as the formulas: check a theorem's hypotheses before you use its conclusion, justify that an optimum really is one, and state your units. These are the marks of a mathematician who can be trusted.
