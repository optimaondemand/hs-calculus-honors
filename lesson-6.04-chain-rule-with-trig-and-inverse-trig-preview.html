📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Every tool this module has built comes together today, and one new trick, run in reverse, opens the next module early.
Lesson 6.01 and 6.02 gave you six trigonometric derivatives. Lesson 6.03 gave you the Chain Rule. Today combines them: functions like \(\sin(3x^2)\), where a trigonometric function sits on the outside of an algebraic inside, and functions nested three layers deep. Then the lesson turns the Chain Rule around. If \(y=\arcsin x\), you do not yet have a direct rule for \(y\), but you do know that \(\sin y = x\) — and differentiating both sides of that equation, with the Chain Rule applied to \(y\) as a hidden function of \(x\), hands you the derivative anyway. That maneuver has a name, implicit differentiation, and Module 7 will make it official. Today you get an early, honest look at what it can already do.

📚 PART 1 · THE CHAIN RULE ON TRIGONOMETRIC FUNCTIONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
For \(y=\sin(3x^2)\), what is the outer function and what is the inner function? Guess before reading on.

For \(y=\sin(3x^2)\), let \(u=3x^2\), so \(y=\sin u\). The Chain Rule gives \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} = \cos(u)\cdot\frac{du}{dx}\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big[\sin(3x^2)\big] = \cos(3x^2)\cdot 6x = 6x\cos(3x^2) \]

Combining the Chain Rule with the General Power Rule

Differentiate \(y=\tan^3 x\), meaning \((\tan x)^3\). Here the outer function is a power, \(u^3\), with inner function \(u=\tan x\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big[\tan^3 x\big] = 3\tan^2 x\cdot\sec^2 x \]

Nesting the Chain Rule three layers deep

Differentiate \(y = \sqrt{\sin(2x)}\), which stacks a square root on the outside, sine in the middle, and \(2x\) on the inside. Peel one layer at a time, from the outside in, multiplying by each layer's own derivative:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\sin(2x)}}}_{\text{outermost: }\sqrt{\,\cdot\,}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\cos(2x)}_{\text{middle: }\sin(\,\cdot\,)} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{2}_{\text{innermost: }2x} \]

\[ = \frac{\cos(2x)}{\sqrt{\sin(2x)}} \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Each layer contributes exactly one factor, and every factor must appear. Stopping after the first two layers of a three-layer chain (writing \(\frac{\cos(2x)}{2\sqrt{\sin(2x)}}\) and forgetting the innermost factor of 2 from \(\frac{d}{dx}[2x]\)) is the most common way this goes wrong. Count your layers before you start, and check that you have multiplied by one factor per layer at the end.

📚 PART 2 · RUNNING THE CHAIN RULE IN REVERSE: A PREVIEW OF INVERSE TRIG DERIVATIVES

Let \(y = \arcsin x\), so by definition \(\sin y = x\), with \(y\) restricted to \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). There is no direct formula for \(y\) yet, but both sides of \(\sin y = x\) are equal functions of \(x\), so their derivatives with respect to \(x\) must also be equal. The left side is where the trick lives: \(y\) is secretly a function of \(x\), so differentiating \(\sin y\) with respect to \(x\) is a Chain Rule problem, with \(y\) itself playing the role of the inner function:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big[\sin y\big] = \frac{d}{dx}\big[x\big] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \]

Solve for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\cos y} \]

This answer is in terms of \(y\), and the goal is a derivative in terms of \(x\). Since \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), \(\cos y \ge 0\), so \(\cos y = \sqrt{1-\sin^2 y} = \sqrt{1-x^2}\) by the Pythagorean identity and \(\sin y = x\). Substituting back,

\[ \boxed{\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[\arcsin x\big] = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}} \]

The same maneuver on \(y=\arctan x\), where \(\tan y = x\), gives \(\sec^2 y \cdot\frac{dy}{dx}=1\), so \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\sec^2 y} = \frac{1}{1+\tan^2 y} = \frac{1}{1+x^2}\) by the Pythagorean identity for tangent:

\[ \boxed{\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[\arctan x\big] = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}} \]

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The move you just made — differentiating both sides of an equation that defines \(y\) implicitly in terms of \(x\), then solving for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) — is called implicit differentiation. It works for any equation relating \(x\) and \(y\), not only inverse trig functions, and Module 7 will develop it as a technique in its own right, alongside the full family of inverse function derivatives it unlocks. Today's derivation is an honest preview using only the Chain Rule, the same way Lesson 5.04 previewed the derivative of \(\sin x\) using a rule Module 6 had not officially taught yet.

Since \(\arccos x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \arcsin x\), differentiating both sides with the Constant and Sum Rules gives \(\frac{d}{dx}[\arccos x] = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\) immediately, with no new derivation required.

📚 PART 3 · A PHYSICAL APPLICATION: SIMPLE HARMONIC MOTION

A mass bobbing on a spring, with no friction, has position given by \(x(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi)\), where \(A\) is the amplitude, \(\omega\) is the angular frequency, and \(\varphi\) is a phase shift setting the starting position. Its velocity is \(x'(t)\), found with the Chain Rule, inner function \(u=\omega t+\varphi\):

\[ x'(t) = A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)\cdot\omega = A\omega\cos(\omega t+\varphi) \]

Differentiate once more for acceleration, using the Chain Rule again:

\[ x''(t) = -A\omega^2\sin(\omega t+\varphi) = -\omega^2\, x(t) \]

▶  Before you reveal: why does \(x''(t) = -\omega^2 x(t)\) matter? What does it say, in words, about how the spring's acceleration relates to its position?
It says the acceleration is always proportional to the position, and always points the opposite direction: the farther the mass is displaced, the harder it is pulled back. That single relationship, acceleration proportional to negative position, is what makes the motion oscillate forever (undamped) rather than fly off or die out, and it is exactly the four-step differentiation cycle from Lesson 6.01 (\(\sin\to\cos\to-\sin\to-\cos\)) showing up as physics rather than as algebra. You will build on this equation directly when Module 12 studies motion.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The Chain Rule works forward, peeling layers off a composite function, and it works in reverse, differentiating both sides of an equation that only implicitly defines one variable in terms of another. Module 6 leaves you with all six trigonometric derivatives, a real chain of layered functions you can peel apart with confidence, and an early, honest look at \(\arcsin x\) and \(\arctan x\). Module 7 opens by making that reverse trick official, gives you the full family of inverse function derivatives, and adds logarithms and exponentials to the toolkit.
