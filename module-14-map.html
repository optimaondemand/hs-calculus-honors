The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
Why does finding an area come down to undoing a derivative?
📖 WHAT THIS MODULE IS ABOUT
Module 13 built the definite integral the hard way, as the limit of Riemann sums. This module shows that the hard way was never necessary. Lesson 14.01 introduces accumulation functions, the area gathered from a fixed point up to \(x\), and proves Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem: the derivative of the accumulated area is the function being accumulated. Lesson 14.02 turns that into Part 2, a method for evaluating definite integrals with antiderivatives that replaces a limit of infinitely many rectangles with a subtraction. Lesson 14.03 reads the theorem as net change, so a rate of change integrates to a total change, and uses it to analyze the graph of an antiderivative from the graph of its derivative. Lesson 14.04 gives the first technique for finding antiderivatives of composite functions, integration by substitution, which is the Chain Rule run backward. By the end of the module, slope and area, the two great ideas of the course, are one idea seen from two sides.

💡 HOW TO SUCCEED THIS MODULE
This module changes what an integral is to you: not a limit you grind out but a quantity you can read off a derivative. Before you integrate anything, ask two questions. What is being accumulated, and at what rate? And does the integrand satisfy the conditions the theorem needs: is it continuous on the whole interval? Part 1 and Part 2 are promises with conditions attached, and Lesson 14.04 adds a condition of its own, that the limits must change when the variable does. Do the work of checking each answer a second way when you can, for example by differentiating an antiderivative or by comparing with a rough area estimate. Real understanding here looks like knowing why the subtraction F(b) − F(a) gives the same number as infinitely many rectangles, not only that it does.
