📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One function is its own derivative. Today you meet it properly, and everything from the last two lessons comes together to explain its inverse.
Every rule so far has changed a function under differentiation: \(x^2\) became \(2x\), \(\sin x\) became \(\cos x\). One function refuses to change at all. \(e^x\), where \(e\approx2.71828\) is a specific irrational number, is its own derivative, and that single fact is the reason \(e\) matters throughout mathematics and the sciences: any process whose rate of growth is proportional to its own size — population, radioactive decay, compound interest, a heating cup of coffee — is described by a function built from \(e^x\). Today you prove that fact, and then find the derivative of its inverse, \(\ln x\), two different ways using the two tools Lessons 7.01 and 7.02 just gave you — a genuine test of whether those tools actually agree with each other.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to prove that \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[e^x] = e^x\) from the limit definition of the derivative.
2
I will be able to derive \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x] = \dfrac{1}{x}\) by two independent methods and show they agree.
3
I will be able to differentiate \(a^x\) and \(\log_a x\) for any base \(a>0\), and combine all four rules fluently.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, and implicit differentiation (7.01) and the Inverse Function Rule (7.02).
▶
The Chain Rule from Lesson 6.03, and the fact from precalculus that \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) are inverse functions of each other.
📚 PART 1 · WHY E, AND THE DERIVATIVE OF E^X
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If a function equals its own derivative everywhere, what do you expect its graph to look like near any point, in terms of slope versus height?

Among all exponential functions \(b^x\), exactly one base makes \(\frac{d}{dx}[b^x]\) equal to \(b^x\) itself, with no extra constant multiplied in front. That base is called \(e\), and it can be defined as the unique number satisfying

\[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1}{h} = 1 \]

Numerically, \(e \approx 2.71828\ldots\), an irrational number, and this one property is all the proof below needs. Let \(f(x)=e^x\). By the limit definition of the derivative:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^{x+h}-e^x}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^x\cdot e^h - e^x}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}e^x\cdot\frac{e^h-1}{h} \]

Pull the constant \(e^x\) (constant with respect to \(h\)) out of the limit, and apply the defining property of \(e\):

\[ f'(x) = e^x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1}{h} = e^x\cdot 1 \]

\[ \boxed{\dfrac{d}{dx}[e^x] = e^x} \]

🧩 WORTH KNOWING
Notice what this proof does and does not do: it takes the limit \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1}{h}=1\) as the very definition of what \(e\) is, so nothing further needs proving about that limit. This is the same move Lesson 6.01 made in reverse — there, two limits were proved geometrically and then used to define a derivative; here, a limit's value is used to define a number. Mathematics is comfortable defining a quantity by the one property you most need from it.
📚 PART 2 · THE DERIVATIVE OF LN X, TWO WAYS

Method 1: the Inverse Function Rule (Lesson 7.02). \(\ln x\) is the inverse of \(f(y)=e^y\), and \(f'(y)=e^y\). Apply the rule directly:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[\ln x] = \frac{1}{f'(\ln x)} = \frac{1}{e^{\ln x}} = \frac{1}{x} \]

Method 2: implicit differentiation (Lesson 7.01). Let \(y=\ln x\), so \(e^y=x\). Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\), using the Chain Rule on the left:

\[ e^y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{e^y} = \frac{1}{x} \]

Both methods, built from different lessons, land on the identical answer:

\[ \boxed{\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x] = \dfrac{1}{x}} \]

This agreement is not a coincidence to shrug off. It is a genuine cross-check: two lessons built from entirely different starting points, tested against each other on the single most important inverse pair in the course, and they hold together exactly.

📚 PART 3 · ANY BASE: A^X AND LOG BASE A

For a base \(a>0\), \(a\ne1\), rewrite \(a^x\) using the identity \(a^x = e^{x\ln a}\) (valid since \(a=e^{\ln a}\)). Now it is a Chain Rule problem, with inner function \(u=x\ln a\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big[a^x\big] = \frac{d}{dx}\big[e^{x\ln a}\big] = e^{x\ln a}\cdot\ln a = a^x\ln a \]

\[ \boxed{\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[a^x\big] = a^x\ln a} \]

For \(\log_a x\), use the change-of-base identity \(\log_a x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln a}\), where \(\ln a\) is just a constant. Differentiate with the Constant Multiple Rule:

\[ \boxed{\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[\log_a x\big] = \dfrac{1}{x\ln a}} \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[a^x]\) is not \(x\,a^{x-1}\). That answer applies the Power Rule, which is for a variable base with a constant exponent (like \(x^5\)). Here the base is constant and the exponent is the variable; that is an exponential function, and it needs the exponential rule above, carrying its own factor of \(\ln a\). Confusing these two is the single most common error at this point in the course — always check which part of the expression is changing, the base or the exponent, before choosing a rule.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
John Napier published the first logarithm tables in 1614, more than sixty years before Newton and Leibniz developed the calculus, as a way to turn slow multiplication into fast addition for astronomers and navigators doing arithmetic by hand. Leonhard Euler chose the letter \(e\) for this specific constant around 1731 (likely for "exponential," though he never said so explicitly), and proved many of its defining properties, including the one this lesson used to find its derivative.

Worked example: combining the rules

Differentiate \(f(x) = 3e^x - 5\ln x + 2^x\):

\[ f'(x) = 3e^x - \frac{5}{x} + 2^x\ln 2 \]

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

\(e^x\) is its own derivative, \(\ln x\) differentiates to \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) by two independent methods that agree, and both generalize cleanly to any base: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[a^x]=a^x\ln a\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\log_a x]=\dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\). The derivative toolkit for every elementary function — algebraic, trigonometric, exponential, logarithmic — is now complete. Lesson 7.04 asks what happens when a function combines several of these in a form no single rule reaches directly.
