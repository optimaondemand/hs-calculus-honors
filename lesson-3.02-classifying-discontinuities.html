📚 PART 1 · THREE KINDS OF BROKEN

Every discontinuity you will meet in this course falls into one of three families, distinguished by what the one-sided limits are doing.

Removable. Both one-sided limits exist and agree, so \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) \) exists, but either \(f(a)\) is undefined or it does not equal the limit. The graph has a single hole. It earns the name "removable" because redefining the one value \(f(a)\) to equal the limit would patch the hole and make the function continuous there.

Jump. Both one-sided limits exist, but they disagree. The graph steps abruptly from one height to another. Since the two sides do not agree, the two-sided limit does not exist at all, and there is no single value that could patch the gap.

Infinite. At least one one-sided limit is \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\): the function's height grows or falls without bound as \(x\) approaches \(a\) from that side. The graph has a vertical asymptote at \(x = a\).

⚠️ WATCH OUT
A removable discontinuity is still a discontinuity. Calling it "removable" describes what could fix it, not what it currently is. Until someone actually redefines the function at that one point, it remains not continuous there.
📚 PART 2 · WORKED EXAMPLES: ONE OF EACH KIND

Removable: the hole you have already met

\( f(x) = \dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2} \) is undefined at \(x=2\), but \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2} f(x) = 4 \) exists (both one-sided limits equal 4, since for \(x \ne 2\), \(f(x) = x+2\)). The graph is the line \(y=x+2\) with a single hole at \((2,4)\). This is a removable discontinuity: defining \(f(2) = 4\) would make the function continuous there.

Jump: the two sides disagree

Let \( g(x) = \begin{cases} x & x < 1 \\ x + 2 & x \ge 1 \end{cases} \). As \(x \to 1^-\), \(g(x) \to 1\). As \(x \to 1^+\), \(g(x) \to 3\). The one-sided limits both exist, but \(1 \ne 3\), so \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1} g(x) \) does not exist. The graph steps from height 1 up to height 3 at \(x=1\): a jump discontinuity. No single redefinition of \(g(1)\) could patch this, because there is no one value both sides are approaching.

Infinite: the graph runs away

Let \( h(x) = \dfrac{1}{x-3} \). As \(x \to 3^-\), the denominator is a small negative number, so \(h(x) \to -\infty\). As \(x \to 3^+\), the denominator is a small positive number, so \(h(x) \to +\infty\). Neither one-sided limit is finite, so this is an infinite discontinuity, and the graph has a vertical asymptote at \(x=3\).

Removable: one open circle, otherwise unbroken.
Jump: two segments, stepped, at different heights.
Infinite: the curve runs off along a dashed vertical line.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Every discontinuity has a signature written in its one-sided limits: agree at a finite value but miss the point (removable), disagree with each other (jump), or run off to infinity (infinite). Naming the signature honestly, rather than just saying "it's broken," is what lets you reason precisely about a function's behavior. In the next lesson you will meet what continuity buys you: a theorem that guarantees a root exists, just from a sign change.
