Riemann Sums and Approximating Integrals
How can you add up infinitely many infinitely thin pieces and get an exact answer?
📖 WHAT THIS MODULE IS ABOUT
Derivatives break a changing quantity into instants. Integrals gather the instants back up. This module opens Quarter 4 with the oldest problem in the subject: how much is there under a curve? Lesson 13.01 answers with rectangles, left, right, and midpoint, applied to curves and to tables of data such as a velocity reading every few seconds, and asks when each estimate is too high or too low. Lesson 13.02 gives the sums a language, sigma notation, and then lets the number of rectangles grow without bound; the limits at infinity from Module 2 turn an estimate into an exact area. Lesson 13.03 names what that limit defines, the definite integral, and develops signed area, geometry shortcuts, and the properties that let integrals be added, scaled, and compared. Lesson 13.04 returns to approximation with the Trapezoidal Rule, and uses the concavity ideas of Module 11 to say honestly whether each estimate lands above or below the truth.
📅 YOUR LESSONS
WEEK 27
Lesson 13.01 · Approximating Area with Rectangles
Estimate area and distance with left, right, and midpoint rectangles, and judge whether each estimate runs high or low.
Lesson 13.02 · Sigma Notation and Riemann Sums
Write a Riemann sum in sigma notation, find it in closed form, and let n grow to get an exact area.
WEEK 28
Lesson 13.03 · The Definite Integral
Define the integral as a limit of Riemann sums, read it as signed area, and use its properties.
Lesson 13.04 · The Trapezoidal Rule
Approximate integrals from formulas and data tables with trapezoids, and compare the error with the midpoint estimate.

💡 HOW TO SUCCEED THIS MODULE
Every estimate in this module should come with a sentence about its honesty: is it too high, too low, or exact, and how do you know? Draw the picture before you write a sum, label the width of each rectangle, and say which endpoint each height comes from. When you let n grow, say plainly what you are assuming: that the sums settle on a single number. That assumption is the difference between an estimate and a definition, and naming it is the kind of care this course asks of you. Keep your sums as exact fractions until the end, then convert to decimals to compare.
