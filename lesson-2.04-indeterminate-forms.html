📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
0/0 might be anything. That is exactly why it needs a name.
Over the last three lessons, direct substitution has handed you \(0/0\) three separate times, in three separate disguises, and each time a different technique cracked it: factoring, rationalizing, and a geometric squeeze. Today you give that shape a name, meet its cousin \(\infty/\infty\), and take stock of what algebra can and cannot resolve on its own. You will also meet the name of a tool built to finish the job, a tool this course is not yet ready to hand you.

📚 PART 1 · WHY 0/0 CANNOT BE GIVEN A SINGLE VALUE

A limit expression presents an indeterminate form when substituting the target value produces something like \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\), an expression with no fixed numerical meaning of its own. To see why no single value could ever be assigned to \(0/0\), compare three limits that each substitute to that exact form.

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x} = 1 \qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x^2}{x} = 0 \qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x^2} = \infty \]

Every one of these substitutes to \(\dfrac{0}{0}\) at \(x=0\), and yet the three answers are 1, 0, and infinite. The form \(0/0\) does not describe a value; it describes a tie between a shrinking numerator and a shrinking denominator, and which one shrinks faster decides everything. That is what "indeterminate" means: the form alone cannot determine the answer, so each case has to be examined on its own.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
You already resolved an \(\infty/\infty\) form in Lesson 2.01, before this lesson gave it a name. Dividing \( \dfrac{3x^2-1}{2x^2+5} \) by the highest power of x as \(x\to\infty\) is exactly the algebra technique that handles that form, the same way factoring and rationalizing handle 0/0.

Three techniques from this module resolve most of the indeterminate forms algebra can reach: factoring and canceling, rationalizing with a conjugate, and dividing by the highest power for limits at infinity. A fourth tool, the Squeeze Theorem, reaches further, catching limits like \(\sin x/x\) that no purely algebraic move touches. But not every indeterminate limit gives up to any tool available so far. Consider

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x-\sin x}{x^3} \]

This substitutes to \(0/0\). It does not factor. There is no radical to rationalize. It is not a limit at infinity. No obvious pair of bounding functions squeezes it. It genuinely resists every technique you own right now, and it is not the only one.

🧩 WORTH KNOWING · A TOOL FOR NEXT QUARTER
Limits like this one are exactly what L'Hopital's Rule is for: a general method for 0/0 and infinity/infinity forms, proved using the derivative. Since this course has not yet defined the derivative, you are not ready to use or prove the rule, only to know its name and what it is for. Early next quarter, once the derivative exists, you will prove L'Hopital's Rule and put it to work on limits exactly like this one.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The rule that carries L'Hopital's name did not originate with him. The Swiss mathematician Johann Bernoulli was under a paid arrangement to send his mathematical discoveries to the French nobleman Guillaume de L'Hopital, who published them, including this rule, in 1696 in what became the first calculus textbook ever printed. Mathematical credit has not always followed mathematical discovery.
🤖 WORTH KNOWING · WHAT A CAS CAN AND CANNOT DO
A calculator or computer algebra system can evaluate \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{\sin x}{x} \) and report 1 in an instant. It cannot tell you which technique, factoring, rationalizing, dividing by the highest power, or a geometric squeeze, is the reason that particular limit resolves the way it does. Recognizing an indeterminate form and choosing the right technique to resolve it is a judgment that stays yours, and you will still need it next quarter when deciding whether L'Hopital's Rule even applies to a given limit before running it.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

An indeterminate form is a genuine question, not a dead end and not a fixed value. Some are resolved by factoring, some by rationalizing, some by dividing by the highest power, and some, like sin x over x, only by a cleverer argument entirely. A few resist every tool this module has given you, and that is not a failure of your reasoning; it is a real gap this course will close next quarter, once the derivative gives L'Hopital's Rule its proof.
