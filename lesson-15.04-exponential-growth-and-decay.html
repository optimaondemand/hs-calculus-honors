📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What do populations, radioactive atoms, and a savings account have in common?
All three change at a rate proportional to how much of them there is: \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=ky\). That single differential equation, solved by the separation method from Lesson 15.03, gives the formula \(y=y_0e^{kt}\) and explains why exponential functions show up wherever growth feeds on itself. Today you derive the formula, define doubling time and half-life, fit the model to data, and then ask the question a careful mathematician always asks: where does this model stop being true? This closes the new content of Module 15, in Week 31 of the course.

📚 PART 1 · THE EQUATION dy/dt = ky
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A town has 1000 residents and its population grows at a rate proportional to its current size, so a larger town grows faster in absolute terms. Which graph shape do you expect?

Many quantities change at a rate proportional to how much of them there is. A population has more births when it is larger. A radioactive sample has more atoms decaying per second when there are more atoms. A bank balance earning continuous interest earns more per day when the balance is larger. In symbols, with \(y(t)\) the quantity at time \(t\) and \(k\) the constant of proportionality:

\[ \frac{dy}{dt}=k\,y. \]

This is separable, with \(g(t)=k\) and \(h(y)=y\). Separate: \(\dfrac{dy}{y}=k\,dt\). Integrate: \(\ln|y|=kt+C\). Exponentiate: \(|y|=e^{C}e^{kt}\), so \(y=Ae^{kt}\) for a constant \(A\). At \(t=0\), \(y(0)=A\), so \(A\) is the starting amount \(y_0\).

Exponential growth and decay
If \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=ky\) and \(y(0)=y_0\), then \(y=y_0e^{kt}\). If \(k>0\) the quantity grows exponentially; if \(k<0\) it decays exponentially. The constant solution \(y=0\) is the case \(y_0=0\).

Check it: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[y_0e^{kt}\bigr]=y_0\,k\,e^{kt}=k\,y\) ✓. This is Lesson 7.03's result, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{x}=e^{x}\), with the Chain Rule bringing down the factor \(k\). Notice the logic runs in both directions now: Lesson 7.03 showed that \(e^{kt}\) satisfies the equation, and today's derivation shows that nothing else does.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Why is \(e\) the natural base? Because with base \(e\), the constant \(k\) is exactly the relative rate of change: \(\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dt}=k\). If \(k=0.03\), the quantity is changing at 3% of its current value per unit time, at every instant. That is the meaning of “continuous” growth, and it is why \(e\) appears in compound interest, populations, and decay alike.

Example 1: doubling time. A bacterial culture starts at 1000 cells and doubles every 3 hours. Find \(y(t)\), then the population after 10 hours.

Set \(y=1000e^{kt}\). Doubling at \(t=3\) gives \(2000=1000e^{3k}\), so \(e^{3k}=2\), \(3k=\ln2\), and \(k=\dfrac{\ln2}{3}\approx0.231\) per hour. So \(y(t)=1000e^{(\ln2/3)t}=1000\cdot2^{t/3}\). At \(t=10\): \(y(10)=1000\cdot2^{10/3}\approx10{,}079\) cells. The picture shows the pattern: every equal time step of 3 hours multiplies the population by the same factor, 2.

A population that doubles every 3 hours: 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000, 16000. Equal time steps multiply the quantity by equal factors.

In general, the doubling time of \(y=y_0e^{kt}\) with \(k>0\) is found from \(e^{kT}=2\), so \(T=\dfrac{\ln2}{k}\). It does not depend on \(y_0\): a culture of 1000 and a culture of one million double in the same time. For decay (\(k<0\)), the half-life is found from \(e^{kT}=\tfrac12\), so \(T=\dfrac{\ln2}{|k|}\).

▶  Try it in your notebook first: a quantity grows according to \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=0.04\,y\). Find its doubling time. Then reveal the check.
\(T=\dfrac{\ln2}{0.04}\approx17.33\) time units. (Check: \(e^{0.04\cdot17.33}\approx e^{0.693}=2\) ✓.) The initial amount never entered the calculation.
📚 PART 2 · DECAY, DATA, AND THE LIMITS OF THE MODEL

Example 2: radiocarbon dating. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. A sample has decayed for \(t\) years, with \(y(t)=y_0e^{kt}\).

Half-life gives \(\tfrac12=e^{5730k}\), so \(k=-\dfrac{\ln2}{5730}\approx-1.21\times10^{-4}\) per year. (a) The fraction remaining after 10,000 years is \(e^{10000k}=2^{-10000/5730}\approx0.298\), about 29.8%. (b) A bone retains 30% of its original carbon-14. Its age solves \(0.30=e^{kt}\): \(t=\dfrac{\ln0.30}{k}=\dfrac{5730\,\ln(1/0.30)}{\ln2}\approx9953\) years, roughly 9950 years. Notice what the answer is: a model-based estimate, resting on the assumption that the decay rate has been constant and that the initial carbon-14 level is known.

Example 3: fitting the model to data. A population is 400 at \(t=0\) and 520 at \(t=2\) years. Assuming \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=ky\), find \(k\) and predict the population at \(t=5\). From \(520=400e^{2k}\): \(e^{2k}=1.3\), \(k=\dfrac{\ln1.3}{2}\approx0.1312\) per year. Then \(y(5)=400e^{5k}=400\cdot1.3^{2.5}\approx771\). Two data points fixed the two unknowns, \(y_0\) and \(k\); everything else is a prediction.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not confuse \(k\) with a yearly percentage. A population that grows 3% per year, compounded once a year multiplies by 1.03 each year, which corresponds to \(k=\ln1.03\approx0.02956\), not \(0.03\). The value \(k=0.03\) means 3% growth continuously. For small percentages the two are close, but they are not equal.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH MODEL IS THIS?
Match each situation to what the equation \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=ky\) says about \(k\).
1. A drug leaves the bloodstream so that its amount is 80 mg at \(t=0\) and 40 mg at \(t=6\) hours.
2. An account earns interest continuously at \(\dfrac{dA}{dt}=0.05A\), starting from \(A(0)=1000\) dollars.
3. A quantity increases by exactly 20 units each year, regardless of its size.

Where the model stops being true. The solution \(y=y_0e^{kt}\) grows without bound if \(k>0\). A culture that doubles every 3 hours would, after 10 days (80 doublings), exceed any physical container; no real population grows exponentially forever, because food, space, and competition push back. The equation \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=ky\) is an excellent model for the early stage of growth, or for radioactive decay where nothing interferes, and a poor one for the later stages of a crowded system. A good mathematician states the model, solves it exactly, and then says honestly where it can no longer be trusted. Models with a limit on growth, such as \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=ky\bigl(1-\dfrac{y}{L}\bigr)\), are also separable and are met in later courses.

Convince a classmate: your friend says “exponential growth means the quantity grows by the same amount each hour.” Use the 1000-cell culture, which gains 1000 cells in its first 3 hours, to show what it actually does in its second 3 hours.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
Exponential models are easy to fit and easy to misuse. If a chatbot or a spreadsheet trendline extrapolates an exponential fit far beyond your data, ask what physical limit it is ignoring. The formula can produce a number for any \(t\); only your judgment can say whether the number means anything.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

When a quantity changes at a rate proportional to itself, \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=ky\), it follows \(y=y_0e^{kt}\): doubling time \(\dfrac{\ln2}{k}\), half-life \(\dfrac{\ln2}{|k|}\), and a model whose assumptions are part of the answer. You can now recover a function from the way it changes, which is the second great use of the integral. Next module returns to the integral's first and most visual job, area.
