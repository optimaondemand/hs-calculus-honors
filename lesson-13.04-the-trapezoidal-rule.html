📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A rectangle pretends a curve is flat. What if you let it tilt instead?
Most integrals that arise in the real world have no tidy formula, and most real-world functions arrive as a table of measurements rather than as an equation. For those, you need a good approximation and, just as important, an honest account of how good it is. The Trapezoidal Rule replaces each rectangle with a trapezoid, which follows a rising or falling curve far better than a flat top can. It is the first member of a family of methods used constantly in engineering and science, and it has a lovely property: the direction of its error is predicted by concavity, a tool you already own from Lesson 11.01.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to apply the Trapezoidal Rule to a table of equally spaced data and to a formula, and relate it to the left and right sums.
2
I will be able to predict whether the Trapezoidal Rule over- or underestimates an integral from the concavity of the function, and compare it with the midpoint sum.
3
I will be able to use the error bound \(\dfrac{K(b-a)^3}{12n^2}\) to decide how many subintervals a required accuracy needs.

📚 PART 1 · TRAPEZOIDS INSTEAD OF RECTANGLES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A cart rolls down a ramp, and a sensor records its speed every 2 seconds: 0, 3, 7, 12, 20 feet per second at \(t=0,2,4,6,8\). The speed only ever increases. You want the distance traveled in 8 seconds. A left-endpoint sum uses the speed at the start of each 2-second interval; a right-endpoint sum uses the speed at the end. Which is the better single estimate, or is there something better than either?

Lessons 13.01 and 13.02 used rectangles. A rectangle pretends the function is flat across each small interval. But a curve that is climbing is not flat, and a better pretense is available: draw a straight line from the curve's height at one end of the piece to its height at the other. The strip under that line is a trapezoid, and a trapezoid with parallel sides \(f(x_{i-1})\) and \(f(x_i)\) and width \(\Delta x\) has area

\[ \frac{f(x_{i-1})+f(x_i)}{2}\,\Delta x. \]

Add up all \(n\) trapezoids. Each interior height \(f(x_1),\dots,f(x_{n-1})\) is the right side of one trapezoid and the left side of the next, so it is counted twice, once in each, and then halved. The two end heights are counted once. The result is the Trapezoidal Rule:

The Trapezoidal Rule
With \(\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}\) and \(x_i=a+i\,\Delta x\), \[ T_n=\frac{\Delta x}{2}\Bigl[f(x_0)+2f(x_1)+2f(x_2)+\cdots+2f(x_{n-1})+f(x_n)\Bigr]\approx\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

The cart, finished. With \(\Delta t=2\) and the five readings \(0,3,7,12,20\):

\[ T_4=\frac{2}{2}\bigl[0+2(3)+2(7)+2(12)+20\bigr]=\bigl[0+6+14+24+20\bigr]=64\text{ feet}. \]

Compare the left sum \(L_4=2(0+3+7+12)=44\) and the right sum \(R_4=2(3+7+12+20)=84\). The truth is trapped between them, and the trapezoid estimate \(64\) is exactly their average, \(\tfrac{44+84}{2}=64\). That is no coincidence.

Trapezoid is the average of left and right
\(T_n=\dfrac{L_n+R_n}{2}\). Indeed \(L_n=\Delta x\,[f_0+\cdots+f_{n-1}]\) and \(R_n=\Delta x\,[f_1+\cdots+f_n]\), and adding them counts \(f_0\) and \(f_n\) once and every interior value twice, which is \(2\cdot\frac{\Delta x}{2}[\cdots]\) with the factor \(\tfrac12\) outside.

Convince a classmate: Explain to a partner why the endpoint heights \(f(x_0)\) and \(f(x_n)\) appear in the Trapezoidal Rule with coefficient \(1\) but every interior height appears with coefficient \(2\).

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Three classic slips. (1) Forgetting the \(\tfrac12\) in \(\tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\). (2) Doubling the endpoints too, giving \(2f(x_0)\) or \(2f(x_n)\). (3) Miscounting: \(n\) trapezoids use \(n+1\) heights. Five table readings means four trapezoids, so \(n=4\) and \(\Delta t=\frac{8-0}{4}=2\), not \(\frac{8}{5}\).
📚 PART 2 · ACCURACY, CONCAVITY, AND THE ERROR BOUND

A table is one source of data; a formula is another. Let us test the rule on an integral we can compute exactly, so that we can see how good it really is. The Fundamental Theorem arrives in Module 14, but you can verify this one from the definition, exactly as you did for \(\int_0^3 x^2\,dx\) in Lesson 13.03: \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 x^2\,dx=\tfrac83\approx2.6667\).

Trapezoids with \(n=4\). \(\Delta x=\tfrac{2-0}{4}=\tfrac12\), and the heights of \(f(x)=x^2\) at \(x=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,2\) are \(0,\tfrac14,1,\tfrac94,4\):

\[ T_4=\frac{1/2}{2}\Bigl[0+2\bigl(\tfrac14\bigr)+2(1)+2\bigl(\tfrac94\bigr)+4\Bigr]=\frac14\Bigl[\tfrac12+2+\tfrac92+4\Bigr]=\frac14\cdot11=\frac{11}{4}=2.75. \]

The estimate \(2.75\) is too big by \(\tfrac{11}{4}-\tfrac83=\tfrac1{12}\approx0.083\). Why too big? Look back at the picture in Part 1. The parabola bends upward, so a straight chord joining two points on it lies above the curve, and each trapezoid holds slightly more than the strip beneath the curve. This is concavity from Lesson 11.01 at work.

Concavity decides the direction of the error
If \(f\) is concave up on \([a,b]\) (\(f''>0\)), then \(T_n\) is an overestimate. If \(f\) is concave down (\(f''<0\)), then \(T_n\) is an underestimate. If \(f\) is a straight line, \(T_n\) is exact.

The midpoint sum, for comparison. With the same four strips and midpoints \(\tfrac14,\tfrac34,\tfrac54,\tfrac74\), the midpoint sum is

\[ M_4=\tfrac12\Bigl[\tfrac1{16}+\tfrac9{16}+\tfrac{25}{16}+\tfrac{49}{16}\Bigr]=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{84}{16}=\tfrac{21}{8}=2.625, \]

which is too small by \(\tfrac83-\tfrac{21}{8}=\tfrac1{24}\approx0.042\). Notice two things. The midpoint sum errs in the opposite direction (an underestimate on a concave-up curve), and its error is half the size of the trapezoid error. A rectangle at the midpoint is built from a tangent-like line that touches the curve at the center of the strip, and a tangent line to a concave-up curve lies below it.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Those two errors point in opposite directions with sizes in the ratio \(2:1\), so a weighted average cancels them. \(\dfrac{T_4+2M_4}{3}=\dfrac{\tfrac{11}{4}+\tfrac{21}{4}}{3}=\tfrac83\), the exact value! That combination is Simpson's Rule, named for the English mathematician Thomas Simpson, who published it in 1743 (the method itself was known to Kepler and to Cavalieri long before). It is exact here because Simpson's Rule is exact for every quadratic. You are not asked to use it in this course, but you are asked to notice how a careful look at the errors leads to a better method.

How big can the error be?

Numerical analysts do not merely estimate; they bound the error. The standard bound, which we state without proof (it comes from the same ideas as the Mean Value Theorem and is proved in a course on numerical analysis), is

\[ \bigl|\int_a^b f(x)\,dx-T_n\bigr|\le\frac{K\,(b-a)^3}{12\,n^2},\qquad K\ge\max_{[a,b]}|f''(x)|. \]

For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([0,2]\), \(f''=2\), so \(K=2\) and the bound with \(n=4\) is \(\dfrac{2\cdot8}{12\cdot16}=\dfrac1{12}\). That is exactly the error we found, because \(f''\) is constant, so this bound cannot be improved. Look at the \(n^2\) in the denominator: doubling \(n\) divides the error bound by 4. Check it: with \(n=8\), \(T_8=\tfrac{43}{16}=2.6875\), and the error is \(\tfrac{43}{16}-\tfrac83=\tfrac1{48}\), exactly one fourth of \(\tfrac1{12}\).

⚡ TRY IT · TRAPEZOID RULE
Use \(\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}\) and \(T_n=\dfrac{\Delta x}{2}[f_0+2f_1+\cdots+2f_{n-1}+f_n]\). Choose each value.
1. A table gives \(f\) at \(x=0,1,2,3\) as \(2,3,5,8\). What is \(T_3\) for \(\int_0^3 f\)?
2. \(T_2\) for \(\int_0^2 x^2\,dx\) (so \(\Delta x=1\), heights \(0,1,4\))?
3. A table has 7 equally spaced readings. How many trapezoids does the rule use, and what is \(\Delta x\) if the interval is \([0,12]\)?
4. If \(f\) is a straight line, how does \(T_n\) compare with \(\int_a^b f\)?
📚 PART 3 · READING THE ERROR BEFORE YOU COMPUTE IT

The error is not random. It follows concavity, and that gives you something an answer from a calculator cannot: the direction of the error, without computing anything. Here is a function with an exact value you will be able to prove in Lesson 14.02: \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx=2\), the area of one arch of the sine wave. With \(n=4\), \(\Delta x=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\):

\[ T_4=\frac{\pi}{8}\Bigl[0+2\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}+1+\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\Bigr)+0\Bigr]=\frac{\pi}{8}\bigl(2+2\sqrt2\bigr)\approx1.896. \]

The sine arch is concave down on \((0,\pi)\) (\(\sin''x=-\sin x<0\)), so the chords lie below the curve and \(T_4\approx1.896\) is an underestimate of \(2\), as predicted. The midpoint sum \(M_4=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\bigl[\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8}+\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{8}+\sin\tfrac{5\pi}{8}+\sin\tfrac{7\pi}{8}\bigr]\approx2.052\) is an overestimate, again on the opposite side. And the error bound? With \(K=1\), \(\dfrac{1\cdot\pi^3}{12\cdot16}\approx0.161\), while the actual error is \(2-1.896\approx0.104\). The bound holds, but it is cautious.

⚡ TRY IT · OVER OR UNDER?
For each integral, the Trapezoidal Rule with several equal subintervals gives an estimate. Without computing it, decide whether it is an overestimate or an underestimate.
1. \(\displaystyle\int_1^2 x^3\,dx\) (\(f''=6x>0\))
2. \(\displaystyle\int_0^4 \sqrt{x}\,dx\) (\(f''=-\tfrac14 x^{-3/2}\))
3. \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 (3x-5)\,dx\)
4. \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 \bigl(-x^2\bigr)\,dx\) with \(n=4\)
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
The error bound and the over/under rule are guarantees only under their hypotheses. The rule needs \(f''\) to keep one sign across the whole interval. If \(f\) changes concavity inside \([a,b]\), the trapezoids overshoot in some strips and undershoot in others, and the sign of the total error cannot be read from concavity alone. Check that the hypothesis holds before you announce which way the error goes.
🤖 AI AWARENESS
A calculator or spreadsheet will happily compute \(T_{1000}\) in a blink. It will not tell you whether the answer is an overestimate, and it will not tell you how many digits to trust. A spreadsheet that reports 15 digits has not made your estimate 15 digits accurate; the error bound \(\dfrac{K(b-a)^3}{12n^2}\) tells you how many are trustworthy. Use the tool to do the arithmetic and the mathematics to judge the answer.
▶  Try it first: how many trapezoids guarantee an error under \(0.001\) for \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 x^2\,dx\)?
Here \(K=2\) and \(b-a=2\), so the bound is \(\dfrac{2\cdot8}{12\,n^2}=\dfrac{4}{3n^2}\). Require \(\dfrac{4}{3n^2}\le0.001\), so \(n^2\ge\dfrac{4}{0.003}\approx1333.3\) and \(n\ge36.5\). Since \(n\) must be a whole number, take \(n=37\). (Check: \(n=36\) gives \(\tfrac{4}{3\cdot1296}\approx0.00103>0.001\), which is not enough; \(n=37\) gives about \(0.000974\).)
▶  Try it first: a sensor records the rate of water flowing into a tank, in gallons per minute, at minutes \(0,5,10,15\): \(4,6,5,9\). Estimate the total gallons in 15 minutes with the Trapezoidal Rule.
Three trapezoids, \(\Delta t=5\): \(T_3=\dfrac52\bigl[4+2(6)+2(5)+9\bigr]=\dfrac52(35)=87.5\) gallons. The rate is not monotonic and its concavity is unknown, so we cannot say whether \(87.5\) is high or low. This is the honest answer to "how good is this estimate?": we know the method, we do not know the direction of the error.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

When a function is known only through data, or has no area formula, the Trapezoidal Rule gives a trustworthy estimate: the average of the left and right sums, with an error you can bound and a direction you can predict from concavity. Module 13 gave you the integral as a limit of sums, as signed area, and as something you can approximate and bound.
