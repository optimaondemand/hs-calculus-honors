📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A theorem is judged by what it lets you prove. This one lets you prove that a flat derivative means a flat function, and that a positive derivative means a rising graph.
Lesson 8.03 stated and proved the Mean Value Theorem. Today you use it. You will prove three facts that sound obvious and that, until this theorem, no one had the tools to prove honestly: a function whose derivative is zero everywhere on an interval is constant; two functions with the same derivative differ by a constant; and a function with a positive derivative is increasing. Each follows from one application of the Mean Value Theorem between two points. You will also see the theorem back in its original habitat, motion, where it settles questions about speed cameras and photo-finish races. This lesson closes Module 8 and, with it, the last new material of Semester 1.

📚 PART 1 · ZERO DERIVATIVES AND CONSTANTS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Two functions \(f\) and \(g\) have exactly the same derivative, \(f'(x)=g'(x)\), at every point of an interval. What do you expect the graphs of \(f\) and \(g\) to look like?

Lesson 8.03 gave you a theorem with real power. Today it pays off, three times. Each payoff is the same move: pick two points, apply the Mean Value Theorem between them, and read off what it forces. Start with the simplest question you can ask about a derivative: what if it is zero everywhere?

Theorem A: zero derivative means constant
If \(f'(x)=0\) for every \(x\) in an interval \(I\), then \(f\) is constant on \(I\).

Proof. Choose any two points \(a

\[ f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)\,(b-a)=0\cdot(b-a)=0. \]

So \(f(b)=f(a)\). Since \(a\) and \(b\) were arbitrary, \(f\) takes the same value at every point of \(I\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem B: same derivative means differ by a constant
If \(f'(x)=g'(x)\) for every \(x\) in an interval \(I\), then there is a constant \(C\) with \(f(x)=g(x)+C\) on \(I\).

Proof. Let \(h=f-g\). Then \(h'(x)=f'(x)-g'(x)=0\) on \(I\), so by Theorem A, \(h\) is a constant \(C\). That is, \(f(x)-g(x)=C\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example. Suppose \(f'(x)=2x\) for all \(x\), and \(f(1)=4\). The function \(g(x)=x^2\) also has \(g'(x)=2x\), so by Theorem B, \(f(x)=x^2+C\) for some \(C\). Use the given point: \(4=1^2+C\), so \(C=3\), and \(f(x)=x^2+3\). One extra piece of information pins the function down completely. This is the seed of antiderivatives, which Module 15 develops fully: a derivative determines a function only up to a constant.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
The word interval in both theorems is not decoration. \(f(x)=\dfrac{x}{|x|}\) equals \(1\) for \(x>0\) and \(-1\) for \(x<0\), so \(f'(x)=0\) at every point where it is defined, yet \(f\) is not constant. Its domain \((-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\) is two pieces, not one interval. The theorem applies on each piece separately, and says nothing across the gap. The guarantee holds only where its hypotheses hold.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: \(f'(x)=3x^2\) for all \(x\), and \(f(2)=10\). Find \(f(x)\), and then find \(f(0)\). Then reveal the check.
\(g(x)=x^3\) has \(g'(x)=3x^2\), so by Theorem B, \(f(x)=x^3+C\). Use \(f(2)=10\): \(10=8+C\), so \(C=2\) and \(f(x)=x^3+2\). Then \(f(0)=0+2=2\). The reasoning, not the algebra, is the point: Theorem B is what lets you write \(+C\) and be sure no other function qualifies.
📚 PART 2 · THE SIGN OF THE DERIVATIVE

The Mean Value Theorem also converts the sign of the derivative into the direction of the function. This is the first time calculus tells you something about the shape of a graph without plotting a single point, and it is the engine behind most of Quarter 3.

Theorem C: the sign of \(f'\) controls the direction of \(f\)
Let \(f\) be differentiable on an interval \(I\). If \(f'(x)>0\) for all \(x\) in \(I\), then \(f\) is increasing on \(I\): \(adecreasing on \(I\): \(af(b)\).

Proof (for \(f'>0\)). Take \(a0\) and \(b-a>0\), so the product is positive, and \(f(b)>f(a)\). The decreasing case is identical with the sign reversed. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1. \(f(x)=x^3+x\) has \(f'(x)=3x^2+1\). Since \(3x^2\ge0\), we have \(f'(x)\ge1>0\) for every \(x\), so \(f\) is increasing on all of \((-\infty,\infty)\). You did not need a graph, a table of values, or a calculator.

Example 2 (a warning). \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\) has \(f'(x)=-\dfrac{1}{x^2}\), which is negative at every \(x\ne0\). So \(f\) is decreasing on \((-\infty,0)\) and decreasing on \((0,\infty)\), separately. It is not decreasing on the whole domain: \(f(-1)=-1<1=f(1)\), even though \(-1<1\). The domain is two intervals, and Theorem C is about one interval at a time, the same fine print as Part 1.

Example 3 (a one-way street). Theorem C says positive derivative implies increasing; it does not say the reverse. \(f(x)=x^3\) is increasing on \((-\infty,\infty)\), yet \(f'(0)=0\). The derivative is allowed to touch zero at isolated points while the function keeps climbing.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
This lesson sets up Quarter 3. Module 10 turns Theorem C into a method: find where \(f'\) changes sign, and you have found where \(f\) switches between increasing and decreasing, which is exactly where the highs and lows of a graph live. You will not need to graph \(f\) first; the derivative will tell you where to look.

📚 PART 3 · MOTION: THE THEOREM IN ITS NATIVE HABITAT

The Mean Value Theorem began as a fact about motion, so end there. The theorem turns two kinds of evidence about a journey (where you were at two times, and how fast you were going at each moment) into a single conclusion about what must have happened in between.

The speed camera. A car passes a tollbooth at 1:00 and a second booth 60 miles down the road at 1:48, which is \(0.8\) hours later. Its average speed was \(\dfrac{60}{0.8}=75\) mph. Let \(s(t)\) be the car's position. A car's position is continuous and (in any sensible model) differentiable, so the Mean Value Theorem says there is a time \(c\) between 1:00 and 1:48 when the instantaneous speed \(s'(c)\) was exactly 75 mph. If the speed limit were 65 mph, the car exceeded it at some instant, and a ticket issued on that reasoning rests on a theorem, not a hunch.

A model. Suppose a car's position (in miles, \(t\) in hours) is \(s(t)=15t^2\) for \(0\le t\le4\). Then \(s(4)=240\) miles, so the average velocity is \(\dfrac{240-0}{4-0}=60\) mph. The velocity is \(s'(t)=30t\). Solve \(30c=60\): \(c=2\). So at \(t=2\) hours, exactly halfway, the car's speed equals its average. (For any quadratic position function, the guaranteed \(c\) is always the exact midpoint of the interval. For a cubic, it is not.)

The two-car race. Two cars start side by side at time \(0\) and finish a race tied at time \(T\). Must they have had exactly the same speed at some instant? Let \(f\) and \(g\) be their positions, and let \(h=f-g\), the gap between them. Then \(h(0)=0\) because they start together, and \(h(T)=0\) because they finish together. By Rolle's Theorem there is a time \(c\) with \(h'(c)=0\), that is, \(f'(c)=g'(c)\). At that instant the two speedometers read the same, even if one car led for the entire race. This is Rolle's Theorem applied to a difference, the same move that proved Theorem B.

Convince a classmate: a friend says “car A was always ahead of car B except at the start and finish, so they were never going the same speed.” Use the race argument to explain why your friend is wrong.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
If you ask an AI tool to “find the \(c\) in the Mean Value Theorem” for a function, check two things yourself before you trust the answer: did it verify that the hypotheses hold, and did it discard any solution outside the open interval? Tools often solve \(f'(c)=m\) correctly and then forget to check that \(c\) lies strictly between the endpoints. You are responsible for the reasoning; the tool is only a calculator.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The Mean Value Theorem turns local information into global conclusions. A zero derivative on an interval forces a constant function; equal derivatives force a constant difference; a positive derivative forces a rising graph; and a tied finish forces equal speeds at some instant. Every one of those facts comes from the same step: pick two points, apply the theorem between them, and read off what it forces, as long as the hypotheses are true. Next comes the Semester 1 Finals week, where you will review the whole arc, from limits through continuity to every derivative rule you have built. Quarter 3 then puts the Mean Value Theorem to work: tangent lines and linear approximation first, and then the shape of a graph read straight from its derivative.
