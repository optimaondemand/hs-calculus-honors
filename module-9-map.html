Tangent Lines and Linear Approximation
What can a tangent line tell you about a curve near one point, and how far can you trust it?
📖 WHAT THIS MODULE IS ABOUT
Tangent lines are the first thing the derivative gave you. This module asks what else they are good for. Lesson 9.01 turns the slope \(f'(a)\) into complete equations: the tangent line, the normal line perpendicular to it, and the horizontal tangents found where \(f'=0\), including on curves defined implicitly. Lesson 9.02 takes up the edge case a careful student must meet head-on: points where the slope is infinite, and the difference between a vertical tangent line and a cusp. Lesson 9.03 spends the tangent line on estimation, the linearization \(L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\), which lets you compute values like \(\sqrt{4.1}\) by hand. Lesson 9.04 turns the same idea into differentials, estimating how a measurement error spreads into a calculated quantity, and asks the honest question every approximation owes you: how far can you trust it? Semester 2 begins here, with the derivative put to work.

💡 HOW TO SUCCEED THIS MODULE
Every approximation in this module is a promise with a limit on it: the tangent line is a very good stand-in for the curve near the point of tangency and an increasingly poor one far away. Before you trust an estimate, ask two questions. How far is my input from the base point? And does the curve bend toward the tangent line or away from it? Keep the exact value beside your estimate whenever you can check it, and write down the size and direction of the error. Real understanding here looks like stating plainly what you computed, how close you expect it to be, and where it would stop being reliable. Keep your problem sets; the curve-sketching and optimization modules ahead build directly on them.
