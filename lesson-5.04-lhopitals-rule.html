📚 PART 1 · PROVING L'HÔPITAL'S RULE FROM THE DERIVATIVE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Lesson 2.04 said this rule would need the derivative to be proved. Before reading the proof, guess: what part of the derivative's own definition might let you replace f(x) and g(x), near a 0/0 point, with something built from f' and g'? (No wrong answer. Just a guess.)

Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are both differentiable at \(x = a\), with \(f(a) = 0\), \(g(a) = 0\), and \(g'(a) \ne 0\). Since \(f(a) = 0\) and \(g(a) = 0\), you can insert them for free into the limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) without changing anything:

\[ \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)} = \lim_{x\to a} \dfrac{\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}}{\dfrac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a}} \]

The last step multiplies numerator and denominator by the same quantity, \(\dfrac{1}{x-a}\), which changes nothing. But now each piece is a familiar difference quotient: as \(x \to a\), the numerator converges to \(f'(a)\) and the denominator converges to \(g'(a)\), by the very definition of the derivative from Lesson 4.02. Since \(g'(a) \ne 0\), the quotient of the limits is the limit of the quotient:

\[ \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)} \]

This is L'Hôpital's Rule for the \(0/0\) form: when direct substitution gives \(0/0\), the limit of \(f(x)/g(x)\) equals the limit of \(f'(x)/g'(x)\) instead. The rule also holds for the \(\infty/\infty\) form, though that case needs a more general version of the Mean Value Theorem to prove, a theorem this course meets formally in Module 8. For now, use it on both forms; the proof above covers exactly the case you will use most.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
L'Hôpital's Rule only applies to an indeterminate form, \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\). Check by direct substitution first. Applying the rule to a limit that is already determinate, like \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}\dfrac{x+3}{x-1}\), which substitutes cleanly to \(5/1\), produces a wrong answer, not a shortcut. The rule replaces a question you cannot yet answer; it is not a universal first move.
📚 PART 2 · WHEN ONE APPLICATION IS NOT ENOUGH

Sometimes \(f'(a)\) and \(g'(a)\) are themselves both 0, so \(f'(x)/g'(x)\) is still an indeterminate \(0/0\) form at \(a\). Nothing about the proof in Part 1 forbids this: \(f'\) and \(g'\) are simply two new functions, and if they meet the same hypotheses, the same rule applies to them. Apply L'Hôpital's Rule again, to the derivatives of the derivatives, and keep going until the form is no longer indeterminate.

Warm-up example: one application

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3}\dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3}\). Direct substitution gives \(0/0\). Differentiate top and bottom separately:

\[ \lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = \lim_{x\to3}\frac{2x}{1} = 6 \]

This matches the factoring answer from Module 2: \(\dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3} = \dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x+3 \to 6\). Two different tools, the same correct number, exactly the kind of cross-check Module 2 asked you to build the habit of running.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The proof in Part 1 is deliberately the simple case, where g'(a) happens not to be zero. The fully general version of L'Hôpital's Rule, the one that never requires a separate argument for the \(\infty/\infty\) form or for a limit at infinity, was proved rigorously using a strengthened form of the Mean Value Theorem published by Augustin-Louis Cauchy in 1823, more than a century after Bernoulli and L'Hôpital's original 1696 textbook stated the rule without this level of rigor. You will meet Cauchy's tool by name in Module 8.
📚 PART 3 · FINISHING THE LIMIT LESSON 2.04 LEFT OPEN

Return to the exact limit Lesson 2.04 named and set aside:

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x-\sin x}{x^3} \]

Let \(f(x) = x - \sin x\) and \(g(x) = x^3\). Check the form: \(f(0) = 0 - \sin 0 = 0\) and \(g(0) = 0\), so this is \(0/0\), and L'Hôpital's Rule applies. Differentiate once:

\[ f'(x) = 1 - \cos x, \qquad g'(x) = 3x^2 \]

Check the new limit at \(x=0\): \(f'(0) = 1-\cos 0 = 1-1 = 0\), and \(g'(0) = 0\). Still \(0/0\). Apply the rule again:

\[ f''(x) = \sin x, \qquad g''(x) = 6x \]

Check again: \(f''(0) = \sin 0 = 0\), and \(g''(0) = 0\). Still \(0/0\), a third time. Apply the rule once more:

\[ f'''(x) = \cos x, \qquad g'''(x) = 6 \]

Now check the form one last time: \(f'''(0) = \cos 0 = 1\), and \(g'''(0) = 6 \ne 0\). The form is finally determinate. The limit is

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x-\sin x}{x^3} = \frac{f'''(0)}{g'''(0)} = \frac{1}{6} \]

🧩 WORTH KNOWING · A PROMISE KEPT
This is the limit Lesson 2.04 called on to prove that some indeterminate forms genuinely outrun the algebra of Module 2, factoring, rationalizing, dividing by a power, and the Squeeze Theorem. All four of those tools really do fail here; you were not missing a trick. The gap was not in your effort, it was in the toolkit itself, and it stayed open honestly for an entire quarter until the derivative made it closable. That is what it looks like when a course keeps a promise.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

L'Hôpital's Rule replaces an indeterminate \(f(x)/g(x)\) with \(f'(x)/g'(x)\), and it earns that replacement from nothing more exotic than the definition of the derivative itself, applied to two functions that both happen to vanish at the same point. Apply it again whenever the new quotient is still indeterminate, and always check the form before you apply it at all. With this rule and five proved derivative rules now behind you, Module 5 has given you a genuinely complete toolkit for differentiating algebraic functions. Module 6 turns that toolkit loose on trigonometric functions and the single most important rule of the semester: the Chain Rule.
