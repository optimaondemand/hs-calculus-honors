Inflection Points and the Second Derivative Test

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A highway curves left, then right. Where exactly does it switch, and can the second derivative alone tell a mountaintop from a valley floor?
Lesson 11.01 taught you to read concavity from the sign of \(f''\). Today you look at the places where that sign changes. A point on the curve where the bending switches direction is called an inflection point, and finding one takes more care than solving \(f''(x)=0\): some zeros of \(f''\) are inflection points and some are not. Then you put \(f''\) to a second use. At a critical point where the tangent is horizontal, the concavity decides whether you are standing at the bottom of a bowl or the top of a dome. That is the Second Derivative Test. It is fast when it works, and honest about when it does not.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define an inflection point, find the candidates where \(f''\) is zero or undefined, and use a sign chart to confirm which candidates are real inflection points.
2
I will be able to explain with examples why \(f''(c)=0\) does not guarantee an inflection point at \(x=c\), and why the point must be on the curve.
3
I will be able to apply the Second Derivative Test to classify critical points, recognize when it is inconclusive, and switch to the First Derivative Test from Lesson 10.02.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON

Your math notebook and a pencil, plus sign charts for \(f''\) and the Concavity Test from Lesson 11.01, critical points and the First Derivative Test from Lessons 10.01 and 10.02, and cusps and vertical tangents from Lesson 9.02.

📚 PART 1 · WHERE THE BEND CHANGES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
In Lesson 11.01, \(f(x)=x^4-4x^3\) was concave up, then concave down, then concave up again. What do you expect to happen to the curve at the two places where the concavity switches?
Inflection point
A point \((c,f(c))\) on the graph of \(f\) is an inflection point if \(f\) is defined (and continuous) at \(c\) and the concavity of \(f\) changes at \(c\): concave up on one side of \(c\) and concave down on the other.

Because concavity is decided by the sign of \(f''\), an inflection point needs \(f''\) to change sign at \(c\). For the functions you meet in this course, \(f''\) can only change sign at a point where it is zero or where it is undefined. So the candidates are the points where \(f''(c)=0\) or \(f''(c)\) does not exist, with \(c\) in the domain of \(f\). A candidate is only a candidate. You confirm it with a sign chart.

Here is the trap in two pictures. Both \(f(x)=x^3\) and \(f(x)=x^4\) have a horizontal tangent at the origin and \(f''(0)=0\). Only one of them has an inflection point.

Function\(f''(x)\)Sign of \(f''\) near 0Concavity at 0Inflection at 0?
\(x^3\)\(6x\)\(-\) then \(+\)changes (down, then up)Yes
\(x^4\)\(12x^2\)\(+\) then \(+\)does not changeNo
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
\(f''(c)=0\) means \(c\) is a candidate, nothing more. For \(f(x)=x^4\), \(f''(0)=0\) but the graph is concave up on both sides, so there is no inflection point. Always confirm that \(f''\) actually changes sign. And always give both coordinates of an inflection point: it is a point of the graph, so write \((c,f(c))\), not just \(x=c\).

Worked example 1: two inflection points

Find the inflection points of \(f(x)=x^4-6x^2\). Differentiate twice and factor:

\[ f'(x)=4x^3-12x,\qquad f''(x)=12x^2-12=12(x-1)(x+1). \]

\(f''\) is zero at \(x=-1\) and \(x=1\) and is defined everywhere. Test one number in each interval:

IntervalTest point\(f''\) thereConcavity
\((-\infty,-1)\)\(x=-2\)\(12(4)-12=36>0\)up
\((-1,1)\)\(x=0\)\(-12\lt 0\)down
\((1,\infty)\)\(x=2\)\(12(4)-12=36>0\)up

The sign changes at both \(x=-1\) and \(x=1\), so both are inflection points. The \(y\)-coordinates are \(f(\pm1)=1-6=-5\), so the inflection points are \((-1,-5)\) and \((1,-5)\).

Worked example 2: where \(f''\) is undefined

For \(f(x)=x^{1/3}\), \(f'(x)=\tfrac13x^{-2/3}\) and \(f''(x)=-\tfrac29x^{-5/3}\). This \(f''\) is never zero, but it is undefined at \(x=0\), and \(f(0)=0\) is defined. For \(x\lt 0\), \(x^{-5/3}\lt 0\), so \(f''>0\) and the curve is concave up. For \(x>0\), \(f''\lt 0\) and the curve is concave down. The concavity changes at a point that is on the curve, so \((0,0)\) is an inflection point, and, as in Lesson 9.02, the tangent line there is vertical. Compare \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\), where \(f''(x)=-\tfrac29x^{-4/3}\lt 0\) on both sides of \(0\): the concavity never changes, so the cusp at the origin is not an inflection point. And for \(f(x)=\tfrac1x\) from Lesson 11.01, \(f''\) does change sign at \(0\), but \(0\) is not in the domain, so there is no point on the curve there and no inflection point.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Every cubic \(f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\) with \(a\ne0\) has exactly one inflection point, because \(f''(x)=6ax+2b\) is a line that changes sign once, at \(x=-\dfrac{b}{3a}\). That point is also the center of symmetry of the cubic: turn the graph half a turn around it and it lands on itself. For \(x^3-3x\), the center is the origin.

📚 PART 2 · THE SECOND DERIVATIVE TEST

Now put \(f''\) to a second use. Suppose \(f'(c)=0\), so the tangent line at \(c\) is horizontal. Picture the curve there. If it is concave up (a bowl), \(c\) is the bottom: a local minimum. If it is concave down (a dome), \(c\) is the top: a local maximum. That picture is a theorem.

The Second Derivative Test
Suppose \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f''(c)\) exists. If \(f''(c)>0\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\). If \(f''(c)\lt 0\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\). If \(f''(c)=0\), the test gives no information.

Why it is true. Take the case \(f''(c)>0\). Since \(f'(c)=0\), the definition of the derivative applied to \(f'\) gives

\[ f''(c)=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f'(x)-f'(c)}{x-c}=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f'(x)}{x-c}>0. \]

A limit that is positive forces the quotient \(\dfrac{f'(x)}{x-c}\) to be positive for every \(x\ne c\) close enough to \(c\). When \(x\lt c\) the denominator is negative, so \(f'(x)\lt 0\); when \(x>c\) the denominator is positive, so \(f'(x)>0\). The derivative changes from negative to positive at \(c\), and the First Derivative Test from Lesson 10.02 says that is a local minimum. The case \(f''(c)\lt 0\) is the mirror image. When \(f''(c)=0\) the quotient argument has nothing to grab, and that is exactly why the test is silent.

Worked example 3: classify two critical points

Classify the critical points of \(f(x)=x^3-3x\). First \(f'(x)=3x^2-3=3(x-1)(x+1)\), so the critical points are \(x=-1\) and \(x=1\). Then \(f''(x)=6x\):

\[ f''(-1)=-6\lt 0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{local max at }(-1,2),\qquad f''(1)=6>0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{local min at }(1,-2). \]

One derivative, two substitutions, two classifications, with no sign chart for \(f'\) needed. That is the test's whole appeal.

When the test is silent

Three functions all have \(f'(0)=0\) and \(f''(0)=0\). The Second Derivative Test cannot separate them, but they behave in three different ways, and the First Derivative Test separates them at once:

Function\(f'(0)\)\(f''(0)\)Sign of \(f'\) near 0What happens at 0
\(x^4\)\(0\)\(0\)\(-\) then \(+\)local minimum
\(-x^4\)\(0\)\(0\)\(+\) then \(-\)local maximum
\(x^3\)\(0\)\(0\)\(+\) then \(+\)neither (an inflection point)
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
\(f''(c)=0\) does not mean there is no extremum at \(c\), and it does not mean there is one. It means this test has nothing to say. As the table shows, the same two numbers \(f'(0)=0\) and \(f''(0)=0\) are shared by a minimum, a maximum, and a point that is neither. When the Second Derivative Test is silent, go back to the sign of \(f'\).
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find and classify every critical point of \(f(x)=x^4-8x^2+3\) with the Second Derivative Test, and give the \(y\)-value of each. Then reveal the check.
\(f'(x)=4x^3-16x=4x(x-2)(x+2)\), so the critical points are \(x=-2,0,2\). Then \(f''(x)=12x^2-16\). At \(x=0\): \(f''(0)=-16\lt 0\), a local maximum at \((0,3)\). At \(x=\pm2\): \(f''(\pm2)=48-16=32>0\), local minima, and \(f(\pm2)=16-32+3=-13\), so the points are \((-2,-13)\) and \((2,-13)\). Every critical point was settled by one substitution.

📚 PART 3 · CHOOSING THE RIGHT TEST

You now own two tests for classifying a critical point. Neither is always better. Choose on purpose.

First Derivative TestSecond Derivative Test
It needsthe sign of \(f'\) on both sides of \(c\)\(f'(c)=0\) and the value \(f''(c)\)
Works atany critical point, including where \(f'\) is undefined (corners, cusps)only critical points with \(f'(c)=0\)
Fails whennever fails (but needs more work)\(f''(c)=0\), where it is silent
Best when\(f''\) is messy or \(c\) is a cusp\(f''\) is easy to compute and nonzero

A sensible strategy: try the Second Derivative Test first for a quick answer, and fall back on the First Derivative Test the moment it is silent or \(f'(c)\) does not exist.

Worked example 4: using both

Classify the critical points of \(f(x)=x^4-4x^3\), the function from Lesson 11.01. Here \(f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2=4x^2(x-3)\), so the critical points are \(x=0\) and \(x=3\), and \(f''(x)=12x(x-2)\).

  • At \(x=3\): \(f''(3)=36>0\), so there is a local minimum, at \((3,-27)\).
  • At \(x=0\): \(f''(0)=0\), so the Second Derivative Test is silent. Use \(f'(x)=4x^2(x-3)\): for \(x\lt 0\) and for \(0\lt x\lt 3\), \(x^2>0\) and \(x-3\lt 0\), so \(f'\lt 0\) on both sides. The derivative does not change sign, so there is no extremum at \(x=0\).

And notice something from Part 1: in Lesson 11.01 you found that \(f''=12x(x-2)\) changes sign at \(x=0\), so \((0,0)\) is also an inflection point. A horizontal tangent at an inflection point, with the curve passing straight through its own tangent line, is a perfectly real thing.

One more case the Second Derivative Test cannot touch at all: \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\) at \(0\). There \(f'(x)=\tfrac23x^{-1/3}\) is undefined, not zero, so the test does not apply. The First Derivative Test does: \(f'\lt 0\) for \(x\lt 0\) and \(f'>0\) for \(x>0\), so \(x=0\) is a local (indeed absolute) minimum at the cusp from Lesson 9.02.

🤖 AI AND GRAPHING TOOLS
A graphing tool will mark inflection points and extrema with a dot, and an AI assistant will classify them in one sentence. Both can be wrong at a cusp, at a flat stretch like \(x^4\) near the origin, or where \(f''\) is zero without changing sign, and you cannot tell without doing the sign analysis yourself. Do the tests by hand first. Then let a tool confirm the picture. Your own justification, with a sign chart or a value of \(f''\), is the part that cannot be handed off.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

An inflection point is a point of the graph where \(f\) is defined and the concavity actually changes, so you find the candidates where \(f''\) is zero or undefined and then confirm the sign change with a sign chart. A zero of \(f''\) alone is not enough, as \(x^4\) shows. At a critical point with \(f'(c)=0\), the Second Derivative Test reads a local minimum from \(f''(c)>0\) and a local maximum from \(f''(c)\lt 0\), and it is silent when \(f''(c)=0\), when the First Derivative Test takes over. Lesson 11.03 combines everything you now know about \(f\), \(f'\), and \(f''\) into a complete method for sketching a curve.
