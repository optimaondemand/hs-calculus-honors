📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Lesson 6.04 used a trick without naming it. Today the trick gets a name, a justification, and a lot more curves to work on.
The circle \(x^2+y^2=25\) is not the graph of a function — it fails the vertical line test — and yet it plainly has a tangent line at every point, with a slope you ought to be able to find. Every rule since Module 4 has differentiated \(y\) written explicitly in terms of \(x\); \(y=x^2\), \(y=\sin x\), and so on. \(x^2+y^2=25\) is not written that way, and solving it for \(y\) produces two ugly square-root pieces that make the Chain Rule messier than it needs to be. Implicit differentiation sidesteps that entirely: differentiate both sides of the equation exactly as they stand, treat \(y\) as a hidden function of \(x\), and solve for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) at the end. You have already done this once, in Lesson 6.04, to find the derivatives of \(\arcsin x\) and \(\arctan x\). Today the technique becomes official, and works on any curve, not just inverse trig functions.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why an equation relating \(x\) and \(y\) can define \(y\) as a function of \(x\) even when it cannot be solved for \(y\) explicitly.
2
I will be able to differentiate both sides of an implicit equation with respect to \(x\), applying the Chain Rule to every \(y\)-term.
3
I will be able to solve the resulting equation for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) and evaluate it at a specific point on the curve.

📚 PART 1 · DIFFERENTIATING BOTH SIDES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
In Lesson 6.04 you differentiated \(\sin y = x\) and got \(\cos y\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 1\), not just \(\cos y = 1\). Why did a \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) appear on the left side but not on the right?

An equation like \(x^2+y^2=25\) does not give \(y\) as an explicit function of \(x\), but near almost any point on the circle, a small piece of the curve does look like the graph of some function \(y=f(x)\) — the graph just needs two pieces (the top half and the bottom half) to cover the whole circle. Implicit differentiation treats \(y\) as one of those hidden functions of \(x\), without ever solving for it, and finds \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) directly:

  1. Differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to \(x\).
  2. Every time you differentiate a term containing \(y\), apply the Chain Rule: differentiate as usual, then multiply by \(\frac{dy}{dx}\).
  3. Solve the resulting equation algebraically for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\).

Worked example: the circle

Find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) for \(x^2+y^2=25\). Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big[x^2\big] + \frac{d}{dx}\big[y^2\big] = \frac{d}{dx}\big[25\big] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \]

Solve for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} \]

At the point \((3,4)\), which does lie on the circle since \(3^2+4^2=25\), the slope is \(\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{3}{4}\). Check this against what you already know from precalculus: the radius to \((3,4)\) has slope \(\frac{4}{3}\), and a tangent line to a circle is always perpendicular to the radius at that point. Perpendicular slopes multiply to \(-1\), and indeed \(\frac{4}{3}\cdot\left(-\frac{3}{4}\right) = -1\). The calculus answer and the geometry agree.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The single most common error is writing \(\frac{d}{dx}[y^2] = 2y\), forgetting the Chain Rule's \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) factor entirely. \(y\) is a function of \(x\), not the variable of differentiation itself, so \(\frac{d}{dx}[y^2] = 2y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}\), never just \(2y\). Every single \(y\)-term needs this extra factor; \(x\)-terms never do.
📚 PART 2 · A CURVE WITH NO EXPLICIT FORMULA AT ALL

The circle could, in principle, be split into two explicit pieces, \(y=\sqrt{25-x^2}\) and \(y=-\sqrt{25-x^2}\). Implicit differentiation earns its keep most clearly on curves where that is not a realistic option. The folium of Descartes, \(x^3+y^3=6xy\), cannot be solved for \(y\) by hand at all, yet it is a perfectly good curve with a well-defined tangent line almost everywhere.

Differentiate both sides of \(x^3+y^3=6xy\) with respect to \(x\). The right side is a product, \(6xy\), so it needs the Product Rule as well as the Chain Rule:

\[ 3x^2 + 3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} = 6y + 6x\frac{dy}{dx} \]

Collect every \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) term on one side and everything else on the other:

\[ 3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} - 6x\frac{dy}{dx} = 6y - 3x^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{dx}\big(3y^2-6x\big) = 6y-3x^2 \]

Divide out the common factor of 3 and solve:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y-x^2}{y^2-2x} \]

The point \((3,3)\) lies on the curve, since \(3^3+3^3=54\) and \(6(3)(3)=54\). At that point, \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{6-9}{9-6} = \frac{-3}{3} = -1\).

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
René Descartes proposed his folium in 1638, in a letter challenging Pierre de Fermat to find its tangent lines — decades before either Newton or Leibniz had formalized the calculus that makes the problem routine. Descartes believed, wrongly, that no general method could find the tangent to a curve like this one. Implicit differentiation is the general method he doubted existed.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

When an equation relates \(x\) and \(y\) without solving for \(y\), differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\) anyway, apply the Chain Rule to every \(y\)-term, and solve for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) at the end. Nothing about the method requires \(y\) to have a tidy explicit formula; that is exactly what makes it more powerful than every rule that came before it.
