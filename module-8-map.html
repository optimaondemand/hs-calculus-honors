Higher-Order Derivatives and the Mean Value Theorem
If a derivative is itself a function, what happens when you differentiate it again, and what must be true somewhere between two points when you know only the endpoints?
📖 WHAT THIS MODULE IS ABOUT
Every derivative you have computed so far is a function, which means it can be differentiated again. Lesson 8.01 names the result: the second derivative, the third, and the notation for each. Lesson 8.02 puts the first two to work on the oldest application of the derivative, motion, where \(s'\) is velocity and \(s''\) is acceleration. Lesson 8.03 then turns from computing derivatives to a theorem about them: Rolle's Theorem, built on the Extreme Value Theorem from Module 3, and its tilted cousin, the Mean Value Theorem, which says that the average rate of change over an interval is matched by an instantaneous rate somewhere inside it. Lesson 8.04 spends that theorem on its consequences: a function with zero derivative must be constant, and the sign of \(f'\) decides whether \(f\) rises or falls. This module closes the content of Semester 1. Week 17 is Semester 1 Finals: a review lesson, a review activity, and the cumulative Semester 1 Final Exam.

💡 HOW TO SUCCEED THIS MODULE
This module changes what you are asked to do with a derivative: instead of computing one, you will often be asked what it guarantees. A theorem is a promise with conditions attached, so before you use Rolle's Theorem or the Mean Value Theorem, write down its hypotheses and check each one against the function in front of you. A missing hypothesis does not mean the conclusion is false; it means the theorem has made no promise. Real understanding here looks like saying plainly which conditions hold, which fail, and what you are therefore entitled to claim. Semester 1 finals week follows this module, so keep your problem sets: they are your best review material.
