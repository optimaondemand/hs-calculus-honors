📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In Module 13 you needed a page of sigma notation and a limit to find that the area under y = x² from 0 to 3 is 9. Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem has handed you a shortcut. How short can it get?
Part 1 said that an accumulation function is an antiderivative of the function being accumulated. Today we turn that around. If you can find any antiderivative of \(f\), then a definite integral is just a subtraction: the antiderivative's value at the top minus its value at the bottom. This is Part 2, and it is the working half of the Fundamental Theorem. It changes integration from a limit of sums into a reversed derivative. As with every great theorem, we will also practice reading the fine print, because an integral computed carelessly can come out with the wrong sign, or can fail to exist at all.

I will be able to state the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2, and prove it from Part 1 and the fact that functions with equal derivatives differ by a constant.
2
I will be able to evaluate definite integrals of powers, trigonometric functions, exponentials, and \(1/x\) by finding antiderivatives.
3
I will be able to distinguish signed area from unsigned area, and recognize when the theorem does not apply.

📚 PART 1 · FROM ACCUMULATION TO EVALUATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
In Module 13 you found the exact value of \(\int_0^{3}x^2\,dx\) with a limit of Riemann sums, a long computation with a summation formula. Today's theorem will let you do it in one line. What do you expect \(\int_0^3 x^2\,dx\) to equal? (A sketch of \(y=x^2\) on \([0,3]\) fits inside a \(3\times9\) rectangle of area 27.)

Part 1 said that differentiating an accumulation function returns the integrand. That means the accumulation function \(F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) is an antiderivative of \(f\): a function whose derivative is \(f\). So to compute a definite integral you need only find any antiderivative. The reason any one will do is a fact from Lesson 8.04: two functions with the same derivative on an interval differ by a constant.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2
Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), and \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\) on \([a,b]\) (that is, \(F'=f\)). Then \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a). \] We write \(F(b)-F(a)\) as \(\Bigl[F(x)\Bigr]_a^b\) or \(F(x)\Big|_a^b\).

Why it is true

Let \(G(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\). By Part 1, \(G'=f\), so \(G\) is an antiderivative of \(f\). Our given \(F\) is also an antiderivative, so \(F\) and \(G\) have the same derivative on \([a,b]\); by Lesson 8.04, \(F(x)=G(x)+C\) for some constant \(C\). Then

\[ F(b)-F(a)=\bigl[G(b)+C\bigr]-\bigl[G(a)+C\bigr]=G(b)-G(a)=\int_a^b f(t)\,dt-0=\int_a^b f(t)\,dt.\qquad\blacksquare \]

Notice that the constant \(C\) cancels. That is why, when evaluating a definite integral, you do not write \(+C\): any antiderivative works, and they all give the same answer.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Part 1 and Part 2 are two sides of one theorem. Part 1 says integrate, then differentiate and you return to the start. Part 2 says differentiate, then integrate (find an antiderivative and subtract its endpoint values) and you also return to the start, adjusted by a constant. Together they say that differentiation and integration are inverse processes.

Example 1. \(\displaystyle\int_0^3x^2\,dx=\Bigl[\tfrac{x^3}{3}\Bigr]_0^3=\tfrac{27}{3}-0=9\). One line, and it matches the estimate. The long Riemann-sum computation from Lesson 13.03 was a proof that the answer is 9; this is a method for getting it fast.

Example 2. \(\displaystyle\int_1^4\sqrt x\,dx=\int_1^4x^{1/2}\,dx=\Bigl[\tfrac23x^{3/2}\Bigr]_1^4=\tfrac23(8)-\tfrac23(1)=\tfrac{14}{3}\).

Example 3. \(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx=\bigl[-\cos x\bigr]_0^\pi=-\cos\pi-(-\cos0)=1+1=2\). The area under one arch of the sine curve is exactly 2.

Example 4. \(\displaystyle\int_1^2\Bigl(3x^2-\dfrac{2}{x^2}\Bigr)dx\). Rewrite \(\dfrac2{x^2}=2x^{-2}\); an antiderivative of \(3x^2-2x^{-2}\) is \(x^3+2x^{-1}\) (check: its derivative is \(3x^2-2x^{-2}\)). Then \(\bigl[x^3+\tfrac2x\bigr]_1^2=(8+1)-(1+2)=6\).

📚 PART 3 · SIGNED AREA, UNSIGNED AREA, AND THE FINE PRINT

Part 2 computes signed area, the quantity the integral has meant since Lesson 13.03. Area above the horizontal axis counts positive; area below counts negative. So an integral can be zero even when plenty of area is present. For example,

\[ \int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx=\bigl[-\cos x\bigr]_0^{2\pi}=-1-(-1)=0, \]

because the arch above the axis and the arch below cancel. The actual (unsigned) area between the curve and the axis is \(2\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx=4\). To find unsigned area you must split the interval wherever the function changes sign, integrate each piece, and add the absolute values.

A worked example. Let \(f(x)=x^3-x=x(x-1)(x+1)\) on \([-1,2]\). The zeros at \(-1,0,1\) split the interval. An antiderivative is \(F(x)=\tfrac{x^4}{4}-\tfrac{x^2}{2}\).

The graph of \(f(x)=x^3-x\). Blue regions lie above the axis and count positive; the red region lies below and counts negative.

The three pieces: \(\int_{-1}^0=F(0)-F(-1)=0-(-\tfrac14)=\tfrac14\); \(\int_0^1=F(1)-F(0)=-\tfrac14-0=-\tfrac14\); \(\int_1^2=F(2)-F(1)=2-(-\tfrac14)=\tfrac94\). Add them with signs and you get the signed integral \(\tfrac14-\tfrac14+\tfrac94=\tfrac94\) (matching \(F(2)-F(-1)=2-(-\tfrac14)=\tfrac94\), a quick check on the arithmetic). Add the absolute values and you get the unsigned area \(\tfrac14+\tfrac14+\tfrac94=\tfrac{11}{4}\).

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The most dangerous misuse of Part 2 is applying it where its hypothesis fails. Try \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac1{x^2}\,dx\). An antiderivative of \(x^{-2}\) is \(-\dfrac1x\), and a careless evaluation gives \(\bigl[-\tfrac1x\bigr]_{-1}^{1}=-1-1=-2\). But the integrand \(\dfrac1{x^2}\) is positive everywhere, so no integral of it can be negative! The error is that \(f(x)=\dfrac1{x^2}\) is not continuous on \([-1,1]\) (it blows up at \(x=0\)), so Part 2 does not apply. The honest answer is “this theorem does not apply, and the integral needs more careful treatment.” Always check continuity on the whole interval before you subtract.
🤖 AI AWARENESS
An AI tool can evaluate a definite integral instantly, and it can be wrong on exactly the cases above, or give a correct number without telling you the theorem's hypothesis fails. When a tool gives you a value, check three things: Is the function continuous on the interval? Does the sign of the answer agree with the sign of the function? Does an antiderivative's derivative return the integrand? Use the tool to confirm your own work, never to replace it.

Check your understanding. Suppose \(\displaystyle\int_0^4f(x)\,dx=5\) and \(f\) is continuous. Does that tell you the area between the graph and the axis is 5? Not necessarily: it tells you the signed area is 5. If \(f\) dips below the axis somewhere on \([0,4]\), the unsigned area is larger than 5.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2, says that if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(F'=f\), then \(\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\). Any antiderivative works because two antiderivatives differ by a constant that cancels. The integral is signed area, so split at the zeros of \(f\) if you want the unsigned area, and check continuity before you subtract. Lesson 14.03 uses the theorem to measure net change: the integral of a rate is the total change in the quantity, and the graph of an antiderivative can be read from the graph of its derivative.
