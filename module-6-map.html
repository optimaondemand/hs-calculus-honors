Q2 · MODULE 6 · WEEKS 11 AND 12
The Chain Rule and Trigonometric Derivatives
What happens to a derivative when one function is built by feeding one function into another — and can that same idea run in reverse?
📖 WHAT THIS MODULE IS ABOUT
Module 5 finished the algebraic side of differentiation. This module opens a new one. Lessons 6.01 and 6.02 return to the limit definition one more time to prove the derivatives of all six trigonometric functions, using two special limits Module 2 proved months ago for no apparent reason at the time. Lessons 6.03 and 6.04 introduce the Chain Rule, the rule for differentiating a function built by feeding one function into another, and the single most load-bearing rule of the entire year. By the end of the module you can differentiate real composite functions nested several layers deep, and you will have watched the Chain Rule run in reverse to derive the arcsine and arctangent derivatives early, an honest preview of the implicit differentiation Module 7 makes official. Two proofs in this module say plainly where their own rigor stops; naming that edge honestly is as much the lesson as the formula is.
📅 YOUR LESSONS
WEEK 11
Lesson 6.01 · The Derivatives of Sine and Cosine
Prove d/dx[sin x] = cos x and d/dx[cos x] = -sin x from the limit definition, using two special limits from Module 2.
Lesson 6.02 · Derivatives of the Other Trigonometric Functions
Derive the derivatives of tangent, cotangent, secant, and cosecant using the Quotient Rule.
WEEK 12
Lesson 6.03 · The Chain Rule: Differentiating Composite Functions
State and justify the Chain Rule, and apply it to composite algebraic functions.
Lesson 6.04 · The Chain Rule with Trig Functions, and a Preview of Inverse Trig Derivatives
Nest the Chain Rule inside trigonometric functions, then run it in reverse to find the derivatives of arcsin x and arctan x.

💡 HOW TO SUCCEED THIS MODULE
Before you differentiate anything built from more than one function, stop and name the layers out loud: what happens first, what happens to that result second. Students who skip this step and try to apply the Chain Rule from memory are the ones who forget the inner derivative. This module also asks you, twice, to sit with an honest gap — a proof that covers the ordinary case but says plainly where its own argument runs out. Naming the edge of what you actually know, rather than smoothing over it, is the same intellectual honesty Lesson 5.03 asked of the Power Rule, and it belongs here just as much.
