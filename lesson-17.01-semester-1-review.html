Semester 1 Review: From Limits to the Mean Value Theorem
Review Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Eight modules ago you could not say what \(f'(a)\) meant. Now you can differentiate nearly anything — and say what a derivative promises. Is that eight topics, or one argument?
It is one argument. Newton and Leibniz built the calculus without a settled definition of a limit, and a century of mathematicians argued over what an infinitesimal could possibly be; Cauchy's limits finally put a foundation under what already worked. You have just walked that path in the proper order: a limit first, then continuity, then a derivative defined as a limit, then the rules that fall out of the definition, and finally theorems that say what a derivative guarantees. Today's review does not re-teach the semester. It shows you the joints between the parts, trains you to pick the right tool for a problem that carries no label, and asks you to be honest about which promises a theorem actually makes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to trace how each idea of Semester 1 depends on the one before it, from limits to the Mean Value Theorem.
2
I will be able to choose the right tool for an unlabeled limit, derivative, or existence problem, and say why it fits.
3
I will be able to check a theorem's hypotheses before using its conclusion, and state honestly what you are and are not entitled to claim.

📚 PART 1 · THE MAP OF THE SEMESTER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read the map below: if you had to name the one thread that runs from Module 1 to Module 8, which would you pick? There is no wrong answer; commit to one and see how it holds up.

Here is the semester as one chain. Read the second line of each entry as the answer to a single question: what did this module hand to the next one?

MODULE 1
The limit — What a function approaches, made precise: limit laws and one-sided limits.
It gave “approaches” a meaning you can compute with.
MODULE 2
Limits without bound — Limits at infinity, vertical asymptotes, the Squeeze Theorem, indeterminate forms.
It named \(\tfrac00\) as a question with no answer yet, and promised a tool.
MODULE 3
Continuity and the great theorems — Continuity, kinds of discontinuity, the Intermediate and Extreme Value Theorems.
It produced guarantees on closed intervals: a value is reached, a maximum exists.
MODULE 4
The derivative — Average vs. instantaneous rate, the difference-quotient limit, differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) continuous.
It turned a limit into a function: \(f\) became \(f'\).
MODULE 5
Derivative rules I — Sum, power, product, and quotient rules proved from the limit; L'Hôpital's Rule.
It made differentiating routine, and paid Module 2's promise.
MODULE 6
The Chain Rule and trigonometry — The Chain Rule; derivatives of all six trigonometric functions.
It let you differentiate compositions, layer by layer.
MODULE 7
Implicit, inverse, exponential, logarithmic — Implicit differentiation, inverse functions, \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\), logarithmic differentiation.
It completed the toolkit: every function built from the elementary ones can be differentiated wherever it is differentiable.
MODULE 8
Higher-order derivatives and the MVT — Second derivatives, velocity and acceleration, Rolle's Theorem, the Mean Value Theorem.
It turned from computing derivatives to what a derivative guarantees.

Joint 1: the definition and the rules

Every rule you memorized was once a limit you computed. The Power Rule for \(x^3\) is the Module 4 difference quotient, expanded and simplified once:

\[ \frac{(x+h)^3-x^3}{h}=\frac{3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3}{h}=3x^2+3xh+h^2\;\longrightarrow\;3x^2\quad(h\to0) \]

If a rule ever slips from memory, the definition rebuilds it. That is what it means to know the why and not only the procedure.

Joint 2: the promise Module 2 made, kept in Module 5

Lesson 2.04 left \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x-\sin x}{x^3}\) unsolved: substitution gives \(\tfrac00\), and no algebra rescues it. Once the derivative existed, L'Hôpital's Rule settled it. The form is \(\tfrac00\) at every step, which is what licenses each application:

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x-\sin x}{x^3}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{3x^2}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{6x}=\frac16 \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
L'Hôpital's Rule applies only to \(\tfrac00\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). On \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x+1}{x+2}\), substitution already gives \(\tfrac12\). Differentiating top and bottom anyway gives \(\tfrac11=1\), which is wrong. Check the form first, every time.

Joint 3: the great theorems form one chain

The Extreme Value Theorem (Module 3) says a continuous function on a closed interval attains a maximum and a minimum. Rolle's Theorem (Lesson 8.03) spends that guarantee: if such a function also has equal endpoint values and is differentiable inside, an extreme value lies at an interior point where the derivative must be zero. The Mean Value Theorem tilts Rolle's picture until the endpoints no longer match: somewhere inside, the instantaneous rate equals the average rate. So \(\text{EVT}\to\text{Rolle}\to\text{MVT}\) is a single argument, and each step is only as good as the hypotheses it inherits.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Velocity and acceleration are a thread you have only begun to pull. In Module 8, \(s'\) was velocity and \(s''\) was acceleration, and the MVT told you that an average speed of 60 mph over a trip means the speedometer read exactly 60 at some instant. Semester 2 carries the same thread forward through optimization and related rates, and then in reverse, recovering position from velocity.
🤖 AI-AWARENESS
A computer algebra system will return \(\tfrac16\) for the limit above in an instant. It will not tell you whether the form was really \(\tfrac00\) at each step, or why the answer is what it is. Use a tool to check your answer, never to produce your reasoning. On the final, the reasoning is what is being assessed.
📚 PART 2 · WHICH TOOL DOES THIS CALL FOR?

A problem on the final will not say “Chain Rule” at the top. It will hand you a function or a question, and recognizing the tool is part of the work. For each item below, decide which tool to reach for first; the feedback names the signal that should have told you.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH TOOL DOES THIS CALL FOR?
Choose the first tool for each problem. Do not solve it yet; just decide.
1. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1-x}{x^2}\)
2. \(y=\sqrt{x^2+1}\)
3. \(x^3+y^3=6xy\); find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\)
4. \(y=x^{\sin x}\)
5. Show that \(x^3+x-1=0\) has a solution between \(0\) and \(1\).
6. A car covers \(150\) miles in \(2.5\) hours. Show its speed was exactly \(60\) mph at some instant.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Three questions sort almost every problem. (1) Is this a limit with an indeterminate form? (2) Is this a derivative, and if so, how is the function built: sums, products, quotients, compositions, a hidden \(y\), or a variable exponent? (3) Is it asking that something exists, or happens somewhere? Asking them in that order before you pick up the pencil is a habit, not a trick.
📚 PART 3 · MIXED FLUENCY

Now do the work. Each block below has a question; try it in your notebook first, then open the check. Compare your steps, not just your answer.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: differentiate \(f(x)=x^2e^{3x}\).
This is a product, with a Chain Rule step inside \(e^{3x}\): \(f'(x)=2x\,e^{3x}+x^2\cdot3e^{3x}=e^{3x}\,(3x^2+2x)=x\,e^{3x}(3x+2)\).
▶  Try it in your notebook first: differentiate \(g(x)=\arctan(x^2)\).
The outer function \(\arctan u\) has derivative \(\dfrac{1}{1+u^2}\), and the inner function is \(u=x^2\): \(g'(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+(x^2)^2}\cdot2x=\dfrac{2x}{1+x^4}\).
▶  Try it in your notebook first: the curve \(x^3+y^3=6xy\) passes through \((3,3)\). Find the slope there.
Check the point: \(27+27=54=6\cdot9\). Differentiate implicitly: \(3x^2+3y^2y'=6y+6x\,y'\). Collect \(y'\): \(y'(3y^2-6x)=6y-3x^2\), so \(y'=\dfrac{2y-x^2}{y^2-2x}\). At \((3,3)\): \(\dfrac{6-9}{9-6}=-1\).
▶  Try it in your notebook first: a particle moves with \(s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t\) for \(t\ge0\). Find \(v(t)\) and \(a(t)\), then \(v\) at the moment the acceleration is zero.
\(v(t)=s'(t)=3t^2-12t+9=3(t-1)(t-3)\) and \(a(t)=v'(t)=6t-12\). Then \(a(t)=0\) at \(t=2\), where \(v(2)=12-24+9=-3\): the particle is moving in the negative direction at that moment, and \(s(2)=2\).
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find the number \(c\) that the Mean Value Theorem guarantees for \(f(x)=x^3\) on \([0,2]\).
\(f\) is continuous and differentiable everywhere, so the theorem applies. The average rate is \(\dfrac{f(2)-f(0)}{2-0}=\dfrac82=4\). Solve \(f'(c)=3c^2=4\): \(c=\pm\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\), and only \(c=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\approx1.155\) lies in \((0,2)\).

Notice what the last problem did: it checked that the theorem applied before using it, and it threw away a root that solved the equation but fell outside the interval. Both habits return in Part 4.

📚 PART 4 · THE HONEST-REASONING CHECK

Honesty in mathematics has a precise meaning: you may claim only what the hypotheses of a theorem entitle you to claim. When a hypothesis fails, the theorem has made no promise; sometimes its conclusion still happens to hold, and sometimes it fails outright. A careful mathematician says which. Settle each case below.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT ARE YOU ENTITLED TO CLAIM?
For each, say which hypothesis fails or which conclusion the facts support.
1. \(f(x)=|x|\) on \([-1,2]\). The average rate of change is \(\tfrac13\), but \(f'(x)=\pm1\) everywhere it exists. Why does the Mean Value Theorem make no promise here?
2. \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\) has \(f(-1)=-1\) and \(f(1)=1\). Why can you not use the Intermediate Value Theorem to claim a zero on \([-1,1]\)?
3. A classmate computes \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x+1}{x+2}\) by differentiating top and bottom and gets \(1\). What went wrong?
4. \(f\) is differentiable at \(x=a\). Which conclusion are you entitled to?
▶  Try it in your notebook first: check the hypotheses of Rolle's Theorem for \(f(x)=x^3-3x\) on \([-\sqrt3,\sqrt3]\), then find every \(c\) it guarantees.
Hypotheses: \(f\) is a polynomial, so it is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable on the open one. Endpoints: \(f(\sqrt3)=3\sqrt3-3\sqrt3=0\) and \(f(-\sqrt3)=-3\sqrt3+3\sqrt3=0\), so \(f(-\sqrt3)=f(\sqrt3)\). All hold, so some \(c\) has \(f'(c)=0\). Solve \(3c^2-3=0\): \(c=\pm1\), and both lie inside \((-\sqrt3,\sqrt3)\). The theorem promised at least one such point; here there are two.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Semester 1 is one argument. A limit gives “approaches” a meaning you can compute; a derivative is a limit; every rule is that limit worked out once for a whole family of functions; and the great theorems are promises about change whose hypotheses you must check before you claim their conclusions. Carry the habit as much as the formulas. Semester 2 puts the derivative to work on tangent lines, extrema, curve shape, and optimization, and then turns to the integral, which runs the whole story in reverse.
