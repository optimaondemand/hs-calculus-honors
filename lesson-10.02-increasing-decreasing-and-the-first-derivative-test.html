📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Lesson 10.01 gave you the suspects. How do you decide, case by case, which suspects are guilty?
The sign of a derivative is a compass for the graph. Where \(f'\) is positive the function climbs, where it is negative the function falls, and a critical point where the sign flips is a turning point. In this lesson you prove the first part from the Mean Value Theorem, turn it into a bookkeeping method called a sign chart, and use that to classify every critical point as a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither.

2
I will be able to build a sign chart for \(f'\) and use it to find the intervals on which \(f\) increases and decreases.
3
I will be able to apply the First Derivative Test to classify critical points, including points where the derivative does not exist.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT THE SIGN OF f' TELLS YOU
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Suppose \(f'(x)\) is negative at every point of an interval. Moving left to right across that interval, what do you expect the graph of \(f\) to do?

Lesson 10.01 gave you a list of suspects, the critical points. The next question is the one a detective asks: what does each suspect actually do? The answer lives in the sign of \(f'\) on either side. To use it, we first need the right vocabulary.

Increasing and decreasing on an interval
A function \(f\) is increasing on an interval \(I\) if \(f(x_1)<f(x_2)\) whenever \(x_1<x_2\) are in \(I\). It is decreasing on \(I\) if \(f(x_1)>f(x_2)\) whenever \(x_1<x_2\) are in \(I\). A function that is either one is called monotonic on \(I\).

This is a statement about pairs of points, with no mention of derivatives. That is deliberate: it says what the graph does, so a derivative-based test has something to be tested against. The test itself is the payoff of Lesson 8.04.

The Increasing/Decreasing Test
Suppose \(f\) is continuous on an interval \(I\) and differentiable at every interior point of \(I\). If \(f'(x)>0\) for every interior \(x\), then \(f\) is increasing on \(I\). If \(f'(x)<0\) for every interior \(x\), then \(f\) is decreasing on \(I\).

Why it is true

Take any two points \(x_1<x_2\) in \(I\). On \([x_1,x_2]\), \(f\) is continuous, and it is differentiable inside, so the Mean Value Theorem (Lesson 8.03) gives a number \(c\) strictly between them with

\[ f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)\,(x_2-x_1). \]

Now read the right-hand side. The factor \(x_2-x_1\) is positive because \(x_1<x_2\). If \(f'(c)>0\), the product is positive, so \(f(x_2)>f(x_1)\): the function is increasing. If \(f'(c)<0\), the product is negative, so \(f(x_2)<f(x_1)\): decreasing. This is exactly the argument behind Lesson 8.04's claim that a positive derivative means an increasing function, and it is the reason a positive slope, which only describes one instant, controls what the function does over a whole interval.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
The test goes one way. If \(f'>0\) on an interval, \(f\) is increasing there. But an increasing function can have a derivative that is zero at an isolated point: \(f(x)=x^3\) is increasing on all of \((-\infty,\infty)\), yet \(f'(0)=0\). So, from a sign chart, the correct reading is “\(f'>0\) on this interval, therefore increasing,” and never “increasing, therefore \(f'>0\) everywhere.”
📚 PART 2 · THE SIGN CHART

Now we can turn the theorem into a procedure that works every time. It is called a sign chart, and it is the single most reusable tool of the next three weeks.

The steps. (1) Find \(f'(x)\). (2) Mark every critical point of \(f\), and also any \(x\) where \(f\) is not defined, on a number line. These divide the line into open intervals. (3) On each interval, \(f'\) cannot change sign (that would require passing through \(0\), or a break), so test one point of the interval. (4) Read off the sign, and so whether \(f\) increases or decreases there.

Example 1. Return to \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+5\) from Lesson 10.01, where \(f'(x)=3(x-3)(x+1)\) and the critical points are \(x=-1\) and \(x=3\). These cut the number line into three intervals. Test one point in each:

\[ f'(-2)=3(-5)(-1)=15>0,\qquad f'(0)=3(-3)(1)=-9<0,\qquad f'(4)=3(1)(5)=15>0. \]

So \(f\) is increasing on \((-\infty,-1)\) and on \((3,\infty)\), and decreasing on \((-1,3)\). Narrate what the picture says: the graph climbs to \(x=-1\), turns around and falls until \(x=3\), then turns again and climbs forever.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not skip the test points and guess that the signs alternate. They often do, but not always: for \(f(x)=x^4-4x^3\), whose derivative is \(4x^2(x-3)\), the sign is negative on both sides of \(x=0\), because \(x^2\) is positive on both sides. One test point per interval is the only reliable method, and it takes seconds.
📚 PART 3 · THE FIRST DERIVATIVE TEST

A sign chart tells you where \(f\) increases and decreases. The extremum verdict then falls out in one line, because a peak is exactly a place where the graph stops climbing and begins to fall.

The First Derivative Test
Suppose \(c\) is a critical point of \(f\) and \(f\) is continuous at \(c\). Look at the sign of \(f'\) just to the left and just to the right of \(c\).
  • If \(f'\) changes from positive to negative at \(c\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\).
  • If \(f'\) changes from negative to positive at \(c\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\).
  • If \(f'\) does not change sign at \(c\), then \(f\) has no extremum at \(c\).

The reason is the Increasing/Decreasing Test. If \(f'>0\) just to the left, then \(f\) is increasing there, so values rise up to \(f(c)\). If \(f'<0\) just to the right, \(f\) is decreasing there, so values fall away from \(f(c)\). So \(f(c)\) is at least as high as every nearby value: a local maximum. The other cases are the same argument with the signs changed.

Example 2 (two critical points, two different verdicts). Let \(f(x)=x^4-4x^3\). Then \(f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2=4x^2(x-3)\), so the critical points are \(x=0\) and \(x=3\). Test one point in each interval:

\[ f'(-1)=4(1)(-4)=-16,\qquad f'(1)=4(1)(-2)=-8,\qquad f'(4)=4(16)(1)=64. \]

The signs are \(-,\,-,\,+\). At \(x=0\) the sign is negative on both sides, so there is no extremum there, even though \(f'(0)=0\). At \(x=3\) the sign changes from \(-\) to \(+\), so \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x=3\), with value \(f(3)=81-108=-27\). This is the question Lesson 10.01 left open, and now it is settled.

Example 3 (a cusp). Let \(f(x)=x^{5/3}-5x^{2/3}\). Differentiate and factor:

\[ f'(x)=\tfrac53x^{2/3}-\tfrac{10}{3}x^{-1/3}=\frac{5(x-2)}{3\sqrt[3]{x}} \]

The critical points are \(x=2\) (numerator zero) and \(x=0\), where \(f'\) does not exist but \(f\) is defined. Testing: \(f'(-1)=\dfrac{5(-3)}{3(-1)}=5>0\), \(f'(1)=\dfrac{5(-1)}{3(1)}=-\tfrac53<0\), and \(f'(3)=\dfrac{5(1)}{3\sqrt[3]{3}}>0\). The signs are \(+,\,-,\,+\), so \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x=0\), with \(f(0)=0\), and a local minimum at \(x=2\), with \(f(2)=2^{2/3}(2-5)=-3\sqrt[3]{4}\approx-4.762\). The maximum sits at a cusp, a place no equation \(f'(x)=0\) would ever find. That is why Lesson 10.01 insisted on both kinds of critical point.

⚡ TRY IT · READ THE SIGN CHART
In each row, \(c\) is a critical point of a function that is continuous at \(c\). Decide what the sign of \(f'\) says.
1. \(f'\) goes from \(-\) to \(+\) at \(c\)
2. \(f'\) is \(+\) on both sides of \(c\), with \(f'(c)=0\)
3. \(f'\) goes from \(+\) to \(-\) at \(c\)
4. \(f'\) goes from \(-\) to \(+\) at \(c\), and \(f'(c)\) does not exist (a corner)
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find the intervals of increase and decrease of \(f(x)=xe^{-x}\), and classify every critical point. Commit, then reveal the check.
\(f'(x)=e^{-x}-xe^{-x}=e^{-x}(1-x)\). Since \(e^{-x}>0\) always, the only critical point is \(x=1\). Test: \(f'(0)=1>0\) and \(f'(2)=-e^{-2}<0\). So \(f\) is increasing on \((-\infty,1)\) and decreasing on \((1,\infty)\), and \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x=1\) with value \(f(1)=e^{-1}\approx0.368\).

Convince a classmate: in two sentences, why is “\(f'(c)=0\)” not enough to decide whether \(c\) is a maximum, a minimum, or neither? Use \(x^2\), \(-x^2\) and \(x^3\) at \(x=0\) as your three pieces of evidence.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool will draw the curve and let you see the peak. That is good evidence, but a window of the graph is not a proof: a hill that is too small for the window, or a cusp, can be missed. The sign chart is the argument; the picture is the check. Use the tool to confirm what your reasoning predicts, and when the two disagree, trust neither until you find out why.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The sign of \(f'\) controls the behavior of \(f\): positive means increasing, negative means decreasing, and the Mean Value Theorem is why. A sign chart organizes this, and the First Derivative Test reads a verdict off it, a local maximum if the sign goes from \(+\) to \(-\), a local minimum if it goes from \(-\) to \(+\), and no extremum if it does not change. Lesson 10.03 asks a bigger question: not just where a function peaks nearby, but where it is largest or smallest on a whole interval.
