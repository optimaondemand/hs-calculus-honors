📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If you have a fixed length of fence, what is the largest pen you can build?
The oldest optimization problem in the Western tradition may be a legend. In Virgil's Aeneid, Queen Dido, fleeing her brother Pygmalion, is told she may have only as much land for her new city as an ox-hide can cover. She cuts the hide into one long thin strip, lays it out along the coast, and encloses a whole hill: Carthage. Whether or not it happened, the problem is real and beautiful: with a fixed length of boundary, how much can you enclose? This module is about the most useful move in all of calculus, turning a story about the world into a function and letting the derivative find the best case. Today you learn the routine that does it: draw, name, constrain, state the domain, optimize and justify, and answer in a sentence. The calculus you need you already own. What you are building now is the habit of translating, and the discipline of saying why the answer is the best one.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to translate an optimization story into an objective function of one variable, using a labeled diagram, the constraint, and a stated domain.
2
I will be able to find the maximum or minimum with the Closed Interval Method, a first-derivative sign chart, or the Second Derivative Test, choosing the tool that the domain allows.
3
I will be able to justify that a critical point is the extremum you want, reject candidates outside the domain, and answer the original question in a full sentence with units.

📚 PART 1 · THE SIX-MOVE ROUTINE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A farmer has 200 m of fencing and wants to enclose a rectangular pen with the greatest possible area. Before any calculus, what is your instinct about the shape?

Every optimization problem is a story that has to be turned into a function before calculus can touch it. The derivative cannot read words. It can only differentiate a formula in one variable. So the real work of an optimization problem is the translation, and the differentiation at the end is the easy part. Here is the routine, in six moves, that you will use all week:

  1. Draw the situation and label every quantity that can change.
  2. Name the objective. Write the quantity you want to make largest or smallest (area, cost, distance, volume) as a formula, possibly in several variables.
  3. Use the constraint. The story always ties the variables together (a fixed amount of fence, a fixed volume). Solve the constraint for one variable and substitute, so the objective becomes a function of one variable.
  4. State the domain. Which values of that variable make sense in the story? The domain decides which tool you may use in move 5.
  5. Optimize and justify. Find the critical points, then prove which one gives the maximum or minimum, using the Closed Interval Method (Lesson 10.03, Absolute Extrema on a Closed Interval), a sign chart for the first derivative (Lesson 10.02), or the Second Derivative Test (Lesson 11.02).
  6. Answer the question that was asked, in a full sentence with units.

Worked example 1: the pen with four fenced sides

A farmer has 200 m of fencing and wants a rectangular pen of greatest area. Let the width be \(x\) and the length \(y\), both in meters.

Move 1: draw and label.

Objective. Area \(A = xy\), which has two variables. Constraint. The fence goes around all four sides, so \(2x+2y=200\), which gives \(y=100-x\). Substitute to get a function of one variable:

\[ A(x)=x(100-x)=100x-x^2 \]

Domain. A pen needs positive width and positive length. Since \(y=100-x>0\), we need \(0<x<100\), an open interval. Optimize.

\[ A'(x)=100-2x=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x=50, \qquad A''(x)=-2<0 \]

The second derivative is negative, so the graph of \(A\) is concave down and \(x=50\) is a local maximum (Lesson 11.02). It is the only critical point on the interval, so it is the absolute maximum on the whole domain. As a check on the open ends, \(A\) approaches \(0\) as \(x\to0^+\) and as \(x\to100^-\): the pen disappears at both extremes, so the best pen cannot be at an end. Answer. With \(x=50\) we get \(y=100-50=50\), so the greatest area is \(50\cdot50=2500\ \text{m}^2\), achieved by a \(50\ \text{m}\) by \(50\ \text{m}\) square pen. Your instinct about squares was right.

Worked example 2: change the constraint, change the answer

Now let one side of the pen be a straight river that needs no fence. The same \(200\) m of fence goes on three sides: two ends of length \(x\) and one side of length \(y\) parallel to the river.

Same fence, new constraint.

The constraint is now \(2x+y=200\), so \(y=200-2x\), and the area is

\[ A(x)=x(200-2x)=200x-2x^2, \qquad 0<x<100 \]

Then \(A'(x)=200-4x=0\) gives \(x=50\), and \(A''(x)=-4<0\), so this is the maximum. The pen is \(50\) m wide and \(y=200-2(50)=100\) m long, with area \(50\cdot100=5000\ \text{m}^2\). The river bank is worth a lot: the same fence now encloses twice the area, and the best shape is no longer a square. The shape of the optimum depends on the constraint, which is why you must read the story before you write any formula.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not differentiate \(A=xy\) as it stands. With two variables, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy]\) is not \(y\) unless \(y\) is a constant, and here \(y\) changes with \(x\). Use the constraint first to write \(y\) in terms of \(x\) (Lesson 7.01 showed what happens when \(y\) secretly depends on \(x\)), and only then differentiate. A second common slip: setting \(A'(x)=0\) and stopping. A critical point is a candidate, not a verdict.
📚 PART 2 · THE DOMAIN DECIDES THE TOOL

The domain you wrote in move 4 is not decoration. It tells you which justification tool is available. When the domain is a closed interval \([a,b]\), the Extreme Value Theorem (Lesson 3.04) guarantees that the continuous objective has an absolute maximum and minimum, and the Closed Interval Method (Lesson 10.03) finds them: compare the objective at the critical points and at the endpoints. When the domain is open or unbounded, you have no such guarantee, so you must justify by a sign chart or by the Second Derivative Test, and you should look at what the objective does near the ends.

Worked example 3: the open-top box

A square sheet of cardboard \(12\) inches on a side has a small square of side \(x\) cut from each corner. The four flaps are folded up to make an open-top box. What value of \(x\) gives the box of greatest volume?

Cut a square of side x from each corner, then fold up the flaps.

The base of the box is \((12-2x)\) by \((12-2x)\) and the height is \(x\), so the objective is already a function of one variable, because the cut size \(x\) is the only thing the builder chooses:

\[ V(x)=x(12-2x)^2 \]

Domain. We cannot cut a negative square, and the two cuts along one edge, \(x+x=2x\), cannot exceed \(12\). So \(0\le x\le 6\), a closed interval, and we may use the Closed Interval Method. Differentiate with the Product and Chain Rules:

\[ V'(x)=(12-2x)^2+x\cdot 2(12-2x)(-2)=(12-2x)(12-6x)=12(x-6)(x-2) \]

The critical points are \(x=2\) and \(x=6\). Compare the volume at each critical point and each endpoint:

\[ V(0)=0, \qquad V(2)=2\cdot8^2=128, \qquad V(6)=6\cdot0^2=0 \]

The largest value is \(128\), at \(x=2\). Answer. Cutting \(2\)-inch squares from the corners gives the box of greatest volume, \(128\ \text{in}^3\). Notice how little the formula alone tells you. Both ends of the interval give a box of volume zero (no height, or no base), which is why the endpoints lose, but you had to check.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Pierre de Fermat described a method for finding maxima and minima in the 1630s, decades before Newton and Leibniz. His idea was to look for the place where a small change in the variable makes no change, to first order, in the quantity being optimized. That is exactly the condition \(f'(x)=0\) in modern language. Calculus did not invent optimization; it made Fermat's special trick into a routine anyone can follow.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: a rectangular pen must enclose \(400\ \text{ft}^2\). What dimensions use the least fence? Then reveal the check.
Let the width be \(x\) and the length \(y\), so \(xy=400\) and \(y=\dfrac{400}{x}\). The perimeter, the quantity to minimize, is \(P(x)=2x+2y=2x+\dfrac{800}{x}\) with domain \(x>0\) (open and unbounded, so there is no Closed Interval Method here). Then \(P'(x)=2-\dfrac{800}{x^2}=0\) gives \(x^2=400\), so \(x=20\) (we reject \(x=-20\) because it is outside the domain). Since \(P''(x)=\dfrac{1600}{x^3}>0\) for \(x>0\), the graph of \(P\) is concave up and \(x=20\) is the absolute minimum. Then \(y=\dfrac{400}{20}=20\), and the least fence is \(P(20)=80\) ft, for a \(20\) ft by \(20\) ft square. Notice that the minimum perimeter for a given area is again a square, the mirror image of Example 1.
⚡ TRY IT · SET UP, THEN CHECK THE DOMAIN
Decide each answer before you click. These are the moves that decide whether an optimization problem is set up correctly.
1. You have \(80\) ft of fence for a rectangular pen fenced on all four sides, with width \(x\). Which function gives the area?
2. What is the natural domain of \(A(x)=x(40-x)\) for that pen?
3. For the box, \(V'(x)=12(x-6)(x-2)\) on \([0,6]\). Which values do you compare?
📚 PART 3 · JUSTIFY AND ANSWER

Finding \(x=50\) is not the end of the problem. Two disciplines turn a calculation into a solution: justify that the critical point really is the maximum or minimum you want, and answer in context, in the words and units of the original story.

Which justification, when?

  • Closed interval domain. Use the Closed Interval Method: compare the objective at the critical points and the endpoints. The biggest value is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum. No further test is needed.
  • One critical point, open or unbounded domain. Show that it is a local extremum, by a sign change of \(f'\) (Lesson 10.02) or by the sign of \(f''\) (Lesson 11.02). If the continuous objective has only one critical point on the interval, a local maximum there is the absolute maximum, and likewise for a minimum.
  • Several critical points, open domain. Compute the objective at each, and also check what it does near the ends of the domain.

Then check that the answer is reasonable. A pen of width \(-4\) m is not a pen, and a box of volume more than the sheet could ever hold is a sign of an algebra slip. A critical point outside the domain is not an answer; it is a number to reject, and saying why is part of the solution. Honesty in mathematics includes reporting not only what the formula produced but whether the story permits it.

🤖 AI AND COMPUTER ALGEBRA
A graphing tool can draw \(A(x)=100x-x^2\) and mark the peak for you in a second. It cannot decide that your constraint was written correctly, that your domain matches the story, or that the answer should be a sentence about a pen. Those are the parts of the problem that are yours. Use a tool afterward to confirm a result you have already justified by hand, never in place of the justification.
⚡ TRY IT · JUSTIFY AND ANSWER
Three more decisions that separate a solution from a calculation.
1. For \(A(x)=100x-x^2\) on \(0<x<100\), \(A'(50)=0\) and \(A''(x)=-2\). Which statement proves the area is greatest at \(x=50\)?
2. Which is the best final answer to Example 1?
3. While solving a pen problem with width \(x>0\), the algebra gives critical points \(x=-4\) and \(x=9\). What do you do with \(x=-4\)?
