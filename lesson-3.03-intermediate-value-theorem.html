📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
An existence proof that never says where.
Continuity is not just a label; it is a promise, and today you collect on the first great theorem it buys you. If a continuous function is negative at one endpoint and positive at another, something remarkable follows: it must cross zero somewhere in between, even though the theorem itself will never tell you exactly where. This is your first taste of a distinctive kind of mathematical certainty: knowing that a solution exists, without being handed a method to find it.

📚 PART 1 · THE THEOREM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A hiker's elevation is 200 feet below a river's surface at the trailhead and 500 feet above it at the summit. If the hiker's elevation changes continuously along the trail, must there be some point on the trail exactly at river level? (No wrong answer. Reason it through before reading on.)

The Intermediate Value Theorem

Suppose \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \([a,b]\). Let \(N\) be any number between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\). Then there exists at least one number \(c\) in \((a,b)\) such that \(f(c) = N\).

In words: a continuous function cannot skip over a value on its way from \(f(a)\) to \(f(b)\). It must pass through every intermediate height, because it has no gaps, jumps, or leaps to sneak through. Notice both hypotheses the theorem asks for: continuity, and a closed interval. Without continuity, a function could jump clean over \(N\) without ever equaling it. The theorem promises that \(c\) exists; it does not tell you what \(c\) is or how to find it.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Bernard Bolzano proved a version of this theorem in 1817, as part of a broader effort to place calculus on a rigorous footing rather than leaning on pictures of curves. An existence theorem like this one is a distinctive kind of mathematical result: it establishes that a solution is out there with total certainty, without constructing it or saying where. That is a different, and in its own way more powerful, achievement than solving an equation directly.
📚 PART 2 · ROOT EXISTENCE FROM A SIGN CHANGE

The most common use of the IVT is to prove a function has a root (a zero) without solving for it. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) have opposite signs, then 0 is a number between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), so the IVT guarantees some \(c\) in \((a,b)\) with \(f(c) = 0\).

Worked example: does \(x^3 + x - 1\) have a zero on \([0,1]\)?

Let \(f(x) = x^3 + x - 1\).

Step 1, continuity: \(f\) is a polynomial, so it is continuous on every closed interval, including \([0,1]\).

Step 2, endpoint values: \(f(0) = 0 + 0 - 1 = -1\), and \(f(1) = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1\).

Step 3, sign change: \(f(0) = -1 < 0\) and \(f(1) = 1 > 0\), so \(0\) lies between \(f(0)\) and \(f(1)\).

Step 4, conclusion: since \(f\) is continuous on \([0,1]\) and \(0\) is between \(f(0)\) and \(f(1)\), the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees some \(c\) in \((0,1)\) with \(f(c) = 0\). The equation \(x^3+x-1=0\) has a solution between 0 and 1. The proof never says what \(c\) is, only that it exists; a numerical method could later close in on its decimal value, but that is a separate task from proving it exists at all.

⚠️ WATCH OUT
Both hypotheses must be checked and stated, not assumed. If a function is not continuous on the interval, or the interval is not closed, the IVT simply does not apply, and no conclusion about a root follows from a sign change alone.
Explain to a classmate: why does a sign change at the endpoints, combined with continuity, guarantee a root somewhere in between? Point to the exact place in the theorem's statement that does the work.
📚 PART 3 · SET UP YOUR OWN ARGUMENT

Proving a root exists always follows the same four steps: name the continuity, evaluate both endpoints, note the sign change, and state the conclusion in full sentences, citing the theorem.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: write a full IVT argument proving that \( g(x) = x^5 - 3x + 1\) has a root on \([0,1]\). Follow all four steps. Then reveal the check.
\(g\) is a polynomial, so it is continuous on \([0,1]\). \(g(0) = 0 - 0 + 1 = 1\), and \(g(1) = 1 - 3 + 1 = -1\). Since \(g(0) = 1 > 0\) and \(g(1) = -1 < 0\), the value \(0\) lies between \(g(0)\) and \(g(1)\). Because \(g\) is continuous on the closed interval \([0,1]\), the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees some \(c\) in \((0,1)\) with \(g(c) = 0\), so \(g\) has a root on \([0,1]\).

1. \(f(x) = x^2 - 5\) is continuous on \([1,3]\); \(f(1) = -4\) and \(f(3) = 4\).
2. \(h(x) = \dfrac{1}{x-2}\) on \([1,3]\); \(h(1) = -1\) and \(h(3) = 1\), but \(h\) has an infinite discontinuity at \(x=2\).
3. \(k(x) = x^2 + 1\) is continuous on \([2,5]\); \(k(2) = 5\) and \(k(5) = 26\).

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Continuity plus a sign change is enough to guarantee a root exists, even without finding it. Every argument needs both hypotheses stated: continuity, and a closed interval. In the next lesson you will meet a second guarantee that continuity buys you, this time about a maximum and a minimum, and you will see exactly why both of the theorem's conditions are non-negotiable.
