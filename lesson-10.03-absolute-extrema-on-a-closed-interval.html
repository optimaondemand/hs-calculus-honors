📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A local maximum is the top of a hill. An absolute maximum is the highest point of all, and it is not always on a hill.
In Lessons 10.01 and 10.02 you found where a function has local highs and lows, and you used the sign of the derivative to say which are which. Today the question gets bigger: over an entire interval, where is the function truly largest, and where is it truly smallest? The answer is a finite checklist. The Extreme Value Theorem (Lesson 3.04) promises that on a closed interval the extremes exist; Fermat's Theorem (Lesson 8.03) shows they can only be at critical points or endpoints; and a table of values finishes the job. You will also learn to say, honestly, when that promise does not apply.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain the difference between local and absolute extrema, and state the hypotheses of the Extreme Value Theorem that make absolute extrema exist.
2
I will be able to use the Closed Interval Method to find the absolute maximum and minimum values of a continuous function on \([a,b]\), and state both the values and where they occur.
3
I will be able to decide when the Closed Interval Method does not apply, and find an absolute extremum another way or explain why none exists.

📚 PART 1 · LOCAL VERSUS ABSOLUTE, AND THE CLOSED INTERVAL METHOD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A hiker walks a marked trail from mile 0 to mile 6 and records her elevation the whole way. Where must the highest point of the trail be? Make a prediction before you read on.

Lessons 10.01 and 10.02 taught you to find local extrema: places where a function is higher or lower than everything nearby. A local maximum is the top of a hill, but it may not be the highest point anywhere. Today's question is different, and more practical: over a whole stretch of the graph, where is the function largest and where is it smallest? Engineers, economists, and anyone who has asked “what is the most I can get?” are asking this question.

Definition: absolute extrema
Let \(f\) be defined on a set \(S\), and let \(c\) be in \(S\). Then \(f(c)\) is the absolute maximum of \(f\) on \(S\) if \(f(c)\ge f(x)\) for every \(x\) in \(S\), and the absolute minimum if \(f(c)\le f(x)\) for every \(x\) in \(S\). The absolute maximum and minimum values are called the absolute extrema.

Notice the vocabulary. The value \(f(c)\) is the extremum; the input \(c\) is the place where it occurs. A careful answer states both: “the absolute maximum value is 17, and it occurs at \(x=4\).”

Recall the Extreme Value Theorem from Lesson 3.04: if \(f\) is continuous on a closed interval \([a,b]\), then \(f\) has both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on \([a,b]\). That theorem says the extrema exist. It does not say where they are. The derivative does that work.

Why the method works. Suppose an absolute maximum occurs at a point \(c\) of the open interval \((a,b)\). Being the highest point on the whole interval, it is certainly the highest point nearby, so \(f(c)\) is also a local maximum. Fermat's Theorem (Lesson 8.03, used again in Lesson 10.01) says that at an interior local extremum, either \(f'(c)=0\) or \(f'(c)\) does not exist. That is, \(c\) is a critical point. So an absolute extremum can hide in only two kinds of places: a critical point in \((a,b)\), or an endpoint \(a\) or \(b\). The same argument works for a minimum.

The Closed Interval Method
To find the absolute maximum and minimum of a continuous function \(f\) on a closed interval \([a,b]\):
1. Check that \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\).
2. Find every critical point of \(f\) in the open interval \((a,b)\).
3. Evaluate \(f\) at each of those critical points and at the two endpoints \(a\) and \(b\).
4. The largest of these values is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum.

Step 3 is the part students are tempted to skip. You are not asking whether a critical point is a max or a min (the First Derivative Test from Lesson 10.02 does that, and it is not needed here). You are just comparing numbers, and the biggest and smallest numbers win.

Example 1. Find the absolute extrema of \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2+1\) on \([-1,4]\).

Step 1. \(f\) is a polynomial, so it is continuous on \([-1,4]\). Step 2. \(f'(x)=3x^2-6x=3x(x-2)\), which is zero at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\). Both lie in \((-1,4)\), and \(f'\) exists everywhere, so these are all the critical points. Step 3. Make a table:

\(x\)\(-1\) (endpoint)\(0\)\(2\)\(4\) (endpoint)
\(f(x)\)\(-3\)\(1\)\(-3\)\(17\)

For instance, \(f(4)=64-48+1=17\) and \(f(2)=8-12+1=-3\). Step 4. The largest value is \(17\) and the smallest is \(-3\). So the absolute maximum value is 17, at \(x=4\), and the absolute minimum value is \(-3\), attained at both \(x=-1\) and \(x=2\). An extremum can be attained at more than one place. The local maximum at \(x=0\) (value 1) is only a hilltop; it is not the highest point on the interval, because the graph climbs past it all the way to \(x=4\).

The graph of f(x) = x³ − 3x² + 1 on [−1, 4]. Gold points are where the absolute extrema occur.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two slips cost students the most points. First, forgetting the endpoints: in Example 1 the absolute maximum is an endpoint, and a student who only checks \(f'(x)=0\) would wrongly report \(1\). Second, reporting the place instead of the value: the absolute maximum value is 17, not 4. Write both, and label which is which.

Example 2 (a critical point where \(f'\) does not exist). Find the absolute extrema of \(f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x^2}=x^{2/3}\) on \([-1,8]\). Here \(f\) is continuous on the interval, and \(f'(x)=\dfrac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}=\dfrac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x}}\). This is never zero, but it fails to exist at \(x=0\), which is in \((-1,8)\) and in the domain of \(f\). So \(x=0\) is the only critical point. Compare \(f(-1)=1\), \(f(0)=0\), \(f(8)=4\). The absolute maximum value is \(4\) at \(x=8\); the absolute minimum value is \(0\) at \(x=0\), a sharp point of the graph (a cusp, as in Lesson 9.02) where the derivative does not exist. The method caught it because Step 2 includes points where \(f'\) is undefined.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: find the absolute extrema of \(g(x)=x^3-12x\) on \([-3,5]\). Write the critical points, the table of values, and the conclusion in a full sentence. Then reveal the check.
\(g'(x)=3x^2-12=3(x-2)(x+2)\), so the critical points are \(x=\pm2\), both in \((-3,5)\). The values: \(g(-3)=-27+36=9\), \(g(-2)=-8+24=16\), \(g(2)=8-24=-16\), \(g(5)=125-60=65\). The absolute maximum value is \(65\), at \(x=5\) (an endpoint), and the absolute minimum value is \(-16\), at \(x=2\) (a critical point). Notice that the local maximum \(16\) at \(x=-2\) is not the absolute maximum.
📚 PART 2 · WHEN THE GUARANTEE FAILS

The Closed Interval Method rests on the Extreme Value Theorem, and the Extreme Value Theorem has hypotheses: \(f\) must be continuous, and the interval must be closed. Optima mathematics keeps asking the same honest question of every theorem: do its hypotheses actually hold here? Watch what happens when they do not.

The interval is not closed. Consider \(f(x)=x^2\) on the open interval \((-1,2)\). The smallest value is \(f(0)=0\). But there is no largest value: the function climbs toward \(4\) as \(x\) approaches \(2\), and \(x=2\) is not allowed, so it never gets there. Every value you could name as a maximum is beaten by a value slightly closer to \(2\). The theorem did not apply, and indeed there is no absolute maximum.

The function is not continuous. Consider \(f(x)=\tan x\) on \([0,\pi]\). The interval is closed, but \(f\) has an infinite discontinuity at \(x=\pi/2\), and it grows without bound just to its left. There is no absolute maximum. Likewise \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\) on \((0,1]\) is continuous on a half-open interval, grows without bound near \(0\), and has no absolute maximum, though its absolute minimum, \(f(1)=1\), exists.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
A failed hypothesis cancels the guarantee, not necessarily the result. On \((-1,2)\), \(x^2\) has no absolute maximum, yet it does have an absolute minimum. The Extreme Value Theorem simply stops promising anything. When the hypotheses fail, you must reason directly about what the function actually does.

A single critical point on a non-closed interval. Sometimes the work you already know how to do answers the question without the Closed Interval Method. Take \(f(x)=x^2-4x+5\) on \((-\infty,\infty)\). Here \(f'(x)=2x-4\), which is negative for \(x\lt 2\) and positive for \(x>2\) (Lesson 10.02). So \(f\) decreases all the way down to \(x=2\) and then increases forever after. The graph never goes below the value at \(x=2\), so the absolute minimum value is \(f(2)=4-8+5=1\), at \(x=2\). There is no absolute maximum, because \(f(x)\to\infty\) in both directions. The Extreme Value Theorem never applied; monotonicity did the job.

📚 PART 3 · THE METHOD IN CONTEXT

The method is the same when the function describes something real; what changes is the sentence you write at the end. A particle moves along a line with position \(s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t\) metres at time \(t\) seconds, for \(0\le t\le5\). What are the greatest and the least positions it reaches during those five seconds? In other words, what are the absolute extrema of \(s\) on \([0,5]\)?

Critical points. \(s'(t)=3t^2-12t+9=3(t-1)(t-3)\), zero at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\), both in \((0,5)\). Table. \(s(0)=0\), \(s(1)=1-6+9=4\), \(s(3)=27-54+27=0\), \(s(5)=125-150+45=20\). Conclusion. The particle's greatest position is \(20\) m, at \(t=5\) s (the end of the interval). Its least position is \(0\) m, reached at \(t=0\) s and again at \(t=3\) s. In the story: it starts at the origin, moves out to \(4\) m, comes back to the origin, then heads off again and is at \(20\) m when the clock stops. The local maximum of \(4\) m at \(t=1\) is not the farthest it ever gets.

Convince a classmate: a classmate says “the maximum is wherever the derivative is zero.” Use the particle's table to explain, in two sentences, exactly when that is true and when it is not.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Pierre de Fermat was finding greatest and least values around 1636, decades before Newton and Leibniz built the calculus. His method, which he called adequality, treated the vanishing of a small quantity as the signal of a maximum or minimum, and it is the ancestor of the idea that the derivative is zero at a hilltop. The Closed Interval Method is Fermat's old problem with a modern guarantee underneath it: the Extreme Value Theorem, proved rigorously only in the nineteenth century by Bolzano and Weierstrass.
🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool will happily draw a window that hides the endpoint where the maximum occurs, or round two nearly equal values until they look tied. If a tool reports an absolute maximum, check three things yourself: that \(f\) is continuous on a closed interval, that every critical point and both endpoints were compared, and that the number reported is the value, not the place. The tool checks arithmetic; you are responsible for the argument.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

On a closed interval, a continuous function has an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum, and each one lives at a critical point or at an endpoint. Find the critical points, add the endpoints, evaluate, and compare: the biggest number and the smallest number are your answers, stated as values and as places. Always check the hypotheses first, because a theorem's guarantee is only as honest as the conditions it rests on.
