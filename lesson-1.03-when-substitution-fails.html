When Substitution Fails: The 0/0 Form
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
0/0 is not an answer. It is a question.
In Lesson 1.02 the quotient law came with a guardrail: it works only when the denominator's limit is not zero. Today you meet the case the guardrail was protecting you from, a limit where direct substitution produces the meaningless expression 0/0. In fact you already solved one of these limits back in Lesson 1.01, without naming it: \( \lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} \) is exactly this form. Today you learn the two algebraic moves, factoring and rationalizing, that turn 0/0 into a real number every time the fraction has a hidden common factor.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize the indeterminate form 0/0 and explain why direct substitution cannot resolve it.
2
I will be able to evaluate a 0/0 limit by factoring the numerator and denominator and canceling the common factor.
3
I will be able to evaluate a 0/0 limit by multiplying by a conjugate to rationalize a root.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil. You will factor and rationalize by hand.
▶
A quick review of factoring differences of squares and cubes, if it has been a while.
📚 PART 1 · NAMING THE PROBLEM

Consider \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} \). Substituting \(x=3\) gives \( \dfrac{9-9}{3-3} = \dfrac{0}{0} \), and \(\dfrac{0}{0}\) is not a number at all. It does not mean the limit is zero, and it does not mean the limit fails to exist. It means substitution has told you nothing, and you must look closer. Mathematicians call this an indeterminate form: a signal to change technique, not a final answer.

The reason a 0/0 form so often hides a real number is algebraic. If both the numerator and the denominator equal zero at \(x=a\), then \((x-a)\) is a factor of both. For every \(x\) near \(a\) but not equal to it, that shared factor may be canceled, and what remains is a simpler expression whose limit substitution can find directly.

💡 ONE RULE TO REMEMBER
0/0 means simplify first, then substitute.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Long before calculators existed, mathematicians and navigators went out of their way to avoid dividing by a root, since long division by an irrational decimal was slow and error-prone by hand. Multiplying a fraction by a cleverly chosen form of 1, built from a conjugate, to clear a root from the denominator, is centuries-old algebra. Today the same move rescues a limit that direct substitution cannot reach.
📚 PART 2 · RESOLVING 0/0 BY FACTORING

Worked example

Evaluate \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} \).

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} &= \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} \quad \textit{factor the difference of squares}\\ &= \lim_{x\to 3}(x+3) \quad \textit{cancel the shared factor, valid since } x \ne 3\\ &= 3+3\\ &= 6 \end{aligned} \]

The cancellation is legal precisely because a limit only cares about values of \(x\) near 3, never \(x=3\) itself. For every such value, \(x-3\) is a genuine nonzero number, so dividing it out of both the numerator and denominator changes nothing about the function's behavior nearby.

Convince a classmate: what exactly are you allowed to cancel here, and why does it matter that \(x\) never actually equals 3 while you take the limit?

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Not every fraction that looks like 0/0 can be repaired by factoring. It works only when the numerator and denominator truly share a common factor. If a fraction is not 0/0 at all, such as \( \dfrac{x+7}{x-2} \) as \(x\to 2\), factoring will not help, because the denominator's limit is zero while the numerator's is not; that limit does not exist, for a different reason entirely.
📚 PART 3 · RESOLVING 0/0 BY RATIONALIZING

Worked example

Evaluate \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x} \). There is no common factor to spot here, but the numerator holds a root, and roots respond to multiplying by their conjugate.

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x} &= \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+4}+2}{\sqrt{x+4}+2} \quad \textit{multiply by the conjugate}\\ &= \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{(x+4)-4}{x\left(\sqrt{x+4}+2\right)} \quad \textit{difference of squares in the numerator}\\ &= \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x}{x\left(\sqrt{x+4}+2\right)}\\ &= \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2} \quad \textit{cancel the shared factor of } x\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{4}+2}\\ &= \frac{1}{4} \end{aligned} \]

The conjugate is chosen so that multiplying it out clears the root from the numerator, at the cost of introducing that same root in the denominator, where it causes no trouble because the denominator is not the part heading to zero after cancellation.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: factor and cancel to evaluate \( \lim_{x\to -2}\dfrac{x^2-4}{x+2} \). Then reveal the check.
\( \dfrac{x^2-4}{x+2} = \dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x+2} = x-2 \) for \(x\ne-2\). So the limit is \(-2-2 = -4\).

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Whenever direct substitution produces 0/0, the fraction is hiding a common factor, and your job is to find it, whether by factoring or by rationalizing, and cancel it before you substitute. 0/0 is never an answer; it is an instruction to look closer. Not every indeterminate expression resolves this neatly, and in Module 2 you will meet limits that call for an entirely different kind of tool.
