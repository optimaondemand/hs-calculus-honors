📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What rule differentiates \(y=x^x\)? None of them, directly — until logarithms turn the question into one you can already answer.
\(y=x^x\) is neither the Power Rule's shape (a variable base with a constant exponent) nor Lesson 7.03's exponential shape (a constant base with a variable exponent). Both the base and the exponent are \(x\), and no rule in this course, as built, reaches it directly. The fix uses the oldest trick a logarithm ever performed: turning a hard operation into an easy one. Napier invented logarithms in 1614 to turn multiplication into addition, for hand calculation. Today's technique, logarithmic differentiation, uses that same power — log of a product is a sum, log of a quotient is a difference, log of a power pulls the exponent down front — to turn a derivative you cannot find directly into one you can.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize a function shape (variable base, variable exponent, or a complicated product/quotient/power) that calls for logarithmic differentiation.
2
I will be able to apply logarithmic differentiation using the log laws, implicit differentiation, and algebraic solving for \(y'\), in order.
3
I will be able to differentiate a product, quotient, or power of several functions by taking logarithms first, instead of a long chain of Product and Quotient Rule steps.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, implicit differentiation (7.01), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x]=\dfrac{1}{x}\) from Lesson 7.03.
▶
The three log laws from precalculus: \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\), \(\ln(a/b)=\ln a-\ln b\), and \(\ln(a^n)=n\ln a\).
📚 PART 1 · THE METHOD, ON X^X
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If you take \(\ln\) of both sides of \(y=x^x\), the log law for powers lets you pull the exponent down: \(\ln y = x\ln x\). What kind of problem is the right side now — and what technique from this week handles the left side, which still has a \(y\) in it?

Logarithmic differentiation follows three steps:

  1. Take \(\ln\) of both sides, and use the log laws to break apart any product, quotient, or power on the right.
  2. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\), using implicit differentiation on the left (which produces \(\frac{1}{y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}\)).
  3. Solve for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) by multiplying both sides by \(y\), then substitute the original expression for \(y\) back in.

Worked example: y = x^x

Let \(y=x^x\) for \(x>0\). Step 1, take \(\ln\) of both sides and apply the power law:

\[ \ln y = \ln(x^x) = x\ln x \]

Step 2, differentiate both sides. The left side needs implicit differentiation (Lesson 7.01) together with \(\frac{d}{dx}[\ln y] = \frac{1}{y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}\); the right side is a product, needing the Product Rule:

\[ \frac{1}{y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = (1)\ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1 \]

Step 3, solve for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) by multiplying both sides by \(y\), then substitute \(y=x^x\) back in:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = y(\ln x + 1) = x^x(\ln x + 1) \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Stopping at \(\frac{1}{y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = \ln x + 1\) and reporting that as the final answer is the most common way this technique goes unfinished. That expression is \(\frac{y'}{y}\), not \(y'\). Always finish the last step: multiply by \(y\), then substitute the original formula for \(y\) back in, so the final answer is written entirely in terms of \(x\).
📚 PART 2 · A FUNCTION NO OTHER RULE CAN TOUCH

\(y=x^x\) at least resembled a power or an exponential. \(y=(x^2+1)^{\sin x}\) resembles neither: the base \(x^2+1\) is not constant, so Lesson 7.03's exponential rule does not apply, and the exponent \(\sin x\) is not constant, so the Power Rule does not apply either. Logarithmic differentiation is the only tool in this course built to reach it.

Step 1:

\[ \ln y = \sin x\cdot\ln(x^2+1) \]

Step 2, differentiate both sides, using the Product Rule on the right (with the Chain Rule needed for \(\ln(x^2+1)\)):

\[ \frac{1}{y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = \cos x\cdot\ln(x^2+1) + \sin x\cdot\frac{2x}{x^2+1} \]

Step 3, multiply by \(y\) and substitute back:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = (x^2+1)^{\sin x}\left[\cos x\,\ln(x^2+1) + \frac{2x\sin x}{x^2+1}\right] \]

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Logarithmic differentiation also shortens ordinary Product- and Quotient-Rule work when several factors are multiplied or divided together, such as \(y=\dfrac{x^3\sqrt{x-1}}{(x+2)^4}\): taking \(\ln\) turns it into \(\ln y = 3\ln x + \tfrac12\ln(x-1) - 4\ln(x+2)\), a sum of simple pieces to differentiate one at a time, rather than one long Quotient Rule computation with several nested Product Rules inside it. The same idea that let Napier's contemporaries multiply four-digit numbers by adding table entries now shortens calculus computations the same way.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: use logarithmic differentiation to find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = x^{\cos x}\) (\(x>0\)). Then reveal the check.
Step 1: \(\ln y = \cos x\cdot\ln x\). Step 2: \(\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\sin x\ln x + \cos x\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\), using the Product Rule on the right. Step 3: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x^{\cos x}\left(-\sin x\ln x + \dfrac{\cos x}{x}\right)\). Notice the same three steps, in the same order, as both worked examples above — the method does not change, only the function inside it does.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Whenever a function's base and exponent are both variable, or a product/quotient tangles together more factors than you want to Product-Rule by hand, take \(\ln\) of both sides first, differentiate implicitly, and solve for \(y'\) by multiplying back through by \(y\).
