📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why isn't the point on a parabola nearest to a given point the one you would guess?
Last lesson gave you the six-move routine. Today you use it on three new kinds of question. A point is closest to a curve; a can uses the least metal for a given volume; a pen costs the least to build. In each, the story supplies a constraint, calculus supplies the critical points, and the domain decides how to justify the answer. Johannes Kepler noticed the idea behind all of them in 1615, when he studied the volumes of wine barrels: near the best shape, small changes in shape barely change the volume. A zero derivative is the mathematics of that observation. Today you will also meet two honest complications. The best answer can sit at an endpoint of the domain, where the derivative is not zero at all. And a model can have a perfectly correct optimum that is the best for its own assumptions only. Both are lessons in saying exactly what you have shown.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find the point on a curve closest to a given point by minimizing the squared distance, and justify the minimum with a sign chart.
2
I will be able to model and solve surface-area, cost, and revenue optimization problems, including problems whose real-world limits put the best answer at an endpoint of the domain.
3
I will be able to check each answer against the original story and report it in context, with units and with the assumptions of the model stated.

📚 PART 1 · THE POINT NEAREST A CURVE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
On the parabola \(y=x^2\), which point is closest to the point \((0,3)\) on the \(y\)-axis? Predict before you compute.

Distance problems ask for the point on a curve nearest to (or farthest from) a given point. The objective is the distance formula. The constraint is that the point must lie on the curve. A point on \(y=x^2\) has the form \((x,x^2)\), so the constraint is built in, and the distance to \((0,3)\) is

\[ D(x)=\sqrt{(x-0)^2+(x^2-3)^2} \]

The distance from (0, 3) to the curve, at the vertex and at two nearer points.

Differentiating a square root is messy, and there is a trick that removes the mess. Because the square root function is increasing and \(D\ge0\), the numbers \(D\) and \(D^2\) are smallest at exactly the same \(x\)-values. So minimize \(D^2\) instead, and take the square root only at the very end to report the distance.

Worked example 1: the closest point

\[ D^2(x)=x^2+(x^2-3)^2=x^4-5x^2+9 \]

Domain. Every real \(x\) gives a point on the curve, so the domain is all of \((-\infty,\infty)\). Differentiate and factor:

\[ (D^2)'(x)=4x^3-10x=2x(2x^2-5) \]

The critical points are \(x=0\) and \(x=\pm\sqrt{5/2}=\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\approx\pm1.581\). Now justify with a sign chart for \((D^2)'\) (Lesson 10.02):

  • For \(x<-\sqrt{5/2}\): \(2x<0\) and \(2x^2-5>0\), so \((D^2)'<0\). Decreasing.
  • For \(-\sqrt{5/2}<x<0\): \(2x<0\) and \(2x^2-5<0\), so \((D^2)'>0\). Increasing.
  • For \(0<x<\sqrt{5/2}\): \(2x>0\) and \(2x^2-5<0\), so \((D^2)'<0\). Decreasing.
  • For \(x>\sqrt{5/2}\): both factors positive, so \((D^2)'>0\). Increasing.

Decreasing then increasing at \(x=\pm\sqrt{5/2}\) gives local minima; increasing then decreasing at \(x=0\) gives a local maximum. The two minima are the only low points and \(D^2\to\infty\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\), so they are absolute minima. At \(x=\sqrt{5/2}\) we have \(y=x^2=\dfrac52\), and

\[ D^2=\frac{25}{4}-\frac{25}{2}+9=\frac{11}{4}, \qquad D=\frac{\sqrt{11}}{2}\approx 1.658 \]

Answer. The points on the parabola closest to \((0,3)\) are \(\left(\pm\sqrt{5/2},\,\tfrac52\right)\approx(\pm1.581,\,2.5)\), each about \(1.658\) units away. The vertex, at distance \(3\), is a local maximum of the distance: the point you guessed first is the farthest point in its neighborhood. A critical point can be a maximum when you are hunting a minimum, which is exactly why the sign chart is not optional.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two slips to avoid. First, after you minimize \(D^2\), remember that the question asks for the distance, so take the square root: \(D^2=\tfrac{11}{4}\) means \(D=\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}{2}\), not \(\tfrac{11}{4}\). Second, do not assume the answer is the \(x\)-value or the point alone. Read the question: the distance, the point, or both? Here the full answer gives both.

📚 PART 2 · A CAN OF LEAST SURFACE AREA

Johannes Kepler turned a wedding into a mathematics problem. In 1613 he bought wine for his second marriage and was struck by how the merchant measured each barrel's volume with a single rod pushed through the bung. Did the rod really measure volume, whatever the barrel's shape? Kepler's study of the question, published in 1615, noticed something that every optimization problem confirms: near the shape of greatest volume, small changes in the shape change the volume very little. That is the geometric meaning of a derivative that equals zero.

Here is the modern form of a barrel question. A cylindrical can must hold exactly \(1000\ \text{cm}^3\) (one liter). What radius and height use the least metal, that is, give the least total surface area?

A closed can of radius r and height h.

Worked example 2: the can of least surface area

Objective. The surface area of a closed cylinder is two circular ends plus the side: \(S=2\pi r^2+2\pi r h\). Constraint. \(V=\pi r^2h=1000\), so \(h=\dfrac{1000}{\pi r^2}\). Substitute:

\[ S(r)=2\pi r^2+2\pi r\cdot\frac{1000}{\pi r^2}=2\pi r^2+\frac{2000}{r}, \qquad r>0 \]

Domain. A can has a positive radius, and nothing in the story bounds it above, so \(r>0\) (open and unbounded). Optimize.

\[ S'(r)=4\pi r-\frac{2000}{r^2}=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 4\pi r^3=2000 \;\Longrightarrow\; r^3=\frac{500}{\pi} \]

So \(r=\left(\dfrac{500}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}\approx5.419\) cm. Is it a minimum? \(S''(r)=4\pi+\dfrac{4000}{r^3}>0\) for every \(r>0\), so \(S\) is concave up everywhere on its domain and the single critical point is the absolute minimum (Lesson 11.02). Now the height. Since \(r^3=\dfrac{500}{\pi}\),

\[ h=\frac{1000}{\pi r^2}=\frac{1000\,r}{\pi r^3}=\frac{1000\,r}{500}=2r \]

Answer. The can of least surface area has radius about \(5.42\) cm and height about \(10.84\) cm, so its height equals its diameter, \(h=2r\). Its surface area is about \(553.6\ \text{cm}^2\). Notice the elegance: the specific volume \(1000\) never appears in the shape \(h=2r\). Change the volume and the can gets bigger, but its proportions stay the same.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Why do many real cans look taller and thinner than \(h=2r\)? Because surface area is not the only cost. Seams, rims, stacking, and the way a can fits a hand all enter the real design, and a model that ignores them gives a different optimum. A good mathematician states the model's assumptions and says what the answer is the best for. The calculus gave the best can for surface area; whether that is the best can is a question about the assumptions.
📚 PART 3 · COST, REVENUE, AND ENDPOINTS

Worked example 3: minimizing cost

A farmer builds a rectangular pen of area \(1800\ \text{m}^2\) along a straight river that needs no fence. The fence parallel to the river costs \(\$10\) per meter, and each of the two ends costs \(\$6\) per meter. What dimensions minimize the cost?

Let each end have length \(x\) and the side parallel to the river have length \(y\). Constraint. \(xy=1800\), so \(y=\dfrac{1800}{x}\). Objective. The cost is \(C=10y+6(2x)=10y+12x\), so

\[ C(x)=12x+\frac{18000}{x}, \qquad x>0 \]

Then \(C'(x)=12-\dfrac{18000}{x^2}=0\) gives \(x^2=1500\), so \(x=10\sqrt{15}\approx38.73\) m (the negative root is outside the domain). Since \(C''(x)=\dfrac{36000}{x^3}>0\), this is the absolute minimum. The parallel side is \(y=\dfrac{1800}{10\sqrt{15}}=12\sqrt{15}\approx46.48\) m, and the cost is

\[ C=12(10\sqrt{15})+\frac{18000}{10\sqrt{15}}=120\sqrt{15}+120\sqrt{15}=240\sqrt{15}\approx \$929.52 \]

Answer. The cheapest pen has ends of about \(38.7\) m and a river-side of about \(46.5\) m, costing about \(\$929.52\). Reasonableness check: \(38.73\times46.48\approx1800\ \text{m}^2\) ✓, and the ends are the shorter dimension, as you would expect: each extra meter of end length costs \$12 (two ends at \$6), while each extra meter along the river costs only \$10.

Worked example 4: when the best answer is at an endpoint

A tour company sells \(400-8p\) tickets when the price is \(p\) dollars, but its bus holds only \(120\) riders. What price maximizes revenue? Revenue is price times riders:

\[ R(p)=p(400-8p)=400p-8p^2 \]

Ignoring the bus, \(R'(p)=400-16p=0\) gives \(p=25\), but then there would be \(400-8(25)=200\) riders, more than the bus holds. The story imposes a constraint: riders \(400-8p\le120\), so \(p\ge35\). Riders cannot be negative either, so \(400-8p\ge0\), giving \(p\le50\). The domain is the closed interval \([35,50]\), and the critical point \(p=25\) lies outside it. By the Closed Interval Method, compare the endpoints:

\[ R(35)=35\cdot120=4200, \qquad R(50)=50\cdot0=0 \]

Answer. The maximum revenue is \(\$4200\), at a price of \(\$35\), with a full bus. The calculus critical point was irrelevant, and the answer sits at an endpoint. If a problem has a real-world limit, the limit belongs in the domain, and an endpoint can be the honest answer.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: if the bus held \(250\) riders instead of \(120\), what price maximizes revenue? Then reveal the check.
Riders \(400-8p\le250\) gives \(p\ge18.75\), and \(400-8p\ge0\) gives \(p\le50\), so the domain is \([18.75,\,50]\). This time the critical point \(p=25\) is inside the domain, where riders \(=200\le250\). Compare \(R(18.75)=18.75\cdot250=4687.5\), \(R(25)=25\cdot200=5000\), and \(R(50)=0\). The maximum is \(\$5000\) at \(p=25\). Same revenue formula, different domain, different answer. The domain decides.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Optimization is one routine in many costumes: a distance, a surface area, a cost, a revenue. Write the objective, use the constraint to reach one variable, state the domain, find the critical points, and justify the extremum with the right tool for the domain. Sometimes the best answer is at an endpoint, and a real-world limit belongs in the domain.
