📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
At the very top of a smooth hill, what is the slope? And what about the top of a hill with a sharp point?
Lesson 8.03 gave you Fermat's observation: at an interior peak or valley, the derivative is zero if it exists. That sentence is quietly the most useful search rule in calculus. If you want the highest or lowest point of a function, you do not have to check every point of a graph, which is infinitely many. You only have to check the critical points, which are usually a handful. Today you learn what a local extremum is, define a critical point, and practice finding them. You will also learn the discipline that goes with the tool: a critical point is a suspect, not a verdict.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define a local maximum, a local minimum, and a critical point, and state Fermat's Theorem for Local Extrema precisely.
2
I will be able to find all critical points of polynomial, rational, radical, and trigonometric functions, including points where the derivative does not exist.
3
I will be able to explain with examples why a critical point is only a candidate: some are extrema and some are not.

📚 PART 1 · LOCAL EXTREMA AND CRITICAL POINTS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Picture a smooth hill. Walk to the very top and stop. If you laid a straight edge along the hill at exactly that point, how would it be tilted?

Every optimization question you will meet from here to the end of the course has the same shape: where is a quantity as large, or as small, as it can be? The tallest point of a graph, the cheapest design for a can, the time at which a ball is highest. The derivative is the instrument for finding them, because the derivative measures tilt. This module builds the whole method in four steps. Today we learn which points to suspect.

Local extrema

Local maximum and local minimum
A function \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\) if \(f(c)\ge f(x)\) for every \(x\) in some open interval around \(c\) (every \(x\) near \(c\)). It has a local minimum at \(c\) if \(f(c)\le f(x)\) for every \(x\) near \(c\). Either is called a local extremum; the plural is extrema.

“Local” is the honest word. The point only has to beat its neighbors, not the whole graph. A hilltop in Florida is a local maximum even though Everest is higher. Comparing a point against every other point of a domain is a different question, absolute extrema, and Lesson 10.03 answers it.

Where to look

Critical point
A number \(c\) in the domain of \(f\) is a critical point (or critical number) of \(f\) if either \(f'(c)=0\) or \(f'(c)\) does not exist.

Why these two cases and no others? Lesson 8.03 gave us Fermat's fact: if \(f\) has a local extremum at an interior point \(c\) and \(f'(c)\) exists, then \(f'(c)=0\). Read it as a search rule. A local extremum at \(c\) leaves only two possibilities, the derivative is zero there, or the derivative is not defined there. A critical point is any point that has not been ruled out.

Fermat's Theorem for Local Extrema (the search rule)
If \(f\) has a local maximum or local minimum at \(c\) in the interior of its domain, then \(c\) is a critical point of \(f\).

Here is the picture of all three cases, which are the three you must know on sight.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
Fermat's Theorem runs in one direction only. Every local extremum is at a critical point. It does not say that every critical point is a local extremum. The right-hand graph above has a horizontal tangent at the marked point and yet is neither a peak nor a valley. A critical point is a suspect, not a verdict. Lesson 10.02 teaches the test that delivers the verdict.
📚 PART 2 · FINDING CRITICAL POINTS

To find the critical points of \(f\): differentiate, then do two separate jobs. First, solve \(f'(x)=0\). Second, look for any \(x\) in the domain of \(f\) where \(f'(x)\) fails to exist. Skipping the second job is the most common way to lose a critical point.

Example 1 (a polynomial). Find the critical points of \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+5\).

Narrate it: a polynomial is differentiable everywhere, so the only critical points are the zeros of \(f'\).

\[ f'(x)=3x^2-6x-9=3(x^2-2x-3)=3(x-3)(x+1) \]

Setting \(f'(x)=0\) gives \(x=3\) and \(x=-1\). Both are in the domain, so the critical points are \(x=-1\) and \(x=3\). The function values there are \(f(-1)=-1-3+9+5=10\) and \(f(3)=27-27-27+5=-22\), so the points on the graph are \((-1,10)\) and \((3,-22)\).

Example 2 (a rational function). Find the critical points of \(f(x)=\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}\).

The denominator is never zero, so \(f\) is defined and differentiable everywhere. By the Quotient Rule,

\[ f'(x)=\frac{(1)(x^2+1)-x(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2}=\frac{1-x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}=\frac{(1-x)(1+x)}{(x^2+1)^2} \]

The numerator is zero at \(x=1\) and \(x=-1\), and the denominator never vanishes, so these are the critical points. The values are \(f(1)=\tfrac12\) and \(f(-1)=-\tfrac12\).

Example 3 (where the derivative does not exist). Find the critical points of \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\).

Here \(f\) is defined for every real \(x\), but

\[ f'(x)=\frac23x^{-1/3}=\frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x}} \]

There is no \(x\) that makes this zero. But \(f'(0)\) does not exist, and \(0\) is in the domain of \(f\) (indeed \(f(0)=0\)). So \(x=0\) is a critical point, and it is the only one. The graph has a cusp there, the same shape you met in Lesson 9.02, where the tangent line turns vertical. And in this case the cusp is a local minimum: \(f(0)=0\) and every other value \(x^{2/3}\) is positive.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
A critical point must be in the domain of \(f\). Take \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\). Its derivative \(f'(x)=-\dfrac1{x^2}\) does not exist at \(x=0\), and \(f'(x)=0\) has no solution. Is \(0\) a critical point? No, because \(0\) is not in the domain of \(f\); the graph simply does not have a point there to suspect. This function has no critical points at all.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find all critical points of \(f(x)=x-2\sin x\) on the interval \((0,2\pi)\), and then give the function value at each. Commit to your answer, then reveal the check.
\(f'(x)=1-2\cos x\). Setting it to zero gives \(\cos x=\tfrac12\), so \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{3}\) and \(x=\tfrac{5\pi}{3}\), both inside \((0,2\pi)\). The derivative exists everywhere, so there are no others. The values are \(f(\tfrac{\pi}{3})=\tfrac{\pi}{3}-\sqrt3\approx-0.685\) and \(f(\tfrac{5\pi}{3})=\tfrac{5\pi}{3}+\sqrt3\approx6.969\). Which of these is a local minimum and which a local maximum? That is exactly the question Lesson 10.02 answers.
📚 PART 3 · SUSPECTS, NOT VERDICTS

Now the honest accounting. Fermat's Theorem says local extrema occur only at critical points. It never promised the reverse. So each critical point must be examined, and there are three outcomes: a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither.

The standard example of “neither” is \(f(x)=x^3\). Its derivative \(f'(x)=3x^2\) is zero at \(x=0\), so \(0\) is a critical point. But \(f(x)<f(0)\) for every \(x<0\) and \(f(x)>f(0)\) for every \(x>0\), so the point is neither a peak nor a valley. The graph passes through a flat moment on its way up. This is the right-hand graph at the top of the lesson.

Mathematicians have a word for the discipline this teaches: honesty, in the specific sense of not claiming more than the evidence shows. A critical point shows only that an extremum is possible there. To say more, you need more evidence.

For each function, decide what kind of point \(x=0\) is. Look at the domain first, then at the derivative.
1. \(f(x)=x^2\)
2. \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\)
3. \(f(x)=x^3\)
4. \(f(x)=|x|\)
5. \(f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}\)

Convince a classmate: a friend says, “The derivative is zero at \(x=0\) for \(f(x)=x^3\), so the function must have a peak or valley there.” In two sentences, explain which half of Fermat's Theorem your friend has reversed, and use \(x^3\) as your evidence.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
A computer algebra system can solve \(f'(x)=0\) in a fraction of a second, and that is a real convenience. But it is solving only the first of the two jobs. Ask it to solve \(f'(x)=0\) for \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\) and it will report no solution, while the true critical point, \(x=0\), comes from the second job. A tool answers the question you ask it. Knowing which question to ask, and which suspects exist outside the equation, is your part.

Looking ahead. You can now list the suspects. For \(f(x)=x^4-4x^3\), the derivative is \(f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2=4x^2(x-3)\), so the critical points are \(x=0\) and \(x=3\). One of them turns out to be a valley and the other to be neither, and you cannot tell which from \(f'(x)=0\) alone. In the next lesson we study the sign of \(f'\) to deliver the verdict.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A local extremum of a function can occur only at a critical point, meaning a point in the domain where the derivative is zero or does not exist. But the rule runs one way: a critical point is only a suspect. Some are peaks, some are valleys, and some, like \(x=0\) for \(x^3\), are neither. Lesson 10.02 shows how the sign of the derivative on either side of a critical point settles the question.
