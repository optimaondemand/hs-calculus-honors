📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two hills each climb the same hundred feet. On one the climb gets steeper with every step; on the other it eases off. How can a derivative tell them apart?
Lesson 8.01 introduced \(f''\) as the rate at which the slope is changing. Today that idea earns a name and a job. The name is concavity: whether a curve bends upward like a bowl or downward like a dome. The job is to read the shape of a graph from a formula. You will define concavity using \(f'\), test it with the sign of \(f''\), build sign charts for \(f''\), and then see a surprising payoff: the tangent-line estimates from Lesson 9.03 are always too low or always too high, and concavity tells you which. Lessons 11.02 and 11.03 build directly on what you learn here.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define concave up and concave down in terms of whether \(f'\) is increasing or decreasing, and explain what each looks like relative to the tangent lines.
2
I will be able to use the sign of \(f''\) to find the intervals of concavity of a function, building a sign chart and respecting any breaks in the domain.
3
I will be able to decide whether a tangent-line estimate from Lesson 9.03 is an overestimate or an underestimate by checking the concavity of the function.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, plus the second derivative from Lesson 8.01, the fact that \(f'>0\) forces \(f\) to increase (Lesson 8.04), and linear approximation (Lesson 9.03).
▶

📚 PART 1 · HOW A CURVE BENDS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You are walking along a path that climbs the whole way, and with every step the ground under you gets steeper. Which sentence describes what is happening to the slope?

Everything in this lesson starts from one definition, and the definition is built from the derivative you already know.

Concave up and concave down
Let \(f\) be differentiable on an open interval \(I\). The graph of \(f\) is concave up on \(I\) if \(f'\) is increasing on \(I\). It is concave down on \(I\) if \(f'\) is decreasing on \(I\).

Read it slowly: concavity is a statement about how the slope behaves, not about whether the function itself is going up or down. The picture shows the difference. On the left, the tangent lines rotate counterclockwise as you move right (the slopes are increasing), and the curve sits above every tangent line, like a bowl that holds water. On the right, the tangent lines rotate clockwise (the slopes are decreasing), and the curve sits below its tangents, like a dome that sheds water.

The definition would be hard to use if you had to decide whether \(f'\) is increasing by looking at pictures. Fortunately you already own the tool that settles it.

The Concavity Test
Suppose \(f''\) exists on an open interval \(I\). If \(f''(x)>0\) for every \(x\) in \(I\), then \(f\) is concave up on \(I\). If \(f''(x)\lt 0\) for every \(x\) in \(I\), then \(f\) is concave down on \(I\).

Why it is true. \(f''\) is the derivative of \(f'\). Lesson 8.04 proved that a function whose derivative is positive on an interval is increasing there. Apply that fact to the function \(f'\): if \(f''>0\) on \(I\), then \(f'\) is increasing on \(I\), which is the definition of concave up. If \(f''\lt 0\), the same argument shows \(f'\) is decreasing, so \(f\) is concave down. No new idea is needed, only the Mean Value Theorem working one level up.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Concavity and “going up or down” are different questions. A function can be falling and concave up, or rising and concave down. The first derivative answers the first question; the second derivative answers the second. Students who mix them up usually read \(f''>0\) as “the function is increasing.” It does not say that. It says the slope is increasing, and a slope can increase from \(-5\) to \(-1\) while the function is still falling.

The table below shows all four combinations. Each row has a real function so you can check the signs yourself.

Sign of \(f'\)Sign of \(f''\)What the slope doesThe curveExample
\(+\)\(+\)positive and growingrising, steepening\(e^x\) for all \(x\)
\(+\)\(-\)positive but shrinkingrising, flattening\(\ln x\) for \(x>0\)
\(-\)\(+\)negative but rising toward 0falling, flattening\(e^{-x}\) for all \(x\)
\(-\)\(-\)negative and getting more negativefalling, steepening\(-e^x\) for all \(x\)

Check one row: for \(f(x)=\ln x\), \(f'(x)=\dfrac1x>0\) and \(f''(x)=-\dfrac1{x^2}\lt 0\) when \(x>0\). The logarithm keeps rising, but ever more slowly, which is what “rising, flattening” means.

📚 PART 2 · SIGN CHARTS FOR THE SECOND DERIVATIVE

To find the intervals of concavity of a function, you do exactly what you did for \(f'\) in earlier lessons, only one derivative up. Three steps:

  1. Find \(f''(x)\) and simplify it, factoring if you can.
  2. Mark every point where \(f''(x)=0\) or \(f''(x)\) is undefined, and every point where \(f\) itself is not defined. These cut the number line into intervals.
  3. Test one number from each interval in \(f''\). The sign you get holds for the whole interval, because \(f''\) cannot change sign without passing through zero or a break.

Worked example 1: a quartic

Find the intervals of concavity of \(f(x)=x^4-4x^3\). Differentiate twice:

\[ f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2,\qquad f''(x)=12x^2-24x=12x(x-2). \]

\(f''\) is zero at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\), and a polynomial has no other breaks. Test one number in each of the three intervals:

IntervalTest point\(f''\) at the test pointConcavity
\((-\infty,0)\)\(x=-1\)\(12(-1)(-3)=36>0\)concave up
\((0,2)\)\(x=1\)\(12(1)(-1)=-12\lt 0\)concave down
\((2,\infty)\)\(x=3\)\(12(3)(1)=36>0\)concave up

So \(f\) is concave up on \((-\infty,0)\) and on \((2,\infty)\), and concave down on \((0,2)\). Notice that the concavity changes at both \(x=0\) and \(x=2\), the two places where \(f''\) is zero. The next lesson asks what that means for the graph.

Worked example 2: a break in the domain

Now \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\). Then \(f'(x)=-x^{-2}\) and \(f''(x)=2x^{-3}=\dfrac{2}{x^3}\). This \(f''\) is never zero, but it is undefined at \(x=0\), and so is \(f\). The break splits the line into two intervals: for \(x\lt 0\), \(x^3\lt 0\) so \(f''\lt 0\) and the graph is concave down; for \(x>0\), \(x^3>0\) so \(f''>0\) and the graph is concave up.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The sign of \(f''\) flips at \(x=0\) in this example, yet nothing special happens at that point of the graph, because there is no point there: the function is not defined at \(0\). In Lesson 11.02 you will see why a genuine change of concavity needs a real point on the curve, and why you should always report concavity on intervals, split at every break, instead of writing one sentence for the whole line.

🤖 AI AND GRAPHING TOOLS
A graphing tool will happily draw the bend of a curve, and an AI assistant will happily tell you the intervals of concavity. Neither will show you why the intervals are what they are, and a plot is easy to misread near a break or a flat stretch. You earn the answer by finding \(f''\) and testing its sign. Then use a tool, if you like, to check that the picture agrees. A tool that agrees with your reasoning is a confirmation; a tool that replaces your reasoning is a risk.
📚 PART 3 · CONCAVITY AND TANGENT-LINE ESTIMATES

Lesson 9.03 already used this idea: you approximated a function near \(x=a\) by its tangent line \(L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\) and used the sign of \(f''\) to predict whether the estimate runs high or low. Now that concavity has a definition, here is the full statement and the reason it is true.

Concavity and tangent-line estimates
If \(f\) is concave up near \(x=a\), the graph lies above its tangent line there, so \(L(x)\) underestimates \(f(x)\). If \(f\) is concave down near \(x=a\), the graph lies below its tangent line, so \(L(x)\) overestimates \(f(x)\).

Why. Look at the gap \(g(x)=f(x)-L(x)\). At \(x=a\), \(g(a)=f(a)-f(a)=0\). Its derivative is \(g'(x)=f'(x)-f'(a)\), so \(g'(a)=0\) as well. And \(g''(x)=f''(x)\), because \(L\) is a line. Suppose \(f''>0\) near \(a\). Then \(g'\) is increasing and equals \(0\) at \(a\), so \(g'\lt 0\) just left of \(a\) and \(g'>0\) just right of \(a\). That makes \(g\) fall to the value \(0\) and then rise, so \(g(x)\ge 0\) near \(a\), meaning \(f(x)\ge L(x)\). The concave-down case is the mirror image. The same sign-of-the-derivative reasoning is what you formalize in Lesson 10.02, Increasing, Decreasing, and the First Derivative Test.

Worked example: estimating a square root

Estimate \(\sqrt{4.1}\) with the tangent line to \(f(x)=\sqrt x\) at \(a=4\). Since \(f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\), \(f'(4)=\dfrac14\), so

\[ L(x)=2+\tfrac14(x-4),\qquad L(4.1)=2+\tfrac14(0.1)=2.025. \]

Now check the concavity. \(f''(x)=-\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}}\lt 0\) for every \(x>0\), so \(f\) is concave down and the tangent line should overestimate. The true value is \(\sqrt{4.1}\approx 2.02485\), and indeed \(2.025>2.02485\). You could have announced the direction of the error before computing a single decimal.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: use the tangent line to \(f(x)=\sqrt x\) at \(a=9\) to estimate \(\sqrt{9.2}\), and predict whether your estimate is too high or too low before you compute the true value. Then reveal the check.
\(f'(9)=\dfrac{1}{2\cdot3}=\dfrac16\), so \(L(9.2)=3+\dfrac16(0.2)\approx 3.0333\). Because \(f''(x)=-\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}}\lt 0\), the function is concave down and \(L\) overestimates. The true value is \(\sqrt{9.2}\approx 3.03315\), which is smaller than \(3.0333\). The prediction came from the sign of \(f''\) alone, with no decimals needed.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A graph is concave up when its slope is increasing and concave down when its slope is decreasing, and the Concavity Test turns that into a sign check on \(f''\). Build a sign chart for \(f''\), split at every point where \(f''\) is zero or undefined and at every break in the domain, and read the concavity off each interval. The sign of \(f''\) also tells you whether a tangent-line estimate runs high or low. In Lesson 11.02 you look at the places where the concavity actually changes, the inflection points, and use \(f''\) to test critical points.
