📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Every derivative rule is an integration rule read backward. The Chain Rule, read backward, is substitution.
By now you can integrate powers, sines, cosines, and exponentials, and you can use any antiderivative to evaluate a definite integral. What you cannot yet do is integrate a composition. The Chain Rule is the rule that builds compositions, so undoing it is the natural next step. This lesson teaches the single technique that handles that: pick an inside function \(u\), check that its derivative appears, convert everything, integrate, and convert back. You will also learn the honest edge of the technique, which matters as much as the technique itself.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize a chain-rule pattern and evaluate an indefinite integral by substitution, then verify the answer by differentiating.
2
I will be able to evaluate a definite integral by substitution, converting the limits to \(u\)-values.
3
I will be able to decide whether substitution applies to a given integral, and explain why when it does not.

📚 PART 1 · RUNNING THE CHAIN RULE BACKWARD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You want an antiderivative of \(2x(x^2+1)^5\). Expanding \((x^2+1)^5\) and multiplying out by \(2x\) is possible but long. Is there something about this expression that might make the job shorter?

Every differentiation rule you learned in Modules 5 through 7 has an integration twin, because an antiderivative is just a derivative read backward. The Power Rule and the Sum Rule reverse easily. The Chain Rule is the harder one to reverse, because after the Chain Rule has done its work, the original structure is hidden inside a product. Recall (Lesson 6.03) that

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[F(g(x))\bigr]=F'(g(x))\,g'(x). \]

Read it backward. If you see an integrand of the form \(f(g(x))\,g'(x)\), where \(f=F'\), then its antiderivative is \(F(g(x))\). That is the whole idea of integration by substitution.

Integration by Substitution (indefinite integrals)
If \(u=g(x)\) is differentiable and \(f\) is continuous on the range of \(g\), then \[ \int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx \;=\; \int f(u)\,du. \] Writing \(du=g'(x)\,dx\) is the shorthand that makes the bookkeeping work: replace everything in the integral by \(u\) and \(du\), integrate, then put \(g(x)\) back in for \(u\).

Worked example 1: the opening question

Find \(\displaystyle\int 2x\,(x^2+1)^5\,dx\).

Choose \(u\). Take \(u=x^2+1\), the inside of the power. Then \(du=2x\,dx\), which is exactly the rest of the integrand. Substitute. The integral becomes \(\int u^5\,du=\dfrac{u^6}{6}+C\). Back-substitute. The answer is

\[ \int 2x\,(x^2+1)^5\,dx=\frac{(x^2+1)^6}{6}+C. \]

Check by differentiating. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{(x^2+1)^6}{6}=\dfrac{6(x^2+1)^5\cdot 2x}{6}=2x(x^2+1)^5\). The check always works, and you should always do it; an antiderivative is the one thing in calculus you can verify completely.

Worked example 2: a constant factor is missing

Find \(\displaystyle\int x^2\sqrt{x^3+5}\,dx\). Take \(u=x^3+5\), so \(du=3x^2\,dx\). The integrand has \(x^2\,dx\) but not \(3x^2\,dx\). A constant can always be repaired: \(x^2\,dx=\tfrac13\,du\). Then

\[ \int x^2\sqrt{x^3+5}\,dx=\tfrac13\int u^{1/2}\,du=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac23\,u^{3/2}+C=\frac{2}{9}\,(x^3+5)^{3/2}+C. \]

▶  Predict first: what substitution handles \(\displaystyle\int \sin x\,\cos^2x\,dx\)? Then work it out.
Take \(u=\cos x\), so \(du=-\sin x\,dx\) and \(\sin x\,dx=-du\). Then \(\int\sin x\cos^2x\,dx=-\int u^2\,du=-\dfrac{u^3}{3}+C=-\dfrac{\cos^3x}{3}+C\). Check: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[-\tfrac13\cos^3x\bigr]=-\tfrac13\cdot 3\cos^2x\cdot(-\sin x)=\sin x\cos^2x\). The sign from \(du=-\sin x\,dx\) is where students slip; the derivative check catches it.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Leibniz invented the notation \(\int\) and \(du\) because he wanted the symbols themselves to do the bookkeeping. That is why \(du=g'(x)\,dx\) looks like treating \(du/dx\) as a fraction. It is not literally a fraction, but the Chain Rule guarantees that the manipulation is valid, which is why a notation chosen for convenience has lasted over three centuries.
📚 PART 2 · SUBSTITUTION WITH DEFINITE INTEGRALS

For a definite integral you have two honest options, and you must not mix them.

Option 1: find the antiderivative first. Work the indefinite integral in \(x\), return to \(x\), then plug in the original limits. Option 2: change the limits. When you switch to \(u\), also switch the limits to \(u\)-values, using \(u=g(x)\) at each end, and never go back to \(x\).

Substitution for definite integrals
\[ \int_a^b f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx \;=\; \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du. \] The new limits are \(u=g(a)\) at the bottom and \(u=g(b)\) at the top, even when \(g(b)\lt g(a)\).

Worked example 3: change the limits

Find \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 2x\,(x^2+1)^3\,dx\). Let \(u=x^2+1\), so \(du=2x\,dx\). When \(x=0\), \(u=1\); when \(x=1\), \(u=2\). Then

\[ \int_0^1 2x\,(x^2+1)^3\,dx=\int_1^2 u^3\,du=\Bigl[\frac{u^4}{4}\Bigr]_1^2=\frac{16-1}{4}=\frac{15}{4}. \]

Worked example 4: limits that run backward

Find \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\cos^2x\,dx\). Let \(u=\cos x\), so \(du=-\sin x\,dx\). At \(x=0\), \(u=1\); at \(x=\tfrac\pi2\), \(u=0\). So

\[ \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\cos^2x\,dx=-\int_1^0 u^2\,du=\int_0^1 u^2\,du=\frac13. \]

The new lower limit (1) is bigger than the new upper limit (0). That is fine and expected; flipping the limits absorbs the minus sign from \(du\).

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The classic error is a hybrid: substituting \(u\) but keeping the old limits. In Example 3, writing \(\int_0^1 u^3\,du\) gives \(\tfrac14\), not \(\tfrac{15}{4}\). The limits \(0\) and \(1\) are values of \(x\), and once the variable is \(u\) they must be values of \(u\). A good habit: write \(x=\) or \(u=\) next to each limit while you convert.

Worked example 5: when you must solve for x

Find \(\displaystyle\int_1^5 x\sqrt{x-1}\,dx\). There is no \(du\)-shaped factor in sight, but the inside \(x-1\) is so simple that you can still use it. Let \(u=x-1\); then \(du=dx\) and \(x=u+1\). At \(x=1\), \(u=0\); at \(x=5\), \(u=4\).

\[ \int_1^5 x\sqrt{x-1}\,dx=\int_0^4 (u+1)\,u^{1/2}\,du=\int_0^4\bigl(u^{3/2}+u^{1/2}\bigr)\,du=\Bigl[\tfrac25u^{5/2}+\tfrac23u^{3/2}\Bigr]_0^4. \]

At \(u=4\): \(\tfrac25\cdot32+\tfrac23\cdot8=\tfrac{64}{5}+\tfrac{16}{3}=\tfrac{192+80}{15}=\tfrac{272}{15}\). At \(u=0\) the value is 0, so the integral is \(\tfrac{272}{15}\).

📚 PART 3 · WHEN SUBSTITUTION APPLIES, AND WHEN IT DOES NOT

Substitution is the most powerful single technique in this course, and it deserves an honest account of where it stops. It works when the integrand contains a function of \(g(x)\) multiplied by \(g'(x)\), up to a constant factor. It does not work just because a composite function is present.

When it does not apply

Try \(\displaystyle\int (x^2+1)^5\,dx\), the same integrand as Example 1 but without the \(2x\). If you set \(u=x^2+1\), then \(du=2x\,dx\), and there is no \(x\) in the integrand to supply it. You cannot repair a missing variable factor; only a missing constant can be fixed by multiplying and dividing. The tempting mistake is to claim the answer is \(\dfrac{(x^2+1)^6}{6}+C\). Differentiate it and the Chain Rule gives \(2x(x^2+1)^5\), not \((x^2+1)^5\): the check fails, which is exactly what the check is for.

For that integral you would expand \((x^2+1)^5\) and integrate term by term. For others, such as \(\displaystyle\int e^{x^2}\,dx\), no amount of cleverness with the methods of this course will produce a formula. That function does have an antiderivative (the Fundamental Theorem guarantees it, because \(e^{x^2}\) is continuous), but it is not one you can write using the functions you know. Being able to say that clearly, instead of forcing a wrong answer, is part of mathematical honesty.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Substitution is the Chain Rule run backward: when an integrand is a function of \(g(x)\) times \(g'(x)\) (up to a constant), set \(u=g(x)\), integrate in \(u\), and substitute back, or change the limits and never return to \(x\). Always check by differentiating, and be honest when the pattern is absent: a missing variable factor cannot be repaired. With that, the toolkit of this module is complete. In Module 15 you will turn the question around. Given a rate and a starting value, you recover the function, and the first step in Lesson 15.01, Antiderivatives and Initial Conditions, is the one you already know how to take.
