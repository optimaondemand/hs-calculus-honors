📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You know how fast a rocket is climbing at every moment. How do you find where it is?
Everything since Module 4 ran in one direction: start with a function, produce its derivative. Real problems often arrive the other way around. A physician knows how fast a drug concentration is changing and wants the concentration. An engineer knows the acceleration of a car and wants its stopping distance. A town knows its population's growth rate and wants the population. To answer these you have to run differentiation backward, and the first thing you will discover is that the backward direction is not quite a function: it hands back a whole family of answers. Today you learn why, how to find the family, and how one extra piece of information, an initial condition, pins it down to a single curve.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find the general antiderivative of a function using the basic antiderivative table, and check it by differentiating.
2
I will be able to explain why every antiderivative family needs the constant \(+C\), using what the Mean Value Theorem told us about functions with equal derivatives.
3
I will be able to solve initial value problems, including second-order ones, and recover velocity and position from acceleration.

📚 PART 1 · THE FAMILY OF ANTIDERIVATIVES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
The derivative of some unknown function \(F\) is \(F'(x)=2x\). What do you think is the most you can say about \(F\)?

A function \(F\) is called an antiderivative of \(f\) on an interval if \(F'(x)=f(x)\) for every \(x\) in that interval. So \(x^2\) is an antiderivative of \(2x\), and so is \(x^2+5\), and so is \(x^2-\pi\). Is that the whole story, or could some strange antiderivative of \(2x\) look nothing like \(x^2\) plus a constant? Lesson 8.04 (Consequences of the Mean Value Theorem) settles it. There you proved: if two functions have equal derivatives on an interval, they differ by a constant there. Applied here, if \(F\) and \(G\) are both antiderivatives of \(f\), then \(F'=G'=f\), so \(F-G\) is constant. That gives the result we need:

The antiderivative family. If \(F\) is one antiderivative of \(f\) on an interval, then every antiderivative of \(f\) on that interval has the form \(F(x)+C\) for some constant \(C\). We write the whole family as the indefinite integral
\[ \int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+C. \]

The elongated S is read “the integral of” and the \(dx\) says the variable is \(x\). For now treat \(\int f(x)\,dx\) simply as shorthand for “the family of all antiderivatives of \(f\).” Lesson 13.03 (The Definite Integral) gave the same symbol a second meaning, an area-like limit of sums, and Lesson 14.02 (The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2) showed that the two meanings are bound together: that is exactly why the symbols are so similar. Here is the picture of the family for \(f(x)=2x\):

Each curve \(y=x^2+C\) has the same slope \(2x\) at any given \(x\), so the curves are vertical shifts of one another. The gold curve is the one that passes through \((1,3)\), and it is \(y=x^2+2\). The constant \(C\) selects one curve from the family.

The basic antiderivatives

Every derivative formula you learned in Modules 5–7 becomes an antiderivative formula when read backward. Each row below is verified by differentiating the right-hand side:

\(\displaystyle\int x^n\,dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\ \ (n\neq-1)\)\(\displaystyle\int \sec^2x\,dx=\tan x+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int \frac1x\,dx=\ln|x|+C\)\(\displaystyle\int \sec x\tan x\,dx=\sec x+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C\)\(\displaystyle\int \csc^2x\,dx=-\cot x+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int \sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C\)\(\displaystyle\int \csc x\cot x\,dx=-\csc x+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int \cos x\,dx=\sin x+C\)\(\displaystyle\int \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\,dx=\arctan x+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int k\,dx=kx+C\)\(\displaystyle\int \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=\arcsin x+C\)

Two properties let you combine them, because the derivative is linear (Lesson 5.01): \(\int [f(x)+g(x)]\,dx=\int f(x)\,dx+\int g(x)\,dx\) and \(\int k\,f(x)\,dx=k\int f(x)\,dx\). Antiderivatives are found term by term, with constants pulled out front.

Worked examples

Example 1. Find \(\int (6x^2-4x+5)\,dx\). Apply the power rule to each term:

\[ \int (6x^2-4x+5)\,dx = 6\cdot\frac{x^3}{3}-4\cdot\frac{x^2}{2}+5x+C = 2x^3-2x^2+5x+C. \]

Check by differentiating: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(2x^3-2x^2+5x+C)=6x^2-4x+5\). ✓

Example 2. Find \(\displaystyle\int \frac{x^3+2x^2-5}{x^2}\,dx\). There is no quotient rule for antiderivatives, so first split the fraction into powers of \(x\):

\[ \int\left(x+2-5x^{-2}\right)dx=\frac{x^2}{2}+2x-5\cdot\frac{x^{-1}}{-1}+C=\frac{x^2}{2}+2x+\frac5x+C. \]

Example 3. Find \(\displaystyle\int \left(3\sqrt{x}+\frac2x-4e^x+5\cos x\right)dx\). Rewrite \(3\sqrt{x}=3x^{1/2}\), then go term by term:

\[ 3\cdot\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}+2\ln|x|-4e^x+5\sin x+C=2x^{3/2}+2\ln|x|-4e^x+5\sin x+C. \]

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Two students can both be right and still write different answers. Differentiate \((x+1)^3/3\) and you get \((x+1)^2\), so it is an antiderivative of \((x+1)^2\). Expand first and you get \(\dfrac{x^3}{3}+x^2+x+C\), a different-looking answer. They differ by exactly \(\tfrac13\), which is just a different value of the constant. The family is the same family, written two ways. The honest test of any antiderivative is always the same: differentiate it.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not use the power rule on \(\int \dfrac1x\,dx\): the formula would divide by \(n+1=0\). The answer is \(\ln|x|+C\), and the absolute value matters, since \(\ln x\) is not defined for \(x\lt 0\) while \(\dfrac1x\) is. Also, there is no product rule for antiderivatives: \(\int x\cdot x^2\,dx\) is not \(\int x\,dx\cdot\int x^2\,dx\). Multiply out first (\(\int x^3\,dx=\tfrac{x^4}{4}+C\)), or wait for substitution in Lesson 14.04 (Integration by Substitution).

📚 PART 2 · INITIAL CONDITIONS PICK ONE CURVE

An antiderivative family describes infinitely many functions. In applications you usually know something extra: the value of the function at one particular input. That extra fact is an initial condition, and it selects exactly one curve from the family. A derivative equation together with an initial condition is called an initial value problem.

Worked example: one condition, one constant

Find the function \(f\) with \(f'(x)=3x^2-4\) and \(f(1)=5\).

Step 1: find the family. \(f(x)=x^3-4x+C\).

Step 2: use the condition. Substitute \(x=1\) and \(f(1)=5\): \(1-4+C=5\), so \(C=8\).

Step 3: state the particular solution, and check it. \(f(x)=x^3-4x+8\). Then \(f'(x)=3x^2-4\) ✓ and \(f(1)=1-4+8=5\) ✓.

The family is the general solution; the one curve through your point is the particular solution. Always check both: the derivative and the condition.

Two derivatives, two conditions

Sometimes you know a second derivative. Then you antidifferentiate twice, which introduces two constants, and you need two conditions to find them. Find \(f\) given \(f''(x)=6x+2\), \(f'(0)=1\), and \(f(0)=-3\).

\[ f'(x)=\int(6x+2)\,dx=3x^2+2x+C_1,\qquad f'(0)=1\ \Rightarrow\ C_1=1. \]

\[ f(x)=\int(3x^2+2x+1)\,dx=x^3+x^2+x+C_2,\qquad f(0)=-3\ \Rightarrow\ C_2=-3. \]

So \(f(x)=x^3+x^2+x-3\). Notice the order of the work: use the condition on \(f'\) before integrating again, so that each constant is determined one at a time.

📚 PART 3 · RECOVERING MOTION

The most important initial value problems in this course are the ones from motion. In Lessons 8.02 and 12.03 you started from a position function \(s(t)\) and differentiated to get velocity and acceleration. Now run the chain backward:

\[ a(t)\ \xrightarrow{\ \int\ }\ v(t)\ \xrightarrow{\ \int\ }\ s(t), \]

with one constant at each step, and an initial velocity and initial position to fix them. In Lesson 8.02 you were handed the ball's position, \(s(t)=-16t^2+64t+80\). Where could that formula have come from? Rebuild it from physics alone: gravity gives acceleration \(a(t)=-32\) ft/s², the ball is thrown upward at \(v(0)=64\) ft/s from a platform \(s(0)=80\) ft high.

\[ v(t)=\int(-32)\,dt=-32t+C_1,\quad v(0)=64\Rightarrow C_1=64,\quad v(t)=-32t+64. \]

\[ s(t)=\int(-32t+64)\,dt=-16t^2+64t+C_2,\quad s(0)=80\Rightarrow C_2=80. \]

That is exactly \(s(t)=-16t^2+64t+80\), the formula from Lesson 8.02, now derived from the acceleration and two initial conditions instead of being handed to you.

A braking car

A car travels at \(25\) m/s when the driver brakes with a constant acceleration of \(-10\) m/s². How far does it travel before stopping? Set \(s(0)=0\) at the moment the brakes are applied. Then \(v(t)=-10t+25\) and \(s(t)=-5t^2+25t\). The car stops when \(v(t)=0\), at \(t=2.5\) seconds, and by then it has travelled \(s(2.5)=-5(6.25)+25(2.5)=31.25\) meters.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
A graphing tool or computer algebra system will happily report \(\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\ln(x)\), with no constant and no absolute value, and some tools drop \(+C\) entirely. The tool is returning one antiderivative, not the family. You are the one who knows that the answer needs \(+C\) and that an initial condition must fix it. Use technology to check your antiderivative by differentiating, never to skip the reasoning that tells you what to ask.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: a particle moves on a line with velocity \(v(t)=3t^2-12t+9\) m/s and starts at position \(s(0)=2\) m. Find \(s(t)\), then the particle's position at \(t=2\) and \(t=4\). Then reveal the check.
\(s(t)=\int(3t^2-12t+9)\,dt=t^3-6t^2+9t+C\). Using \(s(0)=2\) gives \(C=2\), so \(s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t+2\). Then \(s(2)=8-24+18+2=4\) and \(s(4)=64-96+36+2=6\). Notice the velocity \(3(t-1)(t-3)\) is negative between \(t=1\) and \(t=3\), so the particle goes backward during that stretch; its position at \(t=2\) (4 m) is below its position at \(t=1\) (6 m). The same velocity function with a different starting position would give the same motion shifted by a constant.

Next lesson takes the idea one step further. So far the equation for the unknown function was always of the form \(f'(x)=\) a formula in \(x\) alone. What if the rate of change depends on the function's own value, as in a population whose growth rate depends on how large it already is? That is a differential equation, and a picture called a slope field lets you see its solutions before you can find a single formula.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Every function with an antiderivative has a whole family of them, \(F(x)+C\), because functions with equal derivatives differ only by a constant (Lesson 8.04). The constant is not a loose end; it is information you do not yet have. An initial condition supplies it and selects a single particular solution, and the check is always two-part: differentiate your answer, then test the condition. Lesson 15.02 (Differential Equations and Slope Fields) lets the rate of change depend on the unknown function itself, and shows how to see the solutions as curves before you can write them as formulas.
