📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The number you watched a secant slope approach in the last lesson finally gets a name.
In Lesson 4.01 you shrank \(h\) toward 0 and watched a secant slope settle on an exact number. That number is called the derivative of \(f\) at \(a\), written \(f'(a)\), and it is defined as a limit: the limit of the difference quotient as \(h\) approaches 0. Today you will use this precise definition to compute \(f'(a)\) directly, without a table, and use it to write the equation of the tangent line at that point.

📚 PART 1 · THE DEFINITION

The derivative at a point

The derivative of \(f\) at \(a\), written \(f'(a)\), is the instantaneous rate of change of \(f\) at \(x = a\):

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \]

An equivalent form replaces \(a + h\) with \(x\), so that \(h = x - a\) and \(h \to 0\) becomes \(x \to a\):

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \]

Geometrically, \(f'(a)\) is the slope of the tangent line to the curve \(y = f(x)\) at the point \((a, f(a))\). Once you have \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\), the tangent line is just point-slope form:

\[ y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a) \]

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Newton wrote this quantity with a dot over the variable, calling it a fluxion. Leibniz wrote it as \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), a ratio of infinitely small changes. Neither notation used a prime symbol. That came later, from the French mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who introduced \(f'(x)\) in 1797 as a cleaner way to write the same idea without leaning on infinitesimals at all. All three notations survive today and mean exactly the same thing.
📚 PART 2 · WORKED EXAMPLE

Compute \(f'(2)\) for \(f(x) = x^2\) and find the tangent line

This is the same function and point from Lesson 4.01. Now we compute the limit directly from the definition instead of building a table.

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(2) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(2+h) - f(2)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(2+h)^2 - 4}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{4 + 4h + h^2 - 4}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{4h + h^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} (4 + h) \\ &= 4 \end{aligned} \]

So \(f'(2) = 4\), matching the value the shrinking secant slopes approached in Lesson 4.01. Since \(f(2) = 4\), the tangent line at \(x = 2\) is

\[ y - 4 = 4(x - 2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y = 4x - 4 \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The tangent-line equation needs both \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\): one gives a point on the line, the other gives the line's slope. Forgetting to compute \(f(a)\) separately, or reusing \(f'(a)\) as if it were the point, is a common shortcut that produces the wrong line.
💬 EXPLAIN IT
In your notebook, explain to a classmate why the tangent-line formula \(y - f(a) = f'(a)(x-a)\) needs two separate pieces of information about the curve at \(x = a\), and name what each piece supplies.
📚 PART 3 · YOUR TURN

Try a new function and a new point before checking your work.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: use the definition to find \(f'(3)\) for \(f(x) = x^2 + 1\), then write the tangent line at \(x = 3\). Then reveal the check.
\(f(3) = 10\). \(f(3+h) = (3+h)^2 + 1 = 9 + 6h + h^2 + 1 = 10 + 6h + h^2\). So \(f(3+h) - f(3) = 6h + h^2\), and \(\dfrac{f(3+h)-f(3)}{h} = 6 + h \to 6\) as \(h \to 0\). So \(f'(3) = 6\). The tangent line is \(y - 10 = 6(x - 3)\), which simplifies to \(y = 6x - 8\).

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

\(f'(a)\) is a single number: the exact slope of the tangent line at \(x = a\), defined as the limit of a difference quotient. Compute it by simplifying the algebra first and letting \(h \to 0\) only at the end. In the next lesson you will stop fixing \(a\) and instead let it vary, turning \(f'(a)\) into a new function, \(f'(x)\), that reports the slope everywhere at once.
