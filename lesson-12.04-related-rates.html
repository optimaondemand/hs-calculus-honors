📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A balloon's radius grows at a steady rate. Its volume does not. Calculus is how you find the second rate from the first.
Many things you can measure change at once, and they are tied together by formulas: the radius, area, and volume of a ripple or balloon; the height and distance of a sliding ladder; the depth and volume of water in a tank. When quantities are tied by an equation, their rates of change are tied by the same equation differentiated with respect to time. Today you learn the five-step method for related rates, built from the Chain Rule and implicit differentiation you already have. You will meet its three most important habits: differentiate before you substitute, eliminate extra variables first, and say in words what your answer and its sign mean. You will also see where an answer is a statement about a model, not about the world.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to translate a changing-quantities situation into an equation among variables that are functions of time, using geometry or similar triangles to eliminate extra variables.
2
I will be able to differentiate the relationship with respect to \(t\) using the Chain Rule, substitute the values for the instant only afterward, and report the answer with units and the meaning of its sign.
3
I will be able to solve standard related-rates problems (an expanding circle, a sliding ladder, a filling cone, a moving shadow) and state where a model stops being believable.

📚 PART 1 · RATES THAT ARE TIED TOGETHER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A spherical balloon is inflated so that its radius grows at a steady 2 cm per second. Is its volume growing at a steady rate too?

In Lessons 12.01 and 12.02 you asked for the best value of a quantity. Today a quantity is not sought, it is changing, and so are the others tied to it. A balloon's radius, surface, and volume all change at once, and they are linked by formulas. Because they are linked, their rates are linked. Finding one rate from another is the problem of related rates.

The mathematics is something you already know. Lesson 6.03 gave you the Chain Rule, and Lesson 7.01 used it to differentiate an equation in which \(y\) is secretly a function of \(x\). Here every variable is a function of time \(t\), so differentiating with respect to \(t\) puts a factor like \(\dfrac{dr}{dt}\) on every term where \(r\) appears.

The five-step strategy for related rates
1. Draw and name. Sketch the situation. Give a letter to every quantity that changes, and note which rates are given and which one is wanted.
2. Relate. Write an equation connecting the variables that is true at every moment (a geometry fact, a formula, similar triangles). If there are too many variables, use a second fact to eliminate one.
3. Differentiate with respect to \(t\). Use the Chain Rule on every variable quantity.
4. Substitute last. Only now put in the values at the instant in question.
5. Solve and state the answer with units and a sentence in the context of the problem, including the meaning of the sign.

Example 1: a ripple. A stone is dropped into a still pond, and a circular ripple spreads so that its radius increases at 2 ft/s. How fast is the enclosed area increasing when the radius is 10 ft?

Draw and name: radius \(r\) (feet), area \(A\) (square feet), both functions of \(t\). Given: \(\dfrac{dr}{dt}=2\) ft/s. Wanted: \(\dfrac{dA}{dt}\) at \(r=10\). Relate: \(A=\pi r^2\), true at every moment. Differentiate:

\[ \frac{dA}{dt}=2\pi r\,\frac{dr}{dt}. \]

Substitute last: \(\dfrac{dA}{dt}=2\pi(10)(2)=40\pi\approx125.7\) ft\(^2\)/s. At the moment the ripple is 10 ft across from its center, the area it encloses is growing at about 126 square feet per second.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The most common error is to substitute \(r=10\) before differentiating. Then \(A=\pi(10)^2=100\pi\) is a constant, and its derivative is \(0\): an absurd answer for a ripple that is plainly spreading. The equation \(A=\pi r^2\) is true at every moment only while \(r\) is still a variable. Substituting a number freezes the picture at one instant and throws away all the change you are trying to measure. Always differentiate first, then substitute.

Convince a classmate: in Example 1, if the radius grows at a steady 2 ft/s, why does the area grow faster when the ripple is large? Point to the factor in \(\dfrac{dA}{dt}=2\pi r\,\dfrac{dr}{dt}\) that explains it.

📚 PART 2 · THE SLIDING LADDER AND THE MEANING OF THE SIGN

Example 2: the sliding ladder. A 13-foot ladder leans against a vertical wall. The base is pulled away from the wall at 2 ft/s. How fast is the top sliding down the wall at the moment the base is 5 ft from the wall?

Draw and name: \(x\) is the distance from the wall to the base, \(y\) the height of the top, both in feet and both changing with \(t\). Given: \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=2\). Wanted: \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\) when \(x=5\). Relate: the ladder, wall, and floor form a right triangle with fixed hypotenuse 13, at every moment:

\[ x^2+y^2=169. \]

Differentiate with respect to \(t\): \(2x\dfrac{dx}{dt}+2y\dfrac{dy}{dt}=0\), so

\[ \frac{dy}{dt}=-\frac{x}{y}\,\frac{dx}{dt}. \]

Substitute last. When \(x=5\), the equation gives \(y=\sqrt{169-25}=12\). Then \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=-\dfrac{5}{12}(2)=-\dfrac56\) ft/s. The negative sign means \(y\) is decreasing: at that moment the top of the ladder slides down the wall at \(\tfrac56\approx0.83\) ft/s.

It gets faster. The same formula at \(x=12\) (then \(y=5\)) gives \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=-\dfrac{12}{5}(2)=-4.8\) ft/s. The base has been moving at a steady 2 ft/s, but the top is sliding almost six times faster than before, because the ratio \(x/y\) keeps growing.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
Look at what the formula says as the ladder nears the floor. As \(x\to13\), \(y\to0\), and \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=-\dfrac xy\cdot2\) grows without bound, a sliding speed beyond any speed limit, which no real ladder can reach. The mathematics is right for the model; the model is wrong for a ladder close to the floor, because a real ladder would leave the wall and fall. A related-rates answer is a statement about the model, and an honest solver says where the model stops being believable.

📚 PART 3 · ELIMINATE FIRST, DIFFERENTIATE SECOND

Sometimes the first relation you write has too many variables. The remedy is a second fact that eliminates one, and it must be done before you differentiate.

Example 3: filling a cone. Water pours into an inverted conical tank (point down) at 10 ft\(^3\)/min. The tank is 12 ft tall with a top radius of 6 ft. How fast is the water level rising when the water is 8 ft deep?

Draw and name: water depth \(h\) and surface radius \(r\), both changing. Given: \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}=10\). Wanted: \(\dfrac{dh}{dt}\) when \(h=8\). Relate: the volume of a cone is \(V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h\), but this involves two changing variables. The water's cross-section is similar to the tank's, so \(\dfrac rh=\dfrac6{12}\), that is \(r=\dfrac h2\) at every moment. Eliminate \(r\) first:

\[ V=\frac13\pi\Big(\frac h2\Big)^2h=\frac{\pi}{12}h^3. \]

Differentiate with respect to \(t\): \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}=\dfrac{\pi}{4}h^2\,\dfrac{dh}{dt}\). Substitute last: \(10=\dfrac{\pi}{4}(8)^2\dfrac{dh}{dt}=16\pi\,\dfrac{dh}{dt}\), so

\[ \frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{10}{16\pi}=\frac{5}{8\pi}\approx0.199\ \text{ft/min}. \]

The level rises about 0.2 ft per minute, about 2.4 inches a minute. Check the units: \(\text{ft}^3/\text{min}\) divided by \(\text{ft}^2\) leaves ft/min, as a rate of depth should be. And the sign makes sense: positive, because the water is rising.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
If you give an AI tool a related-rates problem, test it with the strategy above. Does it name the variables? Does it eliminate extra variables before differentiating? Does it substitute only at the end, and state the units and the meaning of the sign? Tools sometimes substitute early and still produce a confident-looking number. The five steps are how you catch that, and the reasoning stays yours.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: a person 6 ft tall walks away from a lamppost 18 ft high at 4 ft/s. How fast is the tip of the shadow moving away from the post, and how fast is the length of the shadow growing? Then reveal the check.
Let \(x\) be the person's distance from the post and \(s\) the shadow's length. Similar triangles (lamp and person, both measured to the tip of the shadow): \(\dfrac{18}{x+s}=\dfrac6s\), so \(18s=6x+6s\), \(12s=6x\), \(s=\dfrac x2\). The tip is at distance \(x+s=\dfrac{3x}{2}\) from the post, so its speed is \(\dfrac32\cdot4=6\) ft/s. The shadow's length grows at \(\dfrac{ds}{dt}=\dfrac12\cdot4=2\) ft/s. Notice that neither rate depends on how far the person has walked: the relation is linear, so the rates are constant.
