📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Guarantees come with fine print.
Today's theorem promises something that sounds almost too convenient: a continuous function on a closed interval always has a highest point and a lowest point. Read that sentence again, though, and notice the two conditions buried inside it. Drop either one, closed or continuous, and the guarantee can quietly disappear. Being precise about when a guarantee applies, and honest about when it does not, is the whole point of today's lesson, and it closes out everything Unit 1 has been building toward.

📚 PART 1 · THE THEOREM AND ITS FINE PRINT

The Extreme Value Theorem

If \(f\) is continuous on a closed, bounded interval \([a,b]\), then \(f\) attains an absolute maximum value and an absolute minimum value on \([a,b]\): there exist numbers \(c\) and \(d\) in \([a,b]\) such that \(f(c) \ge f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \([a,b]\), and \(f(d) \le f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \([a,b]\).

Notice the theorem promises the max and min are actually reached, at some specific point in the interval, not merely approached. Two hypotheses make that promise good: the interval must be closed, and the function must be continuous on it. Remove either one, and the guarantee can fail.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The Extreme Value Theorem is often credited to Karl Weierstrass's rigorous work in the mid-1800s, part of the same broader nineteenth-century project, alongside Cauchy and Bolzano, that replaced calculus's informal picture-drawing with airtight arguments built from limits. The theorem itself only guarantees that a maximum and minimum exist; finding them by hand will wait until you meet the derivative, later this quarter.
📚 PART 2 · WHY BOTH CONDITIONS MATTER

Counterexample 1: continuous, but not closed

Let \(f(x) = x\) on the open interval \((0,1)\). This function is continuous everywhere on \((0,1)\), but the interval is open: it never includes 0 or 1. As \(x\) gets closer and closer to 1, \(f(x)\) gets closer and closer to 1, but \(x=1\) is never actually in the interval, so \(f\) never reaches a value of 1. There is no largest value \(f\) attains, and by the same reasoning, no smallest value either. Dropping "closed" is enough, by itself, to break the guarantee.

Counterexample 2: closed, but not continuous

Let \( g(x) = \begin{cases} x & 0 \le x < 1 \\ 0 & x = 1 \end{cases} \) on the closed interval \([0,1]\). The interval is closed, but \(g\) has a jump discontinuity at \(x=1\): as \(x \to 1^-\), \(g(x) \to 1\), yet \(g(1) = 0\). Values of \(g\) get arbitrarily close to 1 without ever reaching it (since \(g(x) < 1\) for every \(x\) in \([0,1)\), and \(g(1)=0\)), so \(g\) has no absolute maximum on \([0,1]\), even though the interval itself is closed. Dropping continuity is enough, by itself, to break the guarantee too.

⚠️ WATCH OUT
Both hypotheses are required together. A function can be continuous on an interval that fails to be closed, or defined on a closed interval while failing to be continuous, and either gap alone is enough to void the guarantee. State both checks explicitly before you claim a maximum or minimum must exist.
Convince a classmate: using one of the two counterexamples above, explain why it does not violate the Extreme Value Theorem, even though it has no maximum or minimum.

📚 PART 4 · HOW UNIT 1 FITS TOGETHER

Everything in Unit 1 has been building in one direction. Module 1 defined the limit: what a function approaches near a point, whether or not it is defined there. Module 2 extended that idea to the edges of a function's domain and to points where a function's behavior runs off to infinity. Module 3 used the limit to define continuity precisely, three conditions at a time, and then showed that continuity is not just tidy bookkeeping: it is the hypothesis that unlocks two genuine theorems, the Intermediate Value Theorem and the Extreme Value Theorem, each of which guarantees something exists (a root, a maximum, a minimum) without having to construct it.

Next quarter opens with a new question built on all of this: not just what a function approaches, but how fast it is changing at a single instant. That question will define the derivative, and it will turn out to depend, at its very foundation, on the limit you have been studying all along.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A guarantee is only as strong as its hypotheses. The Extreme Value Theorem promises a maximum and a minimum, but only when the interval is closed and the function is continuous on it; drop either condition and the promise can fail. Naming which hypothesis is missing, rather than assuming the guarantee anyway, is the same intellectual honesty that has run through this whole module. Module 4 asks a new question of these continuous functions: how fast are they changing at a single instant?
