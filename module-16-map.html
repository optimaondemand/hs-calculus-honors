Area, Average Value, and Volume
What quantities can an integral add up, and how do you set one up from a picture?
📖 WHAT THIS MODULE IS ABOUT
The integral was born as an area. It can measure far more than that. Lesson 16.01 starts where the year's integration began, with the region between two curves: integrate the top curve minus the bottom curve, find the limits where the curves meet, and split the region where the top curve changes. Lesson 16.02 turns an integral into an average, the height of the rectangle that has the same area as the region under a curve, and connects it back to the Mean Value Theorem you proved in Lesson 8.03. Lesson 16.03 builds volumes from slices whose cross-sectional area you know. Lesson 16.04 spins a region around an axis and finds the volume by disks and washers. Every problem in the module asks for the same habit: draw the picture, choose one thin slice, say what it contributes, then add up the slices. This module closes the content of the year; Week 34 is Semester 2 Finals.

💡 HOW TO SUCCEED THIS MODULE
Before you write an integral, draw the region and one typical slice, and label what the slice measures: a thin strip between two curves, a thin slab with a known face, a thin disk or washer. Then ask three questions. Where does the slice start and stop? What is its area or thickness? Is the slice measured in x or in y? Almost every mistake in this module is a setup mistake, not an antiderivative mistake, so check each radius against the axis of rotation and say in words what you are adding up. Be honest about which setups you are sure of. Real understanding here looks like knowing why the integral is the right tool, not merely getting the number.
