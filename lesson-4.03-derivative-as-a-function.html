📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Stop asking for the slope at one point. Ask for the slope everywhere at once.
So far you have fixed a single point \(a\) and computed the one number \(f'(a)\). Today you let \(a\) vary. Replacing the fixed point with the variable \(x\) turns the derivative from a number into a new function, \(f'(x)\), that reports the slope of the tangent line at every point in the domain. You will compute \(f'(x)\) from the definition, exactly as before, and read the three common ways it gets written.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain how \(f'(a)\), a number at one point, generalizes to \(f'(x)\), a function defined at every point.
2
I will be able to compute \(f'(x)\) from the definition for a polynomial and for a simple root function.
3
I will be able to read and use the notations \(f'(x)\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\), and match the sign of \(f'(x)\) to the behavior of the graph of \(f\).
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, for the difference-quotient algebra with a variable point.
▶
Your rationalizing technique from Module 1, for the root example.
📚 PART 1 · FROM A POINT TO A FUNCTION

Worked example: \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = x^2\)

We repeat the definition from Lesson 4.02, but now with the variable point \(x\) in place of the fixed point \(a = 2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) \\ &= 2x \end{aligned} \]

So \(f'(x) = 2x\) for every \(x\). This single formula reports the slope at any point at once: at \(x = 2\), \(f'(2) = 2(2) = 4\), matching Lessons 4.01 and 4.02 exactly.

💡 NOTICE
In Lesson 4.02 you found \(f'(3) = 6\) for \(f(x) = x^2 + 1\). Using \(f'(x) = 2x\) here gives \(f'(3) = 2(3) = 6\), the same answer, even though that function had a different constant term. Adding 1 to \(x^2\) shifted the whole graph up but did not tilt it, so it did not change the slope anywhere. A constant term always disappears from the derivative.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The very notation \(f(x)\), which lets us write "the function \(f\) evaluated at \(x\)" so compactly, was introduced by the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler in 1734 and popularized in his textbooks over the following decades. Before Euler's notation existed, mathematicians had no clean way to talk about "a function" as its own kind of object, which is exactly the leap this lesson asks you to make with the derivative.
📚 PART 2 · A SIMPLE ROOT FUNCTION

Now try \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\). The difference quotient here has the 0/0 form after substitution, so we rationalize the numerator, exactly as you did with algebraic limits in Module 1.

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x+h} - \sqrt{x}}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x+h} - \sqrt{x}}{h} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h) - x}{h\left(\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}\right)} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h}{h\left(\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}\right)} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}} \\ &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \end{aligned} \]

So \(f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\) for \(x > 0\). Notice the notation choices you will meet throughout the rest of this course: \(f'(x)\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) (if \(y = f(x)\)), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)\big]\) all name this same derivative function.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
\(f'(x)\) and \(f(x)\) are different functions with different graphs. \(f'(x) = 2x\) is a straight line; \(f(x) = x^2\) is a parabola. Do not read \(f'(3) = 6\) as if it were a height on the original curve. It is a slope, read off a separate graph.
📚 PART 3 · READING f AND f' TOGETHER

Stack the graph of \(f\) above the graph of \(f'\) and line up their \(x\)-axes. Where \(f\) is rising, \(f'\) is positive. Where \(f\) has a horizontal tangent (a peak or a valley), \(f' = 0\). Where \(f\) is falling, \(f'\) is negative.

Top: \(f\) falls, bottms out, then rises. Bottom: \(f'\) is negative, crosses zero at the bottom of the dip, then turns positive, right beneath it.

For each description of the graph of \(f\) at \(x = a\), choose what \(f'(a)\) must be.
1. The graph of f is rising steeply at x = a.
2. The graph of f has a horizontal tangent line at a peak at x = a.
3. The graph of f is falling gently at x = a.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Letting the fixed point \(a\) become a variable \(x\) turns one slope into a whole new function, \(f'(x)\), that reports every slope of \(f\) at once. Its sign tells you whether \(f\) is rising, falling, or momentarily flat. In the next lesson you will ask a harder question: are there places where this new function fails to exist at all?
