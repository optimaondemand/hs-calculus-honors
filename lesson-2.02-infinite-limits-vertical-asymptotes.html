📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Some approaches do not settle on a number. They shoot away.
Yesterday x ran out toward infinity while f(x) settled down. Today the roles trade places: x approaches an ordinary, finite point, but f(x) itself grows without bound as it gets there. Both situations borrow the symbol \(\infty\), and it is worth being exact about what that symbol is doing in each case. Here it describes the height of the graph, not the input, near a place where the function refuses to level off.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT AN INFINITE LIMIT MEANS

We write \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \infty \) when f(x) grows arbitrarily large as x approaches a, and \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = -\infty \) when f(x) becomes arbitrarily large in the negative direction. Strictly speaking, in neither case does the limit exist as a real number, since f(x) never settles on one. We use the \(\infty\) notation anyway because it names a single, predictable behavior: the outputs are not merely failing to settle, they are heading in one clear, unbounded direction.

That predictability is exactly what can be missing. If the one-sided limits at a disagree, one side heading to \(+\infty\) and the other to \(-\infty\), there is no single unbounded direction to name, and we simply say the two-sided limit does not exist. The line \(x = a\) is a vertical asymptote of the graph whenever at least one of the one-sided limits there is infinite, whether or not the two sides agree.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \infty \) can look like the limit exists and equals some number called infinity. It does not. The equation is shorthand for "grows without bound," not a value the function reaches. When the two one-sided behaviors disagree in direction, do not write an infinite two-sided limit at all; write that the limit does not exist.
📚 PART 2 · SIGN ANALYSIS NEAR A VERTICAL ASYMPTOTE

To classify an infinite limit, test a point very close to a on each side and track the sign of the result. A denominator that is a small positive number close to zero sends a positive fraction toward \(+\infty\); a small negative denominator sends it toward \(-\infty\).

\(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x-3}\): the two sides disagree, so the two-sided limit does not exist.
\(g(x)=\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}\): both sides agree, so \(\lim_{x\to1} g(x) = \infty\).

Worked example: sides that disagree

Let \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x-3}\). As \(x\to3^{-}\), test \(x=2.999\): the denominator \(x-3=-0.001\) is a small negative number, so \(f(x)\) is a large negative number. As \(x\to3^{+}\), test \(x=3.001\): the denominator is a small positive number, so \(f(x)\) is a large positive number. So

\[ \lim_{x\to3^{-}} \frac{1}{x-3} = -\infty \qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to3^{+}} \frac{1}{x-3} = \infty \]

The one-sided limits disagree, so \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to3} f(x) \) does not exist. Even so, \(x=3\) is a vertical asymptote, because a vertical asymptote only requires that one side be infinite, not that the two sides agree.

Worked example: sides that agree

Let \(g(x) = \dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}\). Because the denominator is squared, it is positive on both sides of \(x=1\), so \(g(x)\) is positive and growing on both sides. Both one-sided limits equal \(+\infty\), and here we may write the agreed two-sided statement

\[ \lim_{x\to1} \frac{1}{(x-1)^2} = \infty \]

📚 PART 3 · A CAUTION: NOT EVERY ZERO DENOMINATOR IS INFINITE

A denominator of zero at \(x=a\) is not, by itself, proof that the limit is infinite. If the numerator is also zero there, the factor causing the zero may cancel, leaving a perfectly finite limit. Consider \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to4} \dfrac{x-4}{x^2-16} \). For \(x\ne4\), \( \dfrac{x-4}{x^2-16} = \dfrac{x-4}{(x-4)(x+4)} = \dfrac{1}{x+4} \), so the limit is \(\dfrac{1}{8}\), a finite number. There is no vertical asymptote at \(x=4\); the function simply has a removable hole there, exactly the situation you studied with 0/0 in Module 1.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

An infinite limit names a single, predictable, unbounded direction, not a value the function reaches, and a vertical asymptote only needs one side to behave that way. When the two sides disagree, the honest statement is that the limit does not exist, not that it equals some infinite quantity.
