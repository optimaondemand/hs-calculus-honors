📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If you know the area of every slice of a solid, you can find the volume of the whole thing, even when the solid has no name.
Lessons 16.01 and 16.02 used the integral to measure area and average value. Today the integral measures something three-dimensional. The idea is Cavalieri's slicing principle: cut a solid into thin slabs, find the area of the face of each slab, and add up the volumes. The calculus is short, since it is the Riemann sum of Lesson 13.03 once more. The real skill is reading a base region and a cross-section description, and building the function \(A(x)\) from them. By the end you will have found volumes of solids made of squares, semicircles, and triangles standing on curved bases.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why \(V=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx\) gives the volume of a solid with known cross-sections, using the idea of thin slabs.
2
I will be able to find the slice length \(s\) across a base region and write \(A\) for squares, semicircles, and triangles.
3
I will be able to set up and evaluate a volume integral with respect to either \(x\) or \(y\), and check the setup with a special case.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, and room to sketch a base region and a typical slice.
▶

📚 PART 1 · SLICING A SOLID INTO KNOWN CROSS-SECTIONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A loaf of bread is sliced into thin, equal-thickness slices, and you know the area of the face of every slice. Which quantity would you add up to get the volume of the loaf?

Lesson 16.01 turned an area into an integral by slicing a region into thin vertical strips, approximating each strip by a rectangle, and letting the number of strips grow without bound, exactly the Riemann-sum story of Lessons 13.01 through 13.03. Today we carry that same idea one dimension up. Instead of cutting a flat region into strips, we cut a solid into thin slabs.

Suppose a solid lies between \(x=a\) and \(x=b\), and suppose that for each \(x\) the slice cut by a plane perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis has area \(A(x)\). A slab of thickness \(\Delta x\) near \(x\) has volume about \(A(x)\,\Delta x\). Add the slabs, let \(\Delta x\to 0\), and you have a Riemann sum turning into an integral:

\[ V=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx. \]

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
This idea is older than calculus. In 1635 Bonaventura Cavalieri, a student of Galileo, published his principle: if two solids have equal cross-sectional area at every height, they have equal volume. Think of two stacks of coins, one straight and one leaning. The shape differs, but the stack holds the same amount of silver. The integral \(\int A(x)\,dx\) is Cavalieri's principle made exact. Archimedes had used the same slicing intuition nearly two thousand years earlier to find the volume of a sphere.

Notice what the formula asks of you. The hard part is never the integral. It is finding \(A(x)\) from a picture. That is a modeling skill, and it is worth learning as a routine:

1. Sketch the base region and a typical slice, and decide which axis the slices are perpendicular to. 2. Find the length \(s\) of the slice across the base, as a function of the variable. 3. Write the area of the cross-section in terms of \(s\). 4. Integrate between the limits where the slices begin and end.

The base region lies in the \(xy\)-plane. Each blue line is the base of one slab; the gold segment, length \(s=x^2\), is the side of a square standing straight up out of the plane.

Worked example 1: squares

The base of a solid is the region between \(y=x^2\) and the \(x\)-axis, for \(0\le x\le 2\). Cross-sections perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis are squares. Find the volume.

Say it aloud: at position \(x\), the slice across the base runs from \(y=0\) up to \(y=x^2\), so its length is \(s=x^2\). A square with side \(s\) has area \(s^2\). So \(A(x)=(x^2)^2=x^4\).

\[ V=\int_0^2 x^4\,dx=\left[\frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^2=\frac{32}{5}. \]

The volume is \(\dfrac{32}{5}\) cubic units. Notice that the integrand is an area, in square units, and the integration adds a length, \(dx\), so the units come out cubic. Checking units this way catches many setup errors.

Worked example 2: semicircles

The base is the region between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) for \(0\le x\le 1\). Cross-sections perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis are semicircles whose diameter lies in the base. Find the volume.

The line is above the parabola on \([0,1]\), so the slice across the base has length \(s=x-x^2\), and this is the diameter of the semicircle. The radius is therefore \(s/2\), and the area of a semicircle is half of \(\pi r^2\):

\[ A(x)=\frac12\pi\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^2=\frac{\pi}{8}\,s^2=\frac{\pi}{8}(x-x^2)^2. \]

\[ V=\frac{\pi}{8}\int_0^1 (x^2-2x^3+x^4)\,dx=\frac{\pi}{8}\left(\frac13-\frac12+\frac15\right)=\frac{\pi}{8}\cdot\frac1{30}=\frac{\pi}{240}. \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
In the semicircle example, students often write the area as \(\tfrac12\pi s^2\), treating the slice length \(s\) as the radius. Ask what the slice is. Here the slice across the base is the diameter, so the radius is \(s/2\). Always decide whether the base length is a side, a diameter, or a radius before you write \(A\).

A circular base. The vertical chord at position \(x\) has length \(s=2\sqrt{4-x^2}\); this chord is one side of an equilateral triangle that stands up out of the plane.

Worked example 3: equilateral triangles on a circle

The base is the disk \(x^2+y^2\le 4\). Cross-sections perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis are equilateral triangles with one side in the base. Find the volume.

At position \(x\), the chord across the circle runs from \(y=-\sqrt{4-x^2}\) to \(y=\sqrt{4-x^2}\), so \(s=2\sqrt{4-x^2}\). An equilateral triangle with side \(s\) has area \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2\), so

\[ A(x)=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\cdot 4(4-x^2)=\sqrt3\,(4-x^2). \]

\[ V=\sqrt3\int_{-2}^{2}(4-x^2)\,dx=\sqrt3\left[4x-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-2}^{2}=\sqrt3\cdot\frac{32}{3}=\frac{32\sqrt3}{3}. \]

A sanity check worth making. Keep the same circular base, but make every cross-section a disk whose diameter is the chord, so \(A(x)=\pi\,(4-x^2)\). Then \(V=\pi\int_{-2}^{2}(4-x^2)\,dx=\dfrac{32\pi}{3}\), which is exactly the volume \(\tfrac43\pi r^3\) of a sphere of radius 2. The method recovers a result Archimedes was proud of. Lesson 16.04 builds on this observation.

📚 PART 2 · SLICING IN OTHER DIRECTIONS, AND CHECKING YOURSELF

Slices do not have to be perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis. When the base region is described more naturally as \(x\) in terms of \(y\), slice perpendicular to the \(y\)-axis and integrate \(A(y)\,dy\). The routine is identical; only the variable changes. Lesson 16.01 made the same move when it integrated with respect to \(y\) to find an area.

Worked example 4. The base is the region bounded by \(y=x^2\), the \(y\)-axis, and the line \(y=4\) (in the first quadrant). Cross-sections perpendicular to the \(y\)-axis are squares with one side in the base. Find the volume.

Slicing at height \(y\) cuts the base from \(x=0\) out to the parabola, where \(x=\sqrt y\). So \(s=\sqrt y\), \(A(y)=s^2=y\), and \(y\) runs from 0 to 4:

\[ V=\int_0^4 y\,dy=\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^4=8. \]

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
Compare Example 1 and Example 4. They use two different base regions, and by coincidence the second volume is a whole number. The coincidence is not the point. The point is that the slice direction told us which variable to use, and the limits then came from the range of that variable, 0 to 4 for \(y\) here. A typical error is to integrate \(A(y)\) from the \(x\)-limits.
⚡ TRY IT · CHOOSE THE CROSS-SECTION AREA
In each row the base has a slice of length \(s\) across it. Pick the cross-section area \(A\) in terms of \(s\).
1. Squares with side \(s\)
2. Semicircles whose diameter is \(s\)
3. Isosceles right triangles whose legs are both \(s\)
4. Equilateral triangles with side \(s\)
▶  Try it in your notebook first: the base is the region between \(y=x^2\) and \(y=2x\). Cross-sections perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis are squares. Find the volume. Then change the squares to isosceles right triangles with a leg in the base, and predict the new volume before computing.
The curves meet where \(x^2=2x\), at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\), and the line is above the parabola in between. So \(s=2x-x^2\). Squares: \(V=\int_0^2(2x-x^2)^2\,dx=\int_0^2(4x^2-4x^3+x^4)\,dx=\dfrac{32}{3}-16+\dfrac{32}{5}=\dfrac{16}{15}\). Triangles with legs \(s\): each area is \(s^2/2\), exactly half the square, so \(V=\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{16}{15}=\dfrac{8}{15}\). The prediction needs no calculation at all once you see that \(A\) is halved everywhere.

Convince a classmate: Two solids have the same base. Every cross-section of the first is a square with side \(s\), and every cross-section of the second is a semicircle with diameter \(s\). Which solid has more volume? Explain using only the ratio of the two cross-section areas, with no integration.

📚 PART 3 · WHERE THE THINKING LIVES

Tools such as graphing software and computer algebra systems can integrate \(A(x)\) in a moment, and they can even draw the solid. They cannot decide whether the slice length is a side, a diameter, or a radius, because that decision lives in the wording of the problem. A tool that is handed the wrong \(A(x)\) will integrate it flawlessly and return a confident, wrong volume.

🤖 AI AWARENESS
If an AI assistant or a calculator gives you a volume, treat it as a claim to be checked. Ask for the setup, not only the answer: what is \(s\), what is \(A\), what are the limits? Then test the setup yourself with a special case. Replace the base by a shape whose volume you already know and see whether the method reproduces it, as we did with the sphere above. A setup that survives a test is a setup you can trust.

The discipline of this lesson, to sketch first, name \(s\), write \(A\), then integrate, is the same discipline you used when you set up optimization and related-rates problems in Module 12. In both cases the calculus is the easy part. The honest work is turning a picture or a sentence into a function.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A volume is a stack of thin slabs. If you can write the area \(A\) of a typical cross-section as a function of position, then \(V=\int_a^b A\,dx\) (or \(\int A\,dy\)), and everything difficult lies in reading the picture: which length is the side, the diameter, or the radius, and where the slices begin and end. Lesson 16.04 notices that when you rotate a region around an axis, every cross-section is automatically a circle or a ring, so \(A\) is \(\pi\) times a radius squared, and the whole method applies at once.
