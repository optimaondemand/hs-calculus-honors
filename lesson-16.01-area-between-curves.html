📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can find the area under one curve. What if a region is trapped between two?
The integral was born as an area, and for a century that was all it was. Lesson 13.03 measured the area between a single curve and the axis. Today the second curve arrives. The idea is nothing more than subtraction: the area between two curves is the area under the top one minus the area under the bottom one. What takes care is the setup. You will need to decide which curve is on top, where the curves cross, and whether the region needs one integral or several. All of that comes from a sketch, which is why the sketch is the first line of every solution.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to set up and evaluate \(\int_a^b[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx\) for the region between two curves, finding the limits from the points where the curves intersect.
2
I will be able to sketch a region first and use the sketch to decide which curve is on top, and when a region needs to be split into pieces because the curves cross.
3
I will be able to choose between integrating in \(x\) (top minus bottom) and integrating in \(y\) (right minus left), and explain why one integral can be easier than two.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil. A sketch is part of every problem today, so keep scratch paper handy. Recall the definite integral (Lesson 13.03) and the Fundamental Theorem (Lesson 14.02).
▶

📚 PART 1 · TOP MINUS BOTTOM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Lesson 13.03 defined \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) as the signed area between a curve and the \(x\)-axis. Now two curves enclose a region, like the one between \(y=\sqrt x\) and \(y=x^2\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\). Before reading on, how would you find its area?

Here is the region from the question, with the curves labelled. On \([0,1]\) the curve \(y=\sqrt x\) lies on top of \(y=x^2\) (check at \(x=\tfrac14\): \(\sqrt{1/4}=\tfrac12\) and \((\tfrac14)^2=\tfrac1{16}\)).

Slice the region into thin vertical strips, as you did in Lesson 13.01. The strip at \(x\) has height \(\sqrt x-x^2\), the top curve minus the bottom curve, and width \(dx\). Adding up all the strips is a definite integral, and the Fundamental Theorem (Lesson 14.02) finishes the job.

Area Between Two Curves
If \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(f(x)\ge g(x)\) for every \(x\) in \([a,b]\), the area of the region between the curves from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) is \[ A=\int_a^b \bigl[f(x)-g(x)\bigr]\,dx. \] The rule in words: top minus bottom.

Why does this agree with “area under the top, minus area under the bottom”? Because the integral of a difference is the difference of the integrals (the linearity property from Lesson 13.03): \(\int_a^b (f-g)\,dx=\int_a^b f\,dx-\int_a^b g\,dx\). Here is that computation for the region in the figure.

Example 1. Find the area between \(y=\sqrt x\) and \(y=x^2\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\). Top minus bottom is \(\sqrt x-x^2\), so

\[ A=\int_0^1\left(x^{1/2}-x^2\right)dx=\left[\tfrac23x^{3/2}-\tfrac13x^3\right]_0^1=\tfrac23-\tfrac13=\tfrac13. \]

Check it as a difference of two areas: the area under \(\sqrt x\) on \([0,1]\) is \(\tfrac23\), the area under \(x^2\) is \(\tfrac13\), and \(\tfrac23-\tfrac13=\tfrac13\). The two routes agree.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
The formula needs the curves in the right order, but it does not need either curve to be above the \(x\)-axis. Shift both curves up or down by the same amount and every strip keeps the same height, so the area does not change. That is why top minus bottom works even when the region dips below the axis. The only thing that matters is which curve is higher.
📚 PART 2 · FINDING THE LIMITS, AND SKETCHING FIRST

In Example 1 the problem handed you the limits \(x=0\) and \(x=1\). More often a problem says only “the region enclosed by these curves,” and the limits are for you to find. They are the \(x\)-values where the curves cross. The method has three steps, and the first one is a drawing.

  1. Sketch both curves, even roughly, and shade the region. The sketch tells you which curve is on top.
  2. Find the intersections by setting the two expressions equal and solving. These are your limits.
  3. Integrate top minus bottom between the limits, and evaluate.

Example 2. Find the area of the region enclosed by \(y=x^2\) and \(y=x+2\).

Intersections. Solve \(x^2=x+2\), so \(x^2-x-2=0\), so \((x-2)(x+1)=0\), giving \(x=-1\) and \(x=2\). Which is on top? At \(x=0\), the line gives \(2\) and the parabola gives \(0\), so the line is on top throughout \((-1,2)\). Integrate.

\[ A=\int_{-1}^{2}\left(x+2-x^2\right)dx=\left[\tfrac{x^2}{2}+2x-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{2}=\left(2+4-\tfrac83\right)-\left(\tfrac12-2+\tfrac13\right)=\tfrac{10}{3}+\tfrac76=\tfrac92. \]

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Around 250 BCE, Archimedes found the area of a parabolic segment with no calculus at all. He proved it equals \(\tfrac43\) of the area of the largest triangle that fits inside it. Test his claim on Example 2. The chord runs from \((-1,1)\) to \((2,4)\) and has slope \(1\). The triangle’s third vertex is the point of the parabola where the tangent has that same slope, \(2x=1\), at \((\tfrac12,\tfrac14)\). That is a Mean Value Theorem point (Lesson 8.03). The triangle has area \(\tfrac{27}{8}\), and \(\tfrac43\cdot\tfrac{27}{8}=\tfrac92\). Two thousand years apart, the same answer.

When the top curve switches

Sometimes the curves cross inside the interval you want, and the roles of top and bottom swap. Then one integral of “top minus bottom” is wrong, because the integrand changes sign and positive and negative pieces cancel. You must split at each crossing and use the correct order on each piece.

Example 3. Find the total area enclosed between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^3\). Setting \(x^3=x\) gives \(x(x-1)(x+1)=0\), so the curves cross at \(x=-1,\,0,\,1\). On \((0,1)\) the line is on top, because \(x>x^3\) there (try \(x=\tfrac12\)). On \((-1,0)\) the cubic is on top (try \(x=-\tfrac12\): \(x^3=-\tfrac18>-\tfrac12=x\)). The two lobes are mirror images, so

\[ A=\int_{-1}^{0}\left(x^3-x\right)dx+\int_{0}^{1}\left(x-x^3\right)dx=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12. \]

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Integrating \(\int_{-1}^{1}(x-x^3)\,dx\) in one go gives \(0\), because \(x-x^3\) is an odd function and the two lobes cancel. A region with area \(\tfrac12\) does not have area \(0\). Whenever two curves cross inside your interval, find every intersection first and split the integral there. The sketch is what warns you.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: find the area of the region between \(y=\sqrt x\) and \(y=\tfrac x2\) (a line through the origin). Sketch it, find the limits, decide who is on top, and evaluate. Then reveal the check.
Setting \(\sqrt x=\tfrac x2\) gives \(x=0\) or \(\sqrt x=2\), so \(x=4\). At \(x=1\), \(\sqrt1=1>\tfrac12\), so the curve is on top. \[A=\int_0^4\left(\sqrt x-\tfrac x2\right)dx=\left[\tfrac23x^{3/2}-\tfrac{x^2}{4}\right]_0^4=\tfrac{16}{3}-4=\tfrac43.\] If you got a negative number, you subtracted in the wrong order; an area is never negative, so that is your signal to swap.
📚 PART 3 · WHEN \(y\) IS THE BETTER VARIABLE

Everything so far sliced the region into vertical strips and integrated in \(x\). Some regions are easier to slice into horizontal strips. If the region is described by curves written as \(x=\) something in \(y\), or if the top or bottom boundary changes formula partway across, horizontal strips may need one integral where vertical strips need two. The rule is the same with the roles turned a quarter turn: right minus left, integrated with respect to \(y\).

Area Between Curves, Integrating in \(y\)
If \(x=u(y)\) and \(x=v(y)\) are continuous on \([c,d]\) and \(u(y)\ge v(y)\) on that interval, the area between them from \(y=c\) to \(y=d\) is \[ A=\int_c^d \bigl[u(y)-v(y)\bigr]\,dy. \] In words: right minus left.

Example 4. Find the area of the region above the \(x\)-axis, bounded above by \(y=\sqrt x\), on the right by the line \(y=x-2\), and below by the \(x\)-axis. The curves meet where \(\sqrt x=x-2\); squaring gives \(x=x^2-4x+4\), so \(x^2-5x+4=0\), so \(x=1\) or \(x=4\). Check each in the original equation: \(x=1\) gives \(1=-1\), which is false (an extraneous root from squaring), and \(x=4\) gives \(2=2\), true. So the curves meet only at \((4,2)\).

With vertical strips, the bottom boundary is the \(x\)-axis for \(0\le x\le2\) and the line for \(2\le x\le4\), so you need two integrals:

\[ A=\int_0^2\sqrt x\,dx+\int_2^4\left(\sqrt x-(x-2)\right)dx=\tfrac{10}{3}. \]

With horizontal strips, solve each curve for \(x\): \(y=\sqrt x\) becomes \(x=y^2\) (the left boundary), and \(y=x-2\) becomes \(x=y+2\) (the right boundary). The region runs from \(y=0\) up to \(y=2\), and a single integral does it:

\[ A=\int_0^2\left[(y+2)-y^2\right]dy=\left[\tfrac{y^2}{2}+2y-\tfrac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^2=2+4-\tfrac83=\tfrac{10}{3}. \]

Same area, one integral instead of two. Choosing the direction is a matter of looking at the sketch and asking where the boundary curves change.

🤖 AI-AWARENESS
A graphing tool will shade the region and evaluate the integral in a moment. It will not tell you which curve is on top, whether the curves cross inside your interval, or whether you needed two integrals. Those are the decisions that make the answer correct. Use a tool to check an answer you have already set up by hand, never to decide the setup.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The area between two curves is the integral of top minus bottom (or right minus left), taken between the points where the curves cross. A sketch tells you who is on top; solving for the intersections gives the limits; and when the curves swap order inside the interval, you split the integral there, or you slice in the other direction and let one integral do the work of two.
