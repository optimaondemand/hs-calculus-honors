📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If you cannot write down the area under a curve, can you still get close by adding up rectangles, and know which way you missed?
Derivatives broke a changing quantity into instants. This module goes the other way and gathers instants back into a whole: a total distance from a velocity, a total amount from a rate. The first tool is wonderfully plain. Slice the interval into thin pieces, pretend the function is constant on each piece, and add up the rectangles. Today you will build three versions (left, right and midpoint), learn to tell which one overestimates and which one underestimates, and use the same machinery on a table of data. Every later idea in the module, including the exact value of an area, is built on the rectangles you draw today.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to compute left, right and midpoint rectangle sums with \(n\) equal pieces from a formula, a graph or a table of values.
2
I will be able to decide whether a left or right sum overestimates or underestimates when the function is increasing or decreasing, and explain why in a sentence.
3
I will be able to interpret the area under a velocity graph as distance traveled, and state a bracket for it from tabular data without claiming more than the data supports.

📚 PART 1 · THE AREA UNDER A VELOCITY GRAPH
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A runner wears a watch that logs her speed only once every 10 seconds. You want to estimate how far she ran in a minute. For each 10-second stretch, which speed reading would you pair with it?

You already know how to find distance when speed is constant: distance is speed times time, \(d=vt\). Draw that on a graph with time across the bottom and velocity up the side, and \(vt\) is the area of a rectangle of height \(v\) and width \(t\). The distance is an area.

That picture does not break when the speed changes. It simply gets harder to compute. Chop the time into short pieces. On each short piece the speed is almost constant, so the distance covered is almost a rectangle's area. Add the rectangles and you have an estimate of the whole distance. Make the pieces thinner and the estimate should improve. Lesson 8.02 went from position to velocity by differentiating; this module runs the road in the other direction, from velocity back to total change, and it begins with rectangles.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Archimedes, working in Syracuse in the third century BCE, found the area of a region bounded by a parabola by filling it with shapes whose areas he could compute and showing that the leftover gap could be made as small as you like. He had no algebraic notation for it and no limits, only extraordinary patience. The rectangles in this module are the descendants of his method, and in Lesson 13.02 we will recover one of his results with algebra.
📚 PART 2 · LEFT, RIGHT AND MIDPOINT SUMS

Every rectangle approximation needs the same three decisions: the interval \([a,b]\), the number of pieces \(n\), and which point of each piece sets the height.

Rectangle sums with \(n\) equal pieces
Cut \([a,b]\) into \(n\) pieces of equal width \(\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}\), with endpoints \(x_i=a+i\,\Delta x\) for \(i=0,1,\dots,n\). Then\[ L_n=\Delta x\,[f(x_0)+f(x_1)+\cdots+f(x_{n-1})],\qquad R_n=\Delta x\,[f(x_1)+f(x_2)+\cdots+f(x_n)], \]\[ M_n=\Delta x\,[f(m_1)+f(m_2)+\cdots+f(m_n)],\quad m_i=\tfrac{x_{i-1}+x_i}{2}. \]These are the left, right and midpoint sums. Each uses exactly \(n\) heights.

Worked example: \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([0,2]\), \(n=4\)

Here \(\Delta x=\dfrac{2-0}{4}=0.5\), and the endpoints are \(0,\ 0.5,\ 1,\ 1.5,\ 2\). Lay the work out in a vertical table, the way you would on paper:

PieceLeft height \(f(x_{i-1})\)Right height \(f(x_i)\)Midpoint height
\([0,0.5]\)\(0\)\(0.25\)\(f(0.25)=0.0625\)
\([0.5,1]\)\(0.25\)\(1\)\(f(0.75)=0.5625\)
\([1,1.5]\)\(1\)\(2.25\)\(f(1.25)=1.5625\)
\([1.5,2]\)\(2.25\)\(4\)\(f(1.75)=3.0625\)
Sum of heights\(3.5\)\(7.5\)\(5.25\)

\[ L_4=0.5(3.5)=1.75,\qquad R_4=0.5(7.5)=3.75,\qquad M_4=0.5(5.25)=2.625. \]

Left sum, \(n=4\): every rectangle sits under the curve.
Right sum, \(n=4\): every rectangle pokes over the curve.

Narrate it as you would to a classmate: “I cut \([0,2]\) into four pieces of width \(0.5\). The left sum reads each height at the left end of its piece, so it uses \(f(0),f(0.5),f(1),f(1.5)\). The right sum shifts every reading one step to the right. Each sum multiplies the total of the heights by the width.” The true area is somewhere between \(1.75\) and \(3.75\), and the midpoint sum, \(2.625\), is already close to the value we will prove in Lesson 13.02, \(\tfrac83\approx2.667\).

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two slips account for most lost points here. First, the width is \(\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}\), the length of the interval divided by the number of pieces, not \(b-a\) and not \(\dfrac{b-a}{n+1}\). Second, a left sum and a right sum each use exactly \(n\) heights. The left sum drops the last endpoint and the right sum drops the first, so the two lists are not the same list. Count your heights before you multiply.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: for \(f(x)=9-x^2\) on \([0,3]\) with \(n=3\), compute \(L_3\), \(R_3\) and \(M_3\). Then reveal the check.
\(\Delta x=1\). Left heights: \(f(0)=9,\ f(1)=8,\ f(2)=5\), so \(L_3=1(9+8+5)=22\). Right heights: \(f(1)=8,\ f(2)=5,\ f(3)=0\), so \(R_3=13\). Midpoints \(0.5,\ 1.5,\ 2.5\): \(f=8.75,\ 6.75,\ 2.75\), so \(M_3=18.25\). The exact area is \(18\), which you will be able to prove soon: it lies between \(R_3=13\) and \(L_3=22\), and the midpoint sum is the closest of the three.
📚 PART 3 · TOO BIG OR TOO SMALL?

Notice that for \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([0,2]\) the left sum was too small and the right sum was too big. That was not luck. On \([0,2]\) this function is increasing (Lesson 10.02 tells you why: \(f'(x)=2x>0\)), so on every piece the smallest height is at the left end and the largest is at the right end. A left rectangle fits under the curve and a right rectangle sticks up above it.

Over and under for a monotone function
If \(f\) is increasing on \([a,b]\), then \(L_n\le\) (true area) \(\le R_n\). If \(f\) is decreasing, the inequalities reverse: \(R_n\le\) (true area) \(\le L_n\). Either way the true area is trapped between the left sum and the right sum.

Example (a decreasing function). Let \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\) on \([1,3]\) with \(n=4\), so \(\Delta x=0.5\). Left heights \(f(1),f(1.5),f(2),f(2.5)=1,\ 0.6667,\ 0.5,\ 0.4\) give \(L_4=0.5(2.5667)\approx1.2833\). Right heights \(f(1.5),f(2),f(2.5),f(3)=0.6667,\ 0.5,\ 0.4,\ 0.3333\) give \(R_4=0.5(1.9)=0.95\). Because \(f\) is decreasing, the area lies between \(0.95\) and \(1.2833\). (Soon you will be able to show that the exact area is \(\ln3\approx1.0986\), inside the bracket, as the theorem promised.)

Why more pieces help. For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([0,2]\), doubling to \(n=8\) gives \(L_8=2.1875\), \(R_8=3.1875\) and \(M_8=2.65625\). The left and right errors, measured from the exact area \(\tfrac83\approx2.667\), fall from about \(0.92\) and \(1.08\) to about \(0.48\) and \(0.52\): roughly half as large. Thinner rectangles waste less. The bracket closes in, which is exactly the behavior Lesson 13.02 will turn into a limit.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
The “trapped between” guarantee needs \(f\) to be monotone on the whole interval. If a function rises and then falls, a left sum can overcount on one piece and undercount on the next, and the true area need not lie between \(L_n\) and \(R_n\). The midpoint sum carries no over/under guarantee even for monotone functions. State a bracket only when you have checked the hypothesis.
📚 PART 4 · WHEN THE DATA IS A TABLE

Real data arrives as a table, not a formula, and the same sums work on it. The widths are the gaps between readings, the heights are the readings, and the units multiply: meters per second times seconds gives meters.

Example. A cart's velocity is recorded every 2 seconds, and the readings never decrease:

\(t\) (s)02468
\(v\) (m/s)06101213

With \(\Delta t=2\) and four pieces: the left sum is \(2(0+6+10+12)=56\) m, and the right sum is \(2(6+10+12+13)=82\) m. If the cart never slowed down, the distance traveled is at least \(56\) m and at most \(82\) m. Notice the honesty in that sentence. We did not say the cart traveled \(69\) m, the average of the two, and we did not claim anything about what happened between readings beyond the assumption that the velocity never decreased. A table can support a bracket; it cannot support a decimal place the data never contained.

Convince a classmate: in two sentences, why does the area under a velocity graph measure distance? Then say what would change if the cart were reversing, so that the velocity were negative on some stretch.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A rectangle sum estimates an area by adding \(\Delta x\) times a list of heights, with \(\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}\) and exactly \(n\) heights taken at the left ends, the right ends or the midpoints of the pieces. For a monotone function the left and right sums trap the true area between them, and thinner rectangles close the gap. When the area under a velocity graph is the distance, the same sums estimate distance from a table. Lesson 13.02 gives the sums a language, sigma notation, and then asks what happens to \(R_n\) as \(n\) grows without bound.
