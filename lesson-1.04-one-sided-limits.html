📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Approach from the left, approach from the right. Do they agree?
Every table you have built so far has quietly assumed something: that approaching from below and approaching from above tell the same story. For most functions they do. But some functions genuinely behave differently on the two sides of a point, jumping from one height to another, and the two-sided limit you have been estimating since Lesson 1.01 simply does not exist for them. Today you learn to track each side separately, and to state precisely when the two sides are allowed to be combined into a single limit.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read and evaluate one-sided limits \( \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) \) and \( \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) \) from a graph or a piecewise formula.
2
I will be able to state and apply the theorem: the two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.
3
I will be able to determine whether a two-sided limit exists at a jump in a piecewise or step graph, and explain why.

📚 PART 1 · TWO ROUTES TO THE SAME POINT

The notation \( \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) \) means the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) using only values less than \(a\), approaching from the left. The notation \( \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) \) means the same approach using only values greater than \(a\), from the right. Each one-sided limit asks about only half the neighborhood of \(a\).

The two-sided limit you have used since Lesson 1.01, \( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \), is really a claim that both routes arrive at the same destination. That claim is a theorem:

💡 THE THEOREM
\( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \) if and only if \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = L \) and \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L \). If the two one-sided limits disagree, or either one fails to exist, the two-sided limit does not exist.
Approaching \(a\) from the left, the curve aims for height 3 (open circle). Approaching from the right, it aims for height 5, where a solid dot marks \(f(a)=5\). The two sides disagree, so \( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \) does not exist, even though \(f(a)\) is defined.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Functions that jump between values are not merely a textbook curiosity. The English engineer Oliver Heaviside, working on telegraph theory in the late 1800s, used a step function, one value before a moment in time and a different value after, to model a switch flipping on. Wherever a system turns on, shuts off, or changes state abruptly, a jump discontinuity is doing real work describing it.
📚 PART 2 · READING ONE-SIDED LIMITS FROM A PIECEWISE FORMULA

Worked example: one where the sides disagree

Let \( f(x) = \begin{cases} x+1 & x < 2 \\ x^2 & x \ge 2 \end{cases} \). Find \( \lim_{x\to 2^-} f(x) \), \( \lim_{x\to 2^+} f(x) \), and \( \lim_{x\to 2} f(x) \).

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\to 2^-} f(x) &= \lim_{x\to 2^-}(x+1) \quad \textit{for } x<2\textit{, use the first piece}\\ &= 2+1 = 3\\[6pt] \lim_{x\to 2^+} f(x) &= \lim_{x\to 2^+}(x^2) \quad \textit{for } x\ge2\textit{, use the second piece}\\ &= (2)^2 = 4 \end{aligned} \]

The left limit is 3 and the right limit is 4. Since they disagree, \( \lim_{x\to 2} f(x) \) does not exist, even though \(f(2)=4\) is perfectly well defined.

Worked example: one where the sides agree

Let \( g(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 & x < 1 \\ 2x-1 & x \ge 1 \end{cases} \). Find \( \lim_{x\to 1} g(x) \).

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\to 1^-} g(x) &= (1)^2 = 1\\ \lim_{x\to 1^+} g(x) &= 2(1)-1 = 1 \end{aligned} \]

Both one-sided limits equal 1, so \( \lim_{x\to 1} g(x) = 1 \). This time the two pieces of the graph meet exactly, with no jump at all.

Explain to a classmate: using the jump example above, why can a limit fail to exist at a point even though the function is defined there? Which value, if either, does the limit actually report?

📚 PART 3 · NOTATION YOU MUST KNOW COLD
⚡ FLASHCARDS · ONE-SIDED NOTATION
Tap a card to reveal what the symbol means. Tap again to flip it back.
\( \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) \)
The limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) using only values less than \(a\): the approach from the left.
\( \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) \)
The limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) using only values greater than \(a\): the approach from the right.
Left limit equals right limit
Exactly the condition needed for the two-sided limit \( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \) to exist.
Jump discontinuity
A point where both one-sided limits exist but disagree, so the two-sided limit does not exist.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A two-sided limit is a claim about agreement: the values approaching from the left and the values approaching from the right must be heading toward the very same number. When they disagree, the limit does not exist, no matter what is plotted at the point itself. Holding the left and right approaches separately, then asking whether they match, is a habit you will lean on for the rest of this course, especially in Module 3, where it becomes part of the very definition of continuity.
