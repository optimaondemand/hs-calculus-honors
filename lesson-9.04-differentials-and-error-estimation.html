📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Every measurement is a little wrong. What separates a scientist from a guesser is knowing how wrong the answer computed from it could be.
Lesson 9.03 made the tangent line an estimator of values. Today you turn it into an estimator of change. You will define the differential \(dy=f'(x)\,dx\), use it to find how an error in a measurement spreads into a computed quantity, and, just as important, learn what a differential estimate does not promise. This is where calculus becomes a habit of honesty about numbers.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define the differential \(dy=f'(x)\,dx\) and compare it with the actual change \(\Delta y\).
2
I will be able to use differentials to estimate the absolute and relative (percent) error in a quantity computed from a measurement, such as the area of a circle or the volume of a sphere.
3
I will be able to state honestly what a differential estimate does and does not guarantee, including its direction of error and what happens when \(dx\) is not small.

📚 PART 1 · THE DIFFERENTIAL: PREDICTING A CHANGE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A square's side is measured as 10 cm, but the ruler could be off by 1%. About how far off, in percent, could the area of the square be?

Lesson 9.03 turned a tangent line into an estimator: \(f(x)\approx L(x)\). Rewrite that statement as a statement about change. If \(x\) moves by a small amount \(\Delta x\), the function really changes by

\[ \Delta y=f(x+\Delta x)-f(x), \]

and the tangent line predicts that it changes by \(f'(x)\,\Delta x\). The prediction gets a name.

Differentials
Let \(dx\) be a small change in the input (the same number as \(\Delta x\)). The differential of \(y=f(x)\) is \[ dy=f'(x)\,dx. \] It is the change in \(y\) along the tangent line, and \(dy\approx\Delta y\) when \(dx\) is small.

Example 1. \(y=x^2\), \(x=3\), \(dx=0.1\). Then \(dy=2(3)(0.1)=0.6\). The actual change is \(\Delta y=3.1^2-3^2=9.61-9=0.61\). The differential is off by \(0.01\), and note that \(0.01=(dx)^2\). The gap is the error of Lesson 9.03.

On y = x squared at x = 3 with dx = 1, the tangent line predicts a change dy = 6, while the curve actually rises by 7. The small gap is the error.

Example 2: watching the gap close. For \(y=x^3\) at \(x=2\), \(dy=3(2)^2\,dx=12\,dx\). Compare it with the real change as \(dx\) shrinks:

\(dx\)\(dy=12\,dx\)\(\Delta y=(2+dx)^3-8\)\(\Delta y-dy\)

Each tenfold decrease in \(dx\) makes the gap about a hundred times smaller, exactly the behavior Lesson 9.03 proved: the error in a tangent-line estimate shrinks faster than the distance.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
You have written \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) since Module 4 as one symbol for the derivative. Differentials let it read like a fraction: from \(dy=f'(x)\,dx\), dividing by \(dx\) gives \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=f'(x)\). That is a definition we chose, not a proof that the derivative is secretly a quotient. Leibniz, who invented the notation, liked how it made the Chain Rule look like cancelling fractions; it is a helpful memory aid, not a theorem.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: for \(y=\sqrt x\) at \(x=25\) with \(dx=0.2\), find \(dy\), then compare with the actual change \(\sqrt{25.2}-5\). Then reveal the check.
\(dy=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\,dx=\dfrac1{10}(0.2)=0.02\). The actual change is \(\sqrt{25.2}-5=0.019960\ldots\). The differential \(0.02\) is within \(0.00004\) of the truth. Notice \(dy\) is slightly high, which matches Lesson 9.03: the square root bends downward, so the tangent line lies above the curve.
📚 PART 2 · PROPAGATED ERROR: HOW WRONG COULD I BE?

Here is why differentials matter outside a classroom. Every measurement has uncertainty. If you measure \(x\) with error \(dx\) and then compute a quantity \(y=f(x)\), the uncertainty in \(x\) travels into \(y\). The differential estimates how much.

what it ishow to estimate it
absolute error in \(y\)\(\Delta y\)\(|dy|=|f'(x)|\,dx\)
relative error in \(y\)\(\dfrac{\Delta y}{y}\)\(\left|\dfrac{dy}{y}\right|\) (multiply by 100 for percent)

Example 3: a cube. A cube's edge is measured as \(x=10\) cm, with possible error \(dx=0.1\) cm. Its volume is \(V=x^3=1000\text{ cm}^3\). Then \(dV=3x^2\,dx=3(100)(0.1)=30\). So \(V\approx1000\pm30\text{ cm}^3\), a relative error of \(\dfrac{30}{1000}=3\%\), while the edge was only \(\dfrac{0.1}{10}=1\%\) off. (The true change is \(\Delta V=10.1^3-1000=30.301\).)

Example 4: a circle. A circular plate has radius \(r=5\) cm, with possible error \(dr=0.1\) cm. Its area is \(A=\pi r^2=25\pi\approx78.54\text{ cm}^2\). Then \(dA=2\pi r\,dr=2\pi(5)(0.1)=\pi\approx3.14\). So \(A\approx78.5\pm3.1\text{ cm}^2\). The relative error is \(\dfrac{\pi}{25\pi}=4\%\), while the radius was \(\dfrac{0.1}{5}=2\%\) off.

A pattern shows up. For \(y=x^n\), we have \(dy=nx^{n-1}\,dx\), and dividing by \(y=x^n\):

\[ \frac{dy}{y}=n\,\frac{dx}{x}. \]

The relative error in \(x^n\) is about \(n\) times the relative error in \(x\). That settles the opening check-in: the area of a square (\(n=2\)) doubles a relative error, the volume of a cube (\(n=3\)) triples it. A sphere's volume is \(\tfrac43\pi r^3\); with \(r=6\) and \(dr=0.1\), it has relative error \(3\cdot\frac{0.1}{6}=5\%\), and \(dV=\tfrac{72\pi}{5}\approx45.2\) on \(V=288\pi\approx904.8\).

⚡ TRY IT · PROPAGATED ERROR
Use a differential, or the rule \(dy/y=n\,dx/x\), for each situation.
1. A cube's edge has a \(2\%\) relative error. About what is the relative error in its volume?
2. A circle's radius is \(10\) m with possible error \(0.2\) m. Estimate the error in its area, using \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\).
3. A \(1\%\) error in the radius: which quantity is more sensitive, the area of a circle or the volume of a sphere?
▶  Try it in your notebook first: a ball's radius is measured as \(6\) cm with possible error \(0.1\) cm. Estimate the error in its volume \(V=\tfrac43\pi r^3\) using \(dV\), and the relative error. Then reveal the check.
\(V=\tfrac43\pi(216)=288\pi\approx904.8\text{ cm}^3\). \(dV=4\pi r^2\,dr=4\pi(36)(0.1)=14.4\pi\), which is \(\tfrac{72\pi}{5}\approx45.2\text{ cm}^3\). So \(V\approx904.8\pm45.2\text{ cm}^3\), and \(\dfrac{dV}{V}=\dfrac{14.4\pi}{288\pi}=5\%\), which is \(3\) times the radius's \(\tfrac{0.1}{6}\approx1.67\%\). (The true change is \(\Delta V\approx46.0\), a little larger, because \(V\) bends upward.)
📚 PART 3 · THE FINE PRINT: WHAT A DIFFERENTIAL DOES NOT PROMISE

A differential is a promise with fine print. Before you report a number such as \(1000\pm30\text{ cm}^3\), you owe your reader the fine print.

1. A differential is an estimate, not a bound. In the cube example, \(dV=30\) but the true change was \(30.301\). The estimate was a little low. When the curve bends upward (as \(x^3\) does for \(x>0\)), the tangent line lies below the curve, so \(dy\) underestimates \(\Delta y\); when it bends downward, \(dy\) overestimates. This is the direction rule from Lesson 9.03. The true error can therefore be slightly larger than \(dy\).

2. The estimate fails when \(dx\) is not small. Take \(y=x^2\) at \(x=3\):

\(dx\)\(dy=6\,dx\)\(\Delta y\)how far off is \(dy\)?
0.10.60.61about 1.6%
167about 14%
2121625%

Nothing in the formula \(dy=f'(x)\,dx\) warns you. It computes a number for any \(dx\). The responsibility for asking “is \(dx\) small compared to the scale on which \(f\) curves?” is yours.

3. The inputs are only as honest as the measurement. \(dx=0.1\) is a claim about the instrument. If the ruler's true error is \(0.5\) cm, then \(1000\pm30\) is false precision, however neat the derivative.

Convince a classmate: your lab partner says “the differential gives \(dV=30\), so the volume is exactly \(1030\).” Explain, using this lesson, two reasons that is overclaiming.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING
Reporting \(1000\pm30\text{ cm}^3\) as a differential estimate is honest. Reporting \(1030\) as if it were measured is not. Scientists and engineers write the error next to the answer for the same reason you show your work: a number without its uncertainty cannot be checked, and an answer that cannot be checked is only a claim.
🤖 AI AWARENESS
Ask an AI tool for the “error in the volume of a cube with edge \(10\pm0.1\)” and it may give you \(30\), or \(30.301\), or \(\pm0.3\%\), depending on how it reads the question. Now you know the differences: \(30\) is a differential estimate, \(30.301\) is the exact change, and a percent needs the relative error. You are the one who decides which question is being asked.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A differential is the tangent line's prediction of a change. It lets you carry the uncertainty in a measurement through a formula, and it comes with honest fine print: it is an estimate, it errs in a direction you can predict, and it fails when the change is not small.
