📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Send x as far out as you like, and ask what height the curve is heading toward.
Every limit you studied in Module 1 asked what a function does near a fixed point. This lesson asks a different kind of question: what does a function do as x itself runs out toward infinity? The word for the line a graph settles toward out there is asymptote, from the Greek asymptotos, meaning not falling together. Apollonius of Perga used the idea more than two thousand years ago to describe how the branches of a hyperbola draw closer and closer to certain lines without ever meeting them. Today you will make that ancient picture precise with a limit.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what \( \lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = L \) and \( \lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = L \) mean, in words and in notation.
2
I will be able to evaluate a limit at infinity for a rational function by comparing the degrees of its numerator and denominator and dividing by the highest power of x.
3
I will be able to identify the horizontal asymptote of a rational function, or explain why it has none.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your math notebook and a pencil, for the worked examples and practice.
▶
A graphing calculator or free graphing tool, for checking the end behavior of each function.
📚 PART 1 · THE END BEHAVIOR QUESTION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Picture the graph of \( f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x} \) as x runs far out to the right, toward 1000, then 1,000,000. What do you expect the height of the graph does? (No wrong answer. Just make a prediction.)

A limit as x runs out, not in

A limit at infinity asks the same kind of question as before, aimed in a new direction. Instead of watching x close in on a fixed number, we watch x grow without bound, and ask what height the function is heading toward. We write

\[ \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L \qquad \text{and} \qquad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L \]

read as "the limit of f(x), as x approaches infinity (or negative infinity), equals L." The symbol \(\infty\) is not a number x ever reaches; it is shorthand for growing without bound. If either limit equals a finite number L, the line \(y = L\) is called a horizontal asymptote of the graph: a height the curve presses toward, arbitrarily closely, as x runs out in that direction.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The symbol \(\infty\) itself is fairly young. The English mathematician John Wallis introduced it in 1655 in a treatise on conic sections, borrowing a shape that may echo the Roman numeral for one thousand. It took another two centuries before mathematicians agreed on precisely what a statement like "x approaches infinity" is allowed to mean, which is exactly the limit language you are learning now.
📚 PART 2 · READING END BEHAVIOR FROM DEGREE

For a rational function, the technique is always the same: divide every term, top and bottom, by the highest power of x appearing in the denominator. Every term of the form \(\dfrac{c}{x^n}\) then shrinks toward zero, and only the terms with matching top and bottom powers survive the limit. The outcome depends entirely on how the degrees compare.

📌 THREE CASES, ONE TECHNIQUE
▶  Degree of numerator less than degree of denominator: the limit is 0.
▶  Degree of numerator equal to degree of denominator: the limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients.
▶  Degree of numerator greater than degree of denominator: the limit grows without bound; there is no horizontal asymptote on that side.

Worked example: the equal-degree case

Evaluate \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{3x^2-1}{2x^2+5} \). Both numerator and denominator have degree 2, so we divide every term by \(x^2\).

\[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2-1}{2x^2+5} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\dfrac{3x^2}{x^2}-\dfrac{1}{x^2}}{\dfrac{2x^2}{x^2}+\dfrac{5}{x^2}} \]

\[ = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3-\dfrac{1}{x^2}}{2+\dfrac{5}{x^2}} \]

\[ = \frac{3-0}{2+0} = \frac{3}{2} \]

Because x appears squared throughout, sending x to \(-\infty\) produces the identical calculation, so the horizontal asymptote \(y = \dfrac{3}{2}\) holds in both directions.

Worked example: when there is no horizontal asymptote

Evaluate \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{x^2+1}{x+1} \). The numerator has degree 2, the denominator degree 1, so we divide by \(x\), the highest power in the denominator.

\[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2+1}{x+1} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+\dfrac{1}{x}}{1+\dfrac{1}{x}} \]

As \(x\to\infty\), the numerator still grows without bound while the denominator settles near 1, so the quotient grows without bound as well: the limit is \(\infty\). There is no finite horizontal asymptote here. (Some such graphs press instead toward a slanted line, a case this course does not require.)

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
A horizontal asymptote describes what happens far out, not a wall the graph may never touch nearby. Many graphs cross their own horizontal asymptote once or several times close to the origin before settling toward it out at the edges. Do not rule out a horizontal asymptote just because the graph crosses \(y = L\) somewhere in the middle.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Not every function settles on the same height in both directions. A function like \(g(x) = \dfrac{2x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}\) presses toward \(y = 2\) as x runs toward positive infinity, but toward \(y = -2\) as x runs toward negative infinity. A single graph can have two different horizontal asymptotes, one on each side.
▶  Try it in your notebook first: evaluate \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{5x+1}{x^2-3} \) by dividing by the highest power of x. Then reveal the check.
Divide every term by \(x^2\): \( \dfrac{\dfrac{5}{x}+\dfrac{1}{x^2}}{1-\dfrac{3}{x^2}} \). As \(x\to\infty\) every fraction with x in the denominator shrinks to 0, leaving \(\dfrac{0+0}{1-0} = 0\). This matches the rule: numerator degree 1 is less than denominator degree 2, so the limit is 0 and \(y=0\) is the horizontal asymptote.
