📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives. Today you find out what it actually is, and why.
The Sum Rule worked because addition splits apart cleanly inside a difference quotient. Multiplication and division do not split apart nearly so obligingly, which is why they each need their own proof rather than a guess extended from Lesson 5.01. Today you prove the Product Rule and the Quotient Rule directly from the limit definition, using one clever algebraic trick, adding and subtracting the same expression, that makes both proofs fall into place. You will also lean on a fact from Lesson 4.04: a differentiable function is continuous, and both of today's proofs quietly depend on that.

📚 PART 1 · THE PRODUCT RULE
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Let \(f(x) = x\) and \(g(x) = x\), so \(f(x)g(x) = x^2\). By the Power Rule, \((fg)' = 2x\). But \(f'(x)g'(x) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1\). Since \(2x \ne 1\) for most \(x\), \((fg)' \ne f'g'\). Whatever the correct rule is, it is not simply multiplying the two derivatives together.

Let \(s(x) = f(x)g(x)\), with \(f\) and \(g\) both differentiable at \(x\). Start from the limit definition, and then insert the same quantity, \(-f(x+h)g(x) + f(x+h)g(x)\), which changes nothing because it adds zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} s'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x+h)g(x) + f(x+h)g(x) - f(x)g(x)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ f(x+h)\cdot\frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h} \;+\; g(x)\cdot\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \right] \\ &= f(x)\,g'(x) \;+\; g(x)\,f'(x) \end{aligned} \]

The last step uses two facts at once: the difference quotients converge to \(g'(x)\) and \(f'(x)\) by definition, and \(f(x+h) \to f(x)\) as \(h \to 0\) because \(f\) is differentiable, hence continuous, exactly the implication proved in Lesson 4.04. Without that continuity, the first factor in the first term would not settle down to \(f(x)\) at all. This is the Product Rule:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)g(x)\big] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x) \]

💡 NOTICE
This is the second time in the course that Lesson 4.04's theorem, differentiable implies continuous, has done real work rather than sitting as a fact to memorize. A theorem earns its keep exactly like this: by getting quietly used inside a later proof.
📚 PART 2 · THE QUOTIENT RULE

Let \(q(x) = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\), with \(g(x) \ne 0\). Combine the two fractions in the difference quotient over a common denominator:

\[ q'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\dfrac{f(x+h)}{g(x+h)} - \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x) - f(x)g(x+h)}{h\,g(x+h)g(x)} \]

The same add-and-subtract trick from Part 1 splits the numerator, this time inserting \(-f(x)g(x) + f(x)g(x)\):

\[ f(x+h)g(x) - f(x)g(x+h) = g(x)\big[f(x+h)-f(x)\big] \;-\; f(x)\big[g(x+h)-g(x)\big] \]

Substituting back and dividing every term by \(h\) gives

\[ q'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{g(x)\cdot\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \;-\; f(x)\cdot\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}{g(x+h)g(x)} = \frac{g(x)f'(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{\big[g(x)\big]^2} \]

using \(g(x+h) \to g(x)\) by the same continuity fact as before. This is the Quotient Rule:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{\big[g(x)\big]^2} \]

🧩 WORTH KNOWING · ORDER MATTERS
Unlike the Product Rule, the Quotient Rule is not symmetric in \(f\) and \(g\): the numerator is \(f'g - fg'\), not \(g'f - gf'\), and swapping the order flips the sign. Say the rule aloud the same way every time until the order is automatic: "derivative of the top times the bottom, minus the top times the derivative of the bottom, all over the bottom squared."
📚 PART 3 · WORKED EXAMPLES AND A DIRECT CHECK

Product Rule, verified by expanding first

Differentiate \(f(x) = (x^2+1)(3x-2)\). With \(u = x^2+1\), \(u' = 2x\), and \(v = 3x-2\), \(v' = 3\):

\[ f'(x) = u'v + uv' = 2x(3x-2) + (x^2+1)(3) = 6x^2 - 4x + 3x^2 + 3 = 9x^2 - 4x + 3 \]

Check by expanding first, the way Lesson 5.01 would have handled it: \((x^2+1)(3x-2) = 3x^3 - 2x^2 + 3x - 2\), and the Power Rule gives \(9x^2 - 4x + 3\) directly. Same answer, two representations, one confirming the other.

Quotient Rule

Differentiate \(g(x) = \dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}\). With \(u = 2x+1\), \(u'=2\), and \(v = x-3\), \(v'=1\):

\[ g'(x) = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} = \frac{2(x-3) - (2x+1)(1)}{(x-3)^2} = \frac{2x-6-2x-1}{(x-3)^2} = \frac{-7}{(x-3)^2} \]

Notice this quotient could not have been expanded into a polynomial first the way the product example was; the Quotient Rule was not optional here, it was the only tool available.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Addition splits apart inside a difference quotient; multiplication and division do not, so each earned its own proof, built on the same add-and-subtract trick and quietly leaning on Lesson 4.04's theorem that differentiable implies continuous. With five proved rules now in hand, constant, sum, difference, power, product, and quotient, you can differentiate any polynomial or rational function without ever returning to the limit definition.
