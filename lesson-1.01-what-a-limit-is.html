📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can get as close as you like, without ever arriving.
For two thousand years, mathematicians could find the slope of a straight line but not the slope of a curve at a single point, because a single point gives you nothing to measure against. The whole of calculus begins with one patient idea that dissolves that problem: instead of asking what a function equals at a point, we ask what value it draws near as we approach the point. That value is called a limit, and today you will learn to read it from a table and from a graph.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what \( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \) means in words and in notation.
2
I will be able to estimate a limit from a table of values computed on both sides of the point.
3
I will be able to estimate a limit from a graph and explain why the limit can exist where the function is undefined.

📚 PART 1 · THE QUESTION CALCULUS ASKS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A function has a hole in its graph at \(x = 2\), so \(f(2)\) is undefined. What do you expect happens to the height of the graph as \(x\) slides toward 2 from either side? (No wrong answer. Just make a prediction.)

Approaching, not arriving

Consider the function \( f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} \). At \(x = 2\) the denominator is zero, so \(f(2)\) does not exist. The graph has a hole there. Yet we can still ask a sharper question: as \(x\) creeps toward 2, without ever equaling 2, what value does \(f(x)\) creep toward? This is the question of the limit. We write it as

\[ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = L \]

read aloud as "the limit of \(f(x)\), as \(x\) approaches 2, equals \(L\)." The arrow means approaches. Notice what the notation quietly insists on: it never asks for \(f(2)\). The limit describes the neighborhood of the point, not the point itself. That single distinction is what lets calculus measure a curve where old algebra could not.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Newton and Leibniz built working calculus in the 1600s on an idea of the "infinitely small" that no one could quite define. Critics called it reasoning with ghosts. It took nearly two centuries, and the careful work of Cauchy and Weierstrass in the 1800s, to replace the ghost with the precise idea of a limit you are learning now. Useful mathematics sometimes runs ahead of its own foundations, and is set right later.
📚 PART 2 · ESTIMATING A LIMIT FROM A TABLE

Worked example: build the table on both sides

To estimate \( \displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} \), we choose \(x\)-values that march toward 2 from below and from above, and we compute \(f(x)\) at each. We never use \(x = 2\) itself.

Approaching 2 from the left
\(x\)\(f(x)\)
1.93.9
1.993.99
1.9993.999
1.99993.9999
Approaching 2 from the right
\(x\)\(f(x)\)
2.14.1
2.014.01
2.0014.001
2.00014.0001

From the left the outputs climb through 3.9, 3.99, 3.999 toward 4. From the right they fall through 4.1, 4.01, 4.001 toward 4. Both sides press in on the same number. We conclude

\[ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = 4 \]

even though \(f(2)\) is undefined. There is a good reason the answer is so clean. For every \(x \ne 2\) we may factor and cancel: \( \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x - 2} = x + 2 \). Near \(x = 2\) the function behaves exactly like \(x + 2\), whose value at 2 would be 4. You will make this algebra your main tool in Lesson 1.03.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
A limit of 4 does not mean \(f(2) = 4\). Here \(f(2)\) does not exist at all. The limit reports where the outputs are heading, not where they land. Keep those two questions separate and much of this course becomes clear.
📚 PART 3 · ESTIMATING A LIMIT FROM A GRAPH

A graph tells the same story visually. To read \( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \) from a graph, trace the curve toward \(x = a\) from the left and from the right and see what height the curve is aiming for. An open circle marks a point the graph does not actually include. The limit can still exist there, because the limit cares only about the approach.

The line \(y = x + 2\) with an open circle at \((2, 4)\): the curve aims for height 4, though the point itself is missing.
⚡ TRY IT · READ THE EVIDENCE
For each situation, choose the best conclusion about the limit as \(x\) approaches the point in question.
1. A table shows \(f(x)\) reaching 6.9, 6.99, 6.999 from the left and 7.1, 7.01, 7.001 from the right. Estimate \( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \).
2. A graph is a straight line except for an open circle at \((3, 5)\). What is \( \lim_{x\to 3} f(x) \)?
3. A table shows outputs nearing 1 from the left but nearing 4 from the right. What is \( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \)?
▶  Try it in your notebook first: build a short two-sided table to estimate \( \lim_{x\to 1} \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} \). Then reveal the check.
For \(x \ne 1\), \( \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \dfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x + 1 \). Values from the left (0.9, 0.99, 0.999) give 1.9, 1.99, 1.999; values from the right (1.1, 1.01, 1.001) give 2.1, 2.01, 2.001. Both sides press toward 2, so the limit is 2, even though \(f(1)\) is undefined.

💪 THE ONE IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A limit answers a single, patient question: as the input approaches a point, what one value do the outputs approach? It does not ask what happens at the point, which is why a limit can exist where the function is undefined. Hold that distinction firmly. In the next lesson you will learn the laws that let you find many limits at once, by substitution, without building a table every time.
